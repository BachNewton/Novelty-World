# The producer's standards for prose

What the producer wants from the writing in every story, each with its reason, since a reason covers cases its rule never names. A standard goes in only when the producer has said it or agreed to it as general. One that stops being true is rewritten, not added to. What belongs to a single story (its narrator, its reader, its world) is in that story's own files.

## The reader is never lost

**Clarity comes first.** At every point the reader knows who is there, where and when they are, and what just happened. A reader who is lost can't tell whether the book meant it, whether they missed something, or whether the writing is bad, and all three end the same way: they stop trusting the book. The only confusion allowed is a mystery the reader knows is a mystery.

**Clarity isn't completeness.** The reader needs what it takes to follow a scene and feel it, and nothing more. Where a town is on the map, how old a child is, or what the date is matters only when it matters to someone in the scene; the rest can stay unknown without anyone being lost. A scene that answers every question before it's asked stops being a story and becomes a briefing.

**Write for a reader who isn't you.** The writer has the notes, the outline and the research; the reader has only the page. A place is named with what it means to the people in the scene: how far away it is, what kind of place it is. An idiom, a regional word, a brand or a reference is either clear from the sentence around it or replaced. Obscurity, not foreignness, is the test: a local name for an everyday thing loses readers from the same country too. A joke that leans on a shared shorthand (the present everyone's father gets) is an idiom too, and as easily missed.

**Each sentence can be followed in one reading.** One main thought to a sentence, with its length varied for rhythm. A chain of clauses joined by "and" becomes two sentences or one clear one. A clipped fragment that the reader has to decode says the whole thing instead.

**Numbers appear only where they mean something, and the reader never does arithmetic.** A date, an age, a time or a distance is in the text because someone in the scene feels it, never as a stamp; a page dense with them reads like a record. Where one is needed, give it in the form it's felt: "she was twenty-four when she first fell ill", not two figures to subtract. Every number is computed with the date tool and true when checked.

## Feeling is on the page

**The reader always knows what people feel, and why it matters to them.** A reader can't hear a performance or read the writer's mind, so a feeling the words don't carry isn't in the book. Mostly it shows in what people do, notice and think. When that isn't enough, the feeling is named plainly: the one thing this person would feel at this moment. A feeling dressed up as a paradox or a list of opposites sounds deep and rings false.

**Show first; tell when the world needs it.** Before stating a fact about the world, ask who in the scene cares about it. If someone does, it reaches the reader through them: what they see, do, say or remember. If it's how the world works and the chapter's idea runs through it, the narrator can stop and explain, in a digression with a voice and a point that comes back to a person; science fiction needs these sometimes, and they're welcome when they're rare. If neither, it's an object in the room, or it's cut. A scene that stops to brief the reader fact after fact reads like a textbook.

**The idea is felt, never stated.** A chapter's idea, as its outline puts it, is for the writer. On the page it's the shape of what happens; stated outright, it turns a story into a lecture.

## The text is whole

**A text reads as if it had always been so.** A correction never shows on the page: not as its negation ("an elephant that was not purple"), not as a rule obeyed literally (a time jump that recites the exact months and days because a rule said to give them), and not as a seam where an old passage met a new one. That's why a revision is a fresh draft.

**Repetition is a choice or a mistake.** A word, phrase or image that returns is a motif only when it means more each time; motifs are few, and each returns only when it earns it. Anything else repeated is cut, whether a phrase, a detail or a gesture.

**A character is how they think, not a tic.** People speak in their own ways, but a habit given to a character as a label (a pet word in every speech) is noticed as a device.

**The title is honest.** It names what the chapter is, at its true weight. A title that sounds weightier than its subject (a thriller's title on a family dinner) reads as camp.

## The book is true

**Facts are checked, and earn their place.** Every real-world and canon fact is checked online: law, institutions, places, history, medicine. A fact that does nothing for the story is a liability, not texture, because it can only be wrong.

**A place and its people are written from the inside.** A detail of a culture is true to how people there live and talk, never a visitor's idea of them: no custom, saying or bit of local colour invented to make a scene feel foreign. A cold reader can't catch a false one, because they don't know the place, so check it, and ask the producer when the story is set somewhere they know.

**Illness, disability and identity are written true.** What's hard stays hard and is shown whole, including what it costs the people around it. An illness never grants insight or wisdom to serve a scene, a person isn't confused when the plot needs pathos and lucid when it needs a quotable line, and nobody is defined by their diagnosis. Research comes from clinical sources and from first-person accounts.

**Humour is risky, so it is earned.** Done right, it carries a reader through dark times; done wrong, it ruins a book. It comes from people (how they really joke when things are hard) and from systems followed faithfully to an absurd end, told straight. It is never a quip performed at the reader, never at a person's expense, and never sitcom timing. Every joke is checked by the cold read, and one that doesn't land is cut.

## The writing comes first

**A chapter is written for a reader, never for a machine.** What the voices can or can't perform plays no part in the manuscript. Good writing performs well: the models learned from audiobooks of good novels, and the performance step adapts to the text, never the other way round.
