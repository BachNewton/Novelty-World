# The record: what this repo has tried

Evidence, not rules. Two games have had learned, tuned and searched bots here, and their docs hold the measurements: Monopoly's `bots/docs/` (`METHOD.md`, `EVOLUTION.md`, `RL-DESIGN.md`, `LEAPS.md`) and Risk's `ideas/` (`bot-log.md`, `roadmap.md`, `gpu-engine.md`, `self-play-league.md`, `distilled-v7.md`, `native-v2.md` and the rule-bot docs). Read them for what happened; the method is this skill's, and where a project doc's conclusion is contradicted below, this skill wins and the doc should be amended when next touched. Dates here are the data: they say how long each line took.

## Monopoly (five weeks of strength work, then the language-model seats)

**The hand-written loop.** Sixty-odd versions in five weeks, each one change, each measured by a fishtest-style SPRT (a pair of one-sided tests, 20 Elo margin) and a Bradley–Terry ladder, crowned only on both seed streams and against a panel. The biggest jumps were structural: pricing a rival's monopoly instead of vetoing it (+160 Elo), trading to deny (+216 over the floor), deploying capital faster, halving a denial factor (+71). By the fortieth version the summit was a cluster within about ten Elo that no test could separate, with a rock-paper-scissors cycle at the top; twins can't be told apart; from-scratch rule bots went 0 for 4. The one lever that paid every time was human data: a review of six games against a strong person produced the largest single jump in the project, and probe fleets of agents playing as people found the pricing leaks bot-only play is blind to. The crown gate is all-bot, so human-facing dimensions are vacuous under it: the loop had a strength ratchet and no human-facing one.

**Parameter search (SNES, up to 55 dimensions).** Crowned three times when its objective was the crown's own (a maximin over the panel) and failed when it was an aggregate (it spent its pressure on opponents it already beat). It found a coherent profile the hand-tuned loop had declared converged, and then its ceiling: the fitness is blind to behaviours that cost nothing in self-play (it set a lever that produced an infinite trade loop in a live game), so more and more knobs had to be pinned; a maximin against a panel containing a twin of the base has no gradient; the aggregate produces counters, repaired by a line-search between parent and winner. The record's own conclusion holds: the parameter lever's tier is a robust ceiling and the next gain is structural.

**Rollout search.** A truncated-rollout improvement over the rule bot beat its base and lost to another panel member; at short horizons the leaf flipped buys into declines. The record's reading, that the ceiling is leaf quality rather than depth, is right, and the cost (600 games in 48 minutes on the pool) is why the search couldn't be rated or deepened on the reference engine.

**The reinforcement learner.** All seven phases of an AlphaZero-style loop were built in TypeScript on tfjs-node: a seat-relative encoder, an atomic action vocabulary with a sound mask, a policy and value net, a tree search with chance nodes, a self-play recorder, a trainer. Three measured runs, the longest 400 iterations and 2,400 games, read 0% against the field's weakest bot at every evaluation of six to sixteen games. The record concluded that the structural work was done and "what remains is compute and a sharper ruler". The evidence supports the second half and not the first: a reference engine at tens of games a second, a search of 24 to 40 simulations over a stochastic four-player game that discards its tree at every dice roll, and a policy that starts below its teacher, make a method whose sample needs exceed this machine by orders of magnitude, and nothing ever showed that search-with-net beat net-alone, which is the loop's whole premise. Compute would have scaled a loop that hadn't been shown to improve. The stack (tfjs-node, pinned to Node 22, native-binding workarounds on Windows, no GPU) is a dead end. The supervised probe the record itself proposed as the kill gate for the whole programme, a value fitted to outcomes on 10⁵ recorded states compared with the hand value on held-out games, was never run.

**Language-model seats.** A product line: a seat played by a model through the route, frozen versions of prompt and schema, measured by a scenario suite and by games against people. Its lessons are about prompts and evaluation, not strength: most errors came from the view's facts and the answer's shape, more thinking budget never measured better, and strength is judged against people, never bots. The eGPU's only use in Monopoly was serving local models for these seats.

**Where it stands.** The seated default is the version with the fullest human model; the crown is a version from an external submission; no learned bot exists. Any learned work here starts with a fast engine with parity tests, because the reference engine's speed rules out everything that learns from play, and with a ruler that includes the human-facing surface.

## Risk (two and a half weeks)

**Rule bots and the yardstick.** GD's four Expert personas, ported exactly, as the floor players know; rule-v1 to v7.1 in two weeks, each with a design doc and a measured verdict. The gains shrank version by version (+71 to +90, then +18, +40, +40, +15), and three stall fixes each rated 12 to 15 Elo below the bot they replaced, the cost falling at six players, seated anyway because the stall slowed every measuring run. The stall is an equilibrium of identical evaluations (every seat pays all of weakening a rival and shares the benefit), not a bug. The big step was search: v7 plays its opponents' sampled replies, +115 to +130 over the planner, flat from 8 replies to 32 because its candidates are the planner's best four placements and more replies only sharpen a choice between near-copies. Every version that played one strong person lost every logged game to him, on judgement, with dice that were fair where it was checked.

**Parameter search.** Tuned a planner's preferences against a mixed field with a worst-table objective; the lessons were about noise (cells of 240 games too few to trust; a worst table resting on 70 games has a standard error near 60 Elo) and about the field (a tune against one strong opponent gave back to the weak ones what every strong bot beats them by).

**A learned evaluation (rule-v8).** A net fitted to 524,000 recorded positions beat the hand evaluation on held-out log loss by a clear margin, and the planner playing on it was worse one-out: it had learned that among copies of the recording's bots, a seat that holds back while the others fight wins. The population lesson, measured: an evaluation fit to one population's games carries that population in.

**Distillation (student-v1).** v7's placement search distilled into a small net ranking the planner's candidates, trained with a listwise loss on the teacher's scores, held out by game. At 10⁵ labelled decisions the best student kept about a fifth of the teacher's edge and was still gaining about five points per doubling; in games it beat the planner at three to five players on holdout seeds (+20 Elo) and was even at six. Its limits are named in the record and are right: label noise (the teacher agrees with itself 0.65 at 8 replies, 0.88 at 32) and data, both of which a native teacher supplies cheaply. The 16,000-game recording that would have read the next doubling was started and never measured.

**The native engine (Rust).** Exact against the reference (same random stream, same odds, games interchange), about a hundred times the games per second, bots designed in Rust from the ideas. It made a 201-setting, 272,000-game map of the planner's stall affordable in a couple of days of machine time, and native-v2 (the reply search on the native planner) beat rule-v7.1 at every table on train seeds, the first bot to beat the one to beat, at a tenth of its turn time. It stalls in mirrors (7% at five players, 14% at six, against a 1% bar) because it doesn't close games out; that is the open bug. How a Rust bot plays real games is undecided.

**The GPU engine and self-play (net-v1).** A batched PyTorch engine held bit-exact to the reference on every fixture (lockstep on 8,700 and 45,000 recorded moves; greedy and GD ported exactly, a one-bit CUDA division found and ruled on), then PPO in a league with the whole step captured as one CUDA graph: 45,000 to 127,000 decisions a second on the RTX 4070. A transformer over the board's tokens (128 wide, 4 layers) went from random weights to beating GD's personas at three times par in a night, passed the planner's class in two (better than rule-v6 one-in and one-out on train seeds; the holdout read was dropped), and is below the search bot v7.1. Its plateau was diagnosed with pre-registered predictions, the best record in the repo: the gradient's noise scale was measured at 1.8 million decisions against steps of a few thousand, colder updates slowed the drift without stopping it, a shaping reward on army share taught stalling within 25 iterations, and a long cold run wandered. The record's next step is a bias diagnosis of the update, and its reasoning is sound: a cold run read at matched samples that doesn't climb says the expected gradient isn't pointing up the ruler. The cheaper levers that bear on exactly that come first, none of them tried: the in-training ruler was a ceiling (GD's personas, 500 to 700 Elo below the targets) and a moving field (ladders among the run's own nets), so progress toward the target was read only in rare harness runs of hours each; the league never contained the target's class (the rule bots weren't ported), which is a bias between the training objective and the ruler's; the policy started from noise rather than from an imitation of the strongest bot, a fallback the design named as its arm 2 and never ran (the harness already keeps any bot's games as game records the GPU engine replays move for move; what was never built is the replay into observations and the trainer, since the rule bots can't be batched onto the GPU); the value head's explained variance was never logged; and no two seeds of one recipe were ever compared, so the spread that every "held" and "failed" was read against is unknown. The imitation ceiling test (can this observation and net copy v7.1 at all?) costs an evening and answers the representation question before any more nights of PPO.

**Measurement.** The harness is the best-designed part: a game as a pure function of seed and seating, both mixtures at every table size, the Luce par so one margin means one edge at any table, a ladder played to a precision with sandwich errors, a cache keyed by the code's hash, and GitHub Actions' free runners sharding gauntlets and ladders into the same report a local run prints. Its lesson for every learner: nothing measured on the fast side decided a seating; the harness did.

**Where it stands.** The seated best is the search bot (1669 on the ladder, GD's personas near 1000); the native search beats it and stalls; the student and the net are research bots below it. The assets are exactly those expert iteration needs: a search whose ceiling is its candidates, a native engine that makes labels cheap, a distillation pipeline that carries a fifth of the edge and climbs with data, a GPU trainer, and a value head trained by every self-play run. The record's own roadmap item, the search with a learned evaluation, is that loop's first iteration.

## What the record settles

- **The ruler before the learner**, and a ruler that spans the range with the target in it. Every plateau here was first a reading problem.
- **Strength is population-relative.** An evaluation, a reward or a league fitted to one population carries it in; both mixtures, a fixed panel, and the real judge.
- **A fast exact engine is the prerequisite** for anything that learns from play, and parity against the reference is its kill criterion. The native and GPU ports earned their keep in days.
- **Search is the improvement operator**, and its ceiling is its candidates and its leaf evaluation, which is what learned parts are for.
- **Stand on the strongest thing that exists.** From scratch costs nights to reach what imitation gives in an evening, and a learner that starts from noise plays noise.
- **Reward the goal alone.** Placement credit, shaping on a proxy, and a cap that counts as a result were each learned at once.
- **Pre-registered predictions with confidences** made the Risk self-play record readable and falsifiable; "interesting" results without them taught nothing twice.
- **Human-facing behaviour is invisible to bot-only play.** The canary is people, or probes that play like them.
- **Noise is the usual explanation.** Cells of 240 games, screens under 800 games, ladders among a moving field and 16-game evaluations each misled at least once.

## Conclusions in the project docs that this skill supersedes

- Monopoly `RL-DESIGN.md`: "All-TypeScript" as a locked decision, and "what remains is compute and a sharper ruler". The stack is retired and the method didn't fit the budget; the next learned work there starts with an engine and a supervised probe.
- Monopoly `METHOD.md`: the one-session-per-version loop with a clipboard handoff is a workflow for the hand-written line, not a method for learning; its measurement rules stand.
- Risk `gpu-engine.md`: "from scratch first, as the owner asked", with the imitation warm start named as a fallback. The record doesn't show the owner was told that a warm start from the strongest bot is standard practice, so the fork is open and is re-presented with that information (`SKILL.md`, forks) in Risk's `ideas/learned-bot.md`, the line's plan; the recorder exists, and it costs a night of recording and an evening of training.
- Risk `self-play-league.md`: the bias diagnosis it names next is right in kind, and the cheaper tests of the same question come before it (`diagnosis.md`: the ruler, the league, the representation, the seed spread, then the update's bias).
- Risk `roadmap.md`: a student "is seated only when it comes close to the champion" is a seating rule, not a reason to stop reading the data curve; the next doubling is the measurement that decides the distillation line.
