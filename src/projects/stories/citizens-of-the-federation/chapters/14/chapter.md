# Chapter 14: Toronto

On Sunday mornings at eleven, Carol Flory had a piano lesson from a teacher more than four thousand miles away, who could hear every mistake.

The phone stood on the music rest of Joy's keyboard, in the spare room that had been Carol's for four years and still had Joy's winter coats in its closet. The volume was turned nearly to nothing, because Joy had worked all night and was asleep through the wall. In Helsinki it was six in the evening, and Anna was holding her own phone over the piano she had promised her mother: a real one, upright and secondhand, which had come up four flights of stairs in March while Ruth stood on the landing and told the movers which corners they were about to hit.

"Scale," said Ruth. "Both hands."

Carol played a scale, both hands.

"Your left hand is visiting," said Ruth. "It doesn't live there yet."

"It's fifty-five years old and new in town."

"So am I," said Ruth, who was seventy-seven and had lived in Finland since the autumn. "Again."

Carol played it again. Through the phone came the sounds of a Helsinki kitchen at suppertime: Theo, who was eleven, arguing with somebody in Finnish, and Alma asking in English whether Grandma was being mean to Carol again.

"Grandma's teaching," said Anna, off to one side. "It sounds the same."

At the end Anna came on by herself, as she always did. Alma's teacher had sent a note home. Alma kept walking into door frames, and had stopped putting her hand up to read from the board. The eye doctor had sent them on to the children's hospital for tests on Thursday.

"Glasses, probably," said Anna.

"Tell her to pick red frames," said Carol. "Then everybody always knows where she is."

---

Carol had come to Toronto for two weeks, and stayed four years.

She had a card in her wallet that said she could live in Canada for good, and beside it, because she had never thrown a record away in her life, the Ohio license that said M. She sang tenor on Wednesdays in the church up Bloor Street. On Tuesdays a young organist from Kingston, Jamaica, sat beside her at a real piano and winced on her behalf. And since January she had worked at the Noonien-Singh Institute, because Joy was a nurse there and had told them they needed somebody who read forms to the bottom.

The Institute was a tower of green glass on University Avenue, a block below the hospitals, where everybody on the sidewalk wore a lanyard and walked fast. It had a fountain, and a lounge on the ground floor with a piano nobody played, and families came to it from forty countries, because it treated children nobody else could. It had been called that for longer than anybody who worked there could say. On its website there was a page about fourteen orphans, taken in as babies by one of its scientists and raised in its care, and a photograph of them on the steps, nearly grown. The scientist wasn't named. He had gone some years before, and nobody said where. The staff who remembered him called him the old man. Nobody had ever asked where the orphans came from. People don't, of an orphanage.

Joy had explained the science to Carol once, over supper, with a fork. A child came in with one wrong word in her genes, a word that would kill her. Machines on the eleventh floor, the kind Toronto had been teaching to think for twenty years, wrote her a new passage to go round it. Joy called the passage a network, because it was never only one word. It had to reach into everything the bad word touched. And the child who had come in going blind went home seeing.

That came down, on Carol's floor, to paper. Every child had a consent form. On the line for the patient there was nothing, because the patients were four, and six, and eleven. Under it was a line for whoever spoke for them. Carol kept those.

She had added one thing of her own. When a family came to her desk, she asked first what their child liked to be called, and wrote it small in the margin. The director, Dr. Bastien, had asked her twice not to write in the margins of medical records, and then had stopped asking.

---

The next Sunday there was no lesson. Anna's voice was wrong before she had said anything.

On Thursday the hospital had looked into the back of Alma's eyes, and taken a little of her blood, and a machine had read every gene in it in two days. On Saturday a doctor had sat them down in a room with a box of tissues on the table and said a word.

"Batten disease," said Anna. "There are a lot of kinds. Hers is the one they call six. Her eyes go first. Then she starts to fall, and then the fits. Then she forgets things she knew. How to talk. How to eat." She stopped. "They said maybe ten. Maybe twelve."

Carol held on to the edge of the keyboard. Alma was six.

"Mom's with her. She's teaching her 'Twinkle, Twinkle' with her eyes shut, so she'll know where the notes are by feel." Anna's voice went, and came back. "Ben wants to talk to you."

There was a fumbling, and then Ben, whom Carol had spoken to perhaps four times in her life, and never on purpose.

"They tested us both," he said. "Anna and me. You only get it if both your parents carry it, and we both do. Mine came down from somebody in Toledo." He was quiet a moment. "The doctor asked me what my sister died of. Katie. She was nine. And I didn't know, Carol. I'm forty-five years old and I never asked anybody." He breathed out. "I'm going to find out. I don't know why I'm telling you."

"Because I'm far enough away," said Carol.

"Yes," said Ben. "I think that's it." And he gave the phone back.

"Joy told me once what your Institute does," said Anna. "Children like this." In all their years of Sundays, Carol had never heard her ask for anything. "Carol. Is there anything?"

"I'll find out," said Carol.

---

Joy came in at seven on Monday morning, still in her scrubs, and found Carol at the kitchen table with two cups of coffee.

"CLN6," said Joy, when she had heard it. "We've had two. The network for it isn't done. Bastien says two years, maybe three, before the machines get it right." She put both hands round the cup. "She'd be eight. Nine. It's fast, that one. They'd have to be quick."

"Is there anywhere else?"

"There's nowhere else." Joy looked at her coffee. "I've watched a boy from Lagos who couldn't see his own hand read the eye chart down to the smallest letters. I've watched it eleven times now. Eleven children. I feel it every time." Her voice didn't change, but her hands had gone tight on the cup. "I came up here to feel things again. I do. Every single time."

"Then she'll come here," said Carol.

"Then she'll come here," said Joy, and went to bed.

So Carol knew, all that summer. She knew on Sundays, when Ruth said "Again" down the phone and Alma, in the background, counted the stairs out loud on her way to bed. She knew at her desk, every time a family came in from somewhere far away with a child asleep on somebody's shoulder.

---

In July she started practising at lunch, on the piano in the lounge that nobody played.

One day she had company. A young man had come in with four of the clinic's children trailing after him, and he sat them down at the far table with a chess set, one board between all four of them, and told them that if they could beat him together they could have his dessert for a week. Then he came and sat on the end of Carol's bench.

He was about twenty, in a university sweatshirt with a paperback of Milton stuffed in the front pocket, and he had not shaved. He watched her play the left hand of the hymn she was learning, and when she stopped, he tried it.

He was terrible. He was much worse than she was. He knew it, and he laughed out loud, with his head back, a big laugh for the size of the room.

"The one thing they didn't think to put in," he said. "Joy says you're Carol. Joy says you read the bottom of a form first." He held out his hand. "Khan."

At the far table one of the children moved a bishop and shouted, and Khan turned round and looked at the board.

"That's very good, Amira," he said. "That's genuinely annoying." He went over and lost on purpose, badly enough that the children could see he was doing it and loved him for it, and came back for his tray, and ate their desserts anyway.

Carol had seen the photograph on the steps. She asked Joy that evening which one he was.

"The one who asked why," said Joy, and didn't say any more.

---

In September a small country on the far side of the world became the first to let its clinics change a child before she was born: not to cure her of anything, but to make her more. Within days three countries had announced registers of anybody who had been changed, and the news had found a word for such people. It called them Augments.

Ottawa ordered every laboratory in Canada that worked on human genes to open its records. Dr. Bastien called Carol in with the letter.

"The Tuesday after Thanksgiving," she said. "They'll want the consent files, every one, and somebody to sign that the set is complete. That's you." She said the next part to the letter. "The old man's papers are in the sub-basement. They're his own. They aren't part of the set."

The letter quoted the law. Canada had made it a crime in 2004, before anybody could do it, to change a person's genes in a way her children would inherit. Up to ten years in prison.

At seven that evening, when the floor had emptied, Carol took the stairs to the sub-basement. She had never in her life been able to leave a form half read.

---

There were fourteen boxes on a steel shelf, each with a name on the end in marker. Each was a different first name in front of the same surname, *Noonien-Singh*, and Carol had walked in under that surname every morning since January. It was carved over the front door. Fourteen children had needed something to be called, and someone had given them the building's.

She opened one at random.

On top was a form from 2012, from a fertility clinic out by the airport. A couple who had made more embryos than they needed had given the spares away for research, and had ticked a box: *I understand that donated embryos will not be used to establish a pregnancy.* Under it was a birth certificate from the year after. Under that were consent forms, one for every year of a child's life until the old man went. On the line for the parent or guardian, every year, there was the same signature, a hook and a line that no clerk alive could have read. On the line under it, for the investigator, every year, there it was again, in the same blue ink. Where it said *Print name*, every year, there was nothing.

For twenty-three years at the county, Carol had sent back any form one person had signed on both lines. It was the first thing a clerk learned. You couldn't be the one asking and the one who said yes.

She sat down on the bottom step with the box on her knees. That couple, wherever they were, had given away a few cells in a dish. They didn't know they had a son.

"Box nine's mine," said a voice from the top of the stairs, "if you're going in order."

Khan came down and sat two steps above her, with his elbows on his knees.

"Joy said you'd find your way down here. She said it the week you started. I gave you till the summer." He took box nine off the shelf, lifted out his own forms, and turned the pages slowly, the way you read something you've read before and keep hoping will say something else.

"He used the same pen," he said.

"I saw."

"He gave us the name off the building. All fourteen. He didn't care for us to have his." Khan turned another page. "He named me for a king. I've decided to keep it. It seemed a waste not to."

"Did he ever ask you?"

"Whether I'd like to be born?" He smiled at her. It was a good smile, and slightly crooked, and he was clearly used to it working. "A man in China made two little girls in 2018, and the whole world was horrified, and they put him in prison for three years. We were five. I remember the old man watching it on the news and laughing." He put the forms back in their order. "He did us by hand, you know. Years of guessing. The machines came after, and they learned it off us. Now anywhere with the machines can do in a week what took him half his life. That's what's frightened everybody. It isn't fourteen of us any more." He squared the pages. "Upstairs, they come in dying and go home like us. You know that? The networks can't mend the one broken thing without strengthening everything else. Quicker. Harder to hurt. Hardly ever ill. And their children will be the same."

"Page nine," said Carol, before she knew she was going to say it. *The therapy may produce additional changes in cellular function beyond the intended correction.* She had filed that sentence a hundred times, and taken it for the kind of thing lawyers put in. Now she heard it beside the law in the letter. It wasn't only the fourteen boxes on this shelf. It was every child upstairs. It would be Alma.

"Page nine," said Khan. "Three countries are going to count us. First they count, and then they decide what the count means."

"In Dayton they gave people letters," said Carol. "To sort them by. Then one day a man came with buses."

"I read about Dayton." He looked at her. "I read about you. Your son. I'm sorry. I read everything; it's a fault. I was fifteen. He said *I fired* when they wanted him to say something else, and I've thought about it a great deal." He put box nine back on the shelf, square with the others. "Are you going to tell them?"

"I don't know."

"I won't ask you not to. Nobody ever asked me anything. I'm not going to start that with you." At the top of the stairs he stopped. "When we were small, people felt sorry for us. Orphans. Now there's a word for us on the news, and people are frightened." He seemed to turn it over with real curiosity. "I find I prefer frightened."

---

"You went down," said Joy, the next morning.

"You knew I would."

"I told them you would." Joy sat down across from her, still in her coat.

"I met him at the children's hospital, my first year here," she said. "He was eight. He'd broken his wrist falling off a wall nobody that size could have climbed, and it knit in three weeks, and the radiologist asked to see him again. The next week a woman from the Institute came onto my ward and offered me a job. Their own nurse, for their own children, so they'd never need a hospital again. I knew why they wanted me. I took it anyway."

"Why?"

"Because the first night on the ward he'd asked me why nobody ever came to see him. So I came." Joy looked at her hands. "Every nurse on that ward had children of her own to go home to. He had a man with a pen."

Carol said nothing.

"If they find that basement, they close every floor. The machines, the clinic, all of it. Alma too." Joy didn't look away. "And the law's right. That's the worst of it. Whoever wrote it was picturing exactly him." She got up, and rinsed her cup, and dried it, which she never did. "Mom stayed in Dayton because somebody had to do the arms. These are mine. If you take it to them, I'll come with you and tell them what I did. I'm not asking you for anything."

"Everybody keeps not asking me," said Carol.

---

On Canada's Thanksgiving she and Joy roasted a turkey much too big for two people and didn't talk about it. On the Tuesday the inspectors came.

There were two of them, a woman about Carol's age and a young man with a tablet, and they were courteous and careful and knew what they were doing. Carol had laid the consent files out on the long table in the conference room, in order, every one. All day they read and asked, and she answered.

At four the young man looked up.

"Last thing, Ms. Flory, for the record. Are there any records at this site of research on human embryos?"

She had known all day that somebody would ask it, and that the answer would come out of her own mouth, not off a box.

She thought of one man's signature twice on every page, asking and saying yes, every year, for fourteen children. The law against it was right. Upstairs every week a mother signed under page nine, and her child went home changed in ways nobody had told her, and would hand them on. If Carol said nothing, all of it went on, and she would be the one keeping its records.

And if she said nothing and it came out, as things do, she would be a party to it. Ten years. A permanent resident convicted of anything that carried ten years lost her card. She would lose Canada: Joy's spare room, and the tenor line, and the Tuesday lessons, and Ruth's voice on a Sunday saying *again*. She would be put back over the bridge at Detroit, into a country where her passport said M, and an officer had once promised to keep it.

Then she thought of fourteen names on a list, in the autumn the registers began. She knew what a letter beside a name could turn into. Her son had burned a book so nobody could be sorted by it, and been shot on a levee for it. She thought of Joy in a courtroom. She thought of two children with Alma's illness whose networks were half written, and of Alma herself, counting the stairs on her way to bed.

She knew, too, that it wasn't the same as what Joel had done. The people in Joel's book had done nothing. Behind Carol's fourteen children stood a man with no printed name who had done all of it, and the same silence would keep him too.

"No," said Carol.

The young man wrote it down. She signed the page that said the set was complete, and the woman thanked her for her time.

In the elevator down, a boy of about five rode with his mother as far as the lobby, holding a balloon from the clinic. Carol knew his face from the photocopy of his health card. She had filed his form that morning. The boy looked at her, and then at the floor numbers, and counted them out loud as they went by, and got every one right. He would grow up quicker and harder to hurt than his mother knew, and he would pass it on, and one day somebody might write him down in a register. Carol had just made sure of all of it, with one word. She held on to the rail until the doors opened.

---

On Thursday a family came to her desk from Manila: a father, a grandmother, and a girl of four asleep on the grandmother's shoulder, whose legs had stopped working in the spring.

Carol asked what the girl liked to be called. The grandmother said *Tala*, and Carol wrote it small in the margin.

Then she turned to page nine and read it aloud. When she had read it, she told them what it meant, plainly and slowly. That Tala would walk. That she would also be changed in other ways that nobody could take out again: stronger, quicker to heal, hardly ever ill. That one day her children would be the same. That in some countries, since September, there was a word for children like that.

Dr. Bastien had come to the door of her office and stood there, white.

The father asked whether his daughter would live.

"That's what it's for," said Carol.

The grandmother looked at her for a long moment over the sleeping child.

"Is there anything else we should know?" she said.

Carol had read page nine to them so that nobody would sign for a child without knowing what she was signing. There was one thing more, and it was the thing that would close every floor of the building.

"No," she said.

The father held out his hand for the pen, and signed. Carol initialled the line for the witness, small and neat, the way she had once initialled a box at a counter in Dayton for a woman who wanted a pen.

When they had gone up, Dr. Bastien said, very quietly, that Carol could not say that to families.

"Then find somebody else to keep your forms," said Carol.

Dr. Bastien went back into her office and shut the door, and nobody else was hired.

---

At eleven on Sunday Carol propped the phone on the music rest, and Anna came on alone. Alma had fallen on the stairs at school that week. Not badly. She had been counting, and lost count.

"Joy says two years," said Carol. "Maybe less. When it's ready, you bring her here. And before anybody hands you a pen, I'm going to read you every page of it, out loud. All of it. What it does to her, and to her children, and what people will call her. Then you and Ben decide."

She was promising Anna what she had not given the grandmother from Manila, and she knew what that made her.

"Is it that bad?" said Anna.

"It's that good," said Carol. "That's the trouble with it."

Neither of them said anything for a while. In the background, in the room with the real piano, somebody was picking out a tune with one finger, slowly, getting most of it.

"I'm not telling you so you'll say yes," said Carol. "I'm telling you so you know."
