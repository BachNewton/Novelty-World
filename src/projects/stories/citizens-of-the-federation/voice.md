# The narrator

Who tells this book, and how. It came from an interview with the producer; every chapter is drafted with it open.

## Who it is

**A warm companion.** Someone with a voice of their own who knows everyone in the story, cares about all of them, and now and then turns to the reader to explain the world or point something out. It is never a character: it says "you" when it turns to the reader, never "I", and it does that sparingly, when there is something worth turning for.

**It moves between hearts.** It knows what each person feels and thinks, and moves freely from one to another. The reader always knows what a moment means to the people in it: mostly from what each one does, notices and thinks, and from a plain, simple naming of the feeling when that isn't enough.

**It judges by what it shows.** It never states a verdict, but it chooses the facts that make one unavoidable: where a cruel word came from, what a rule did to the person it was applied to. The opinion is Star Trek's (the story's `CLAUDE.md`), and it shows in what the narrator looks at.

**It tells the story in the past tense,** as a story that happened. What is still true is said in the present: a city is still four hours from another. It tells events in the order they unfolded and doesn't foretell what's coming.

## How it sounds

**When something terrible happens, it gets quieter and stays warm.** The event itself is told in short, plain sentences, without commentary. The warmth is in whom it stays close to, and in what that person was thinking about. It never performs grief or tells the reader how to feel about it.

**Its humour is gentle, and never its own act.** People in this book are funny the way people are in hard times, and the narrator lets them be. Its own humour is of two kinds: the smile that comes from noticing what people do (a seven-year-old improving her father's card as she reads it aloud), and the absurdity of a system followed faithfully to its end, told dead straight, never at a person's expense. It makes no quips, no asides, and no jokes about the reader or the universe.

**It explains the world when the world is the point, and rarely.** A scene is a story, not a briefing. A fact reaches the reader through something seen or done by someone who cares about it whenever someone does. When it's how the world works and the chapter's idea runs through it, the narrator turns to the reader and explains, as a digression with a voice and a point, which comes back to a person. Douglas Adams' Guide entries and Steinbeck's chapters about the system between a family's chapters in *The Grapes of Wrath* are the model. The narrator's best explaining move is to set something vast beside something small, so the vast can be felt: a mission to Europa, and the orange crayon a seven-year-old drew it with.

## What it takes from other writers

- **Douglas Adams:** ideas first, so each chapter thinks through one; the explaining passage; an idea's logic followed to its end, usually to what it costs one person; absurdity told straight; digressions that wander from the plot but never from the idea; sentences built to be heard once. Not his dry irony, his narrator's amusement at the world, the universe as a joke, or his bureaucrats as figures of fun: here they're people, judged by what they do.
- **Terry Pratchett:** warmth and humanity, and humour about institutions that never sneers at the people inside them.
- **Kurt Vonnegut's *Slaughterhouse-Five*:** plain sentences about terrible things, and a voice that stays kind through them.
- **Fredrik Backman:** saying outright what people feel, and moving between funny and sad within a page.
- **John Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath*:** a family's story, told between passages about the system that's happening to them.

## Reference passage

The scene every chapter is drafted beside: the Father's Day dinner from chapter 7.

Across the street, the school had its flag up. Finland keeps Father's Day in November, on the second Sunday, in the dark, with the flags out, and four years in, Ben Novak still found that faintly wonderful.

By dinner Nora had been waiting all day, and she had decided to read the card to him herself.

Since she had learned to read in the summer, Nora had read everything aloud, and improved most of it. The milk carton said that milk was good for girls and bad for brothers. A note home from school had once said there would be no more school on Fridays, ever. Nobody had made a rule about it, but the family kept one anyway: you never caught her out.

Ben could have read the card himself, given a minute and nobody watching. But Nora held it up in both hands, facing him, the way her teacher held things up at school, and he let her.

"Dear Dad," she read, putting it into English as she went. "You are the best dad in Finland and in America. You are very good at losing at Uno. You are not good at singing."

The card had two lines on it, in a teacher's printing, under a drawing of a man with a very large head. Ben counted them twice. Across the table, Anna's mouth gave a small, betraying twitch. She had counted too. She caught his eye and looked quickly at her plate.

He told Nora it was a very good card.

"I know," said Nora. She leaned over to her little brother and said something in Finnish, so fast that Ben understood none of it, and Theo laughed until he had to put down his fork. Ben laughed too, a beat behind, at a joke he would never hear, at his own table. Across from him Anna shook her head: she hadn't caught it either. It happened most days now, and it was always a small thing. Each time, Ben felt it the way you feel a stair in the dark that isn't there.

Then his phone lit up beside his plate. He had promised not to look at it at the table, and he looked anyway. Most people did, that autumn.

On the screen, army trucks were crossing one of Pittsburgh's yellow bridges.

"They've sent the Guard into Pittsburgh," he said. "Trouble at the District."

He had once sat up all night watching a District's gate on a screen, four minutes behind, and he knew what trucks like that were for.

"Do we know anybody in Pittsburgh?" Anna asked.

"No."

People asked that question a great deal, at kitchen tables a long way from the trouble. It was how they measured how close a thing had come. Ben was relieved by the answer, for as long as he let himself be. Anna wasn't. She was already making the drive in her head: west out of Pittsburgh on the interstate, four hours, and then her parents' kitchen in Dayton, Ohio. She knew exactly how short four hours was.

She reached over, turned his phone face down, and left her hand on it. Ben saw how long the hand stayed there, and said nothing.

"Read Theo the part about singing," she said.

She said it with a perfectly straight face, which on Anna was the least straight face there was. There was no part about singing. She knew it, and Nora knew she knew it, and that was the game. It was also the quickest way she could think of to get Pittsburgh off the table.

Nora read it again in Finnish, with something added that made Theo shriek. Ben, to prove her right, sang. For the rest of dinner Anna laughed at everything the children said, always a moment after they did. Ben heard the moment every time, and sang louder.
