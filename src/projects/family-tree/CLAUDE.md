# Family Tree

## Purpose

A tree that serves two aims, and a feature earns its place by serving one of them, or by fixing data the tree otherwise can't represent correctly:

1. **Connection:** answer "how does this person, whom I know by this name, relate to me or to another family member?"
2. **Story:** let the owner learn the story of their people, all of them, however they connect: where each line came from, how the world changed around it through time, and the cultures and identities it carried. Heritage exists for this aim.

It is still not a genealogy app: the story is told simply, from evidence, not as a database of records.

## Who owns what

- **Claude owns the data and the research:** the data model, what gets recorded and how, the research method and standards, sources, storage and migrations. Decide these, build them, and report what changed. The owner doesn't review them first.
- **The owner owns the UI and the project's heart:** what the tree shows and how, and its goals (the aims above). Ask before changing the UI or reinterpreting a goal. A data decision that changes what the UI shows is the owner's call.

The split between the UI and the data layer matters:

- **The UI** is a read-only viewer of a simple relationship tree. Push back on genealogy-software sprawl there: citations, confidence levels, event records and the like never appear on the cards. The person panel shows one person's details, including the research record the tree already keeps, but it stays a short readout.
- **Data model first.** The data model decides what research agents can record, and evidence research couldn't store is lost or buried in notes. So when research finds a kind of evidence the model can't hold, extend the model before the UI: rich data first, since the UI is easy to change later and a data model is hard to change.
- **The data layer** is also Claude's research record. It may carry what future research sessions need to judge how reliable a claim is and what is left to research, such as the research record: where each research question about a person stands, and on what sources. Each such field still has to be something a research session actually reads or acts on; push back on data nobody uses.

## One user

The owner is effectively the only person using this project. A change doesn't need to protect production while it's in progress: no cache-compatibility shims, no guarding against tabs running an old build, no staged rollouts. The owner can reload by hand. The only requirement is that the tree isn't left broken once a change is finished.

## A living tree

Everyone is family whether or not they've passed. The tree never shows who is dead or alive, and it never needs updating when someone dies. There is deliberately no deceased flag on a person. Don't add one, and don't add UI that implies one.

## Data model invariants

Types live in `types.ts`; pure operations and relationship terms live in `logic.ts`. The layout pipeline lives apart, in `layout/` (see Layout).

- **Unions.** Relationships between adults are one list of unions on each person, kept symmetric on both people. Each union has a status: married, divorced, ended-by-death, partner, or ex-partner. Status belongs to the union, never to the person.
- **Ended by death** is only needed when the survivor later remarried or repartnered. It renders like a marriage, not a divorce. The union can optionally record which of the two died. Only that person is ever called "late" in the relationship readout (by the survivor, and in composites like "husband's late wife"). When it isn't recorded, nobody is "late". It is wording only: it never affects layout or the topology hash, and nothing appears on the cards.
- **Names:** first name, last name (current), common name (nickname), and optional birth surname and middle name. The birth surname is research data: it never appears on the cards; the person panel shows it as "née …" when it differs from the last name. The middle name (a full name or just an initial) is a research aid: records tell same-named relatives apart by it. It never appears on the cards, which show the name people know someone by; only the person panel shows it. A name is spelled the way the person and their own household spelled it in life: an emigrant raised abroad keeps their native spelling ("Leppälä") even where US records wrote "Leppala", while a child who lived in the new country under the anglicized form keeps that form, even one born abroad who grew up there. The other spelling goes in notes. So the tree holds both spellings, and every name match (the app's search, the CLI's `find` and its duplicate guard) ignores accents and case, through `foldText` in `src/shared/lib/fold-text.ts`.
- **Notes:** optional free text per person, for research facts that have nowhere else to live (death dates, record numbers, alternate names). Notes never appear on the tree cards.
- **Birth date:** an optional research aid in a fixed, machine-readable shape, so tools and AI can read and edit it reliably instead of parsing notes. It is a partial ISO date: a year, a year and month, or a full date (shaped like `1931`, `1931-06`, `1931-06-16`), as precise as the sources allow. An approximate year (`~1931`) records a year the sources narrow to two adjacent years, such as an age on a census or marriage record; it holds the likelier of the two, and has no month or day. `treeProblems` rejects anything else, including impossible dates. Only the year appears on the cards, as a small green chip under the name; an approximate year keeps its "~", and a card without a birth date shows no chip at all. The full date shows only in the person panel. There is deliberately no death date field: that would be a deceased flag by another name, so death dates stay in notes.
- **Origin evidence:** seven optional strings per person that research fills: the facts about where someone came from that research would otherwise have to redo a search to recover. `birthPlace` is the birthplace as the strongest record words it, most specific first ("Capo d'Orlando, Messina, Italy", "Transylvania"); `birthPlaceToday` is the same place as it is today ("Capo d'Orlando, Sicily, Italy"); `emigrationDate` is when they left the homeland or arrived, shaped and validated like the birth date; `motherTongue` is the mother tongue as a record gives it ("Magyar"); `recordedPeople` is the people or nationality a record states for them, verbatim (a passenger list's "Race or People", a naturalization's nationality); `religion` is the faith or denomination as a record words it, verbatim ("r. kath.", "Greek Catholic"), a cultural marker that can point to a people (a Greek Catholic parish to Rusyn roots); `originLore` is what the family itself says about where this person or their family came from, and who said it ("Austrian, per Given Surname (2026)"). Lore is the family's own answer to "where is your family from?", the closest thing to what heritage means (see Heritage), but it is not record evidence: `recordedPeople` holds only what a record states. Their sources go in notes. `treeProblems` rejects a malformed emigration date and a value with surrounding whitespace. They are research aids: never shown on the cards, only in the person panel, and never part of layout or the topology hash. For anyone who may be living, the birthplace fields hold region and country only, never a town, and religion is not recorded (see Privacy).
- **Research record:** per person, where research on each of three questions stands: `family` (all their partners, every union and any status, and all their children, with any co-parent or none, are in the tree; never their parents), `birthYear` (known, or can't be found), and `heritage` (where their line came from, asked only of people with no parents in the tree). Each question is unset (nobody has looked) or a record of a status (confirmed, exhausted, or open), the full date it held as of, a non-empty list of sources, and a note. The research skill's `standards.md` defines what each status needs. `treeProblems` rejects an unknown status, an impossible date, an empty, padded or repeated source, and a confirmed birth year with no birth date. An approximate year (`~1931`) can be confirmed: it is precise enough for what the tree is for. A later partner or child doesn't invalidate a record, since the date says when it held. Sources and notes live in the public row, so they must be safe to publish (see Privacy). The record shows only in the person panel, never on the cards, and it never affects layout or the topology hash.
- **Heritage:** the peoples a person's lines came from. Its main purpose is to tell the story of the owner's peoples and give the tree color and life; it also points research at the right records. It is called heritage, not nationality (which means citizenship) or ethnicity, and it records origin, not identity. The design is `ideas/heritage-through-time.md`.
  - **The list: peoples, with optional regions.** Values come from a curated list in `heritages.ts`, so every value is one the UI can give a designed look; adding a people or a region is one entry there. Every entry names a **people**, the culture a line carries, independent of borders: a Hungarian born in Transylvania is Hungarian, whatever state holds the town today. A people with a country of its own and one without (the Sámi, the Roma, a Native American nation) are the same kind of entry. England, Scotland and Wales are separate peoples, and Irish covers the whole island. A **region** is an optional, finer homeland within a people (Sicily for an Italian line, Bavaria for a German one), for the eras before the nation-states, when the region was the homeland. A region's code carries its people's code, so a region can't be entered without its people, and the mix treats a region as a heritage of its own. Region slugs are unique across peoples, so one entered under the wrong people is an error rather than a new meaning. Finer detail still (a village, a ship) goes in the origin fields and notes.
  - **Legacy country codes.** A stored tree may still hold the present-day country codes heritage was once entered in. Each maps one to one to the people it stood for (Finland to Finnish, and so on); `normalizeTree` converts them on load, and the change-file parser answers an old code with its people's.
  - **Entries fill gaps.** An entry is a list split equally, and may include unknown. Each person's mix is derived and never stored: each parent passes on half of theirs, and a missing parent passes on an unknown half rather than letting the known half stand for the whole. The person's own entry then fills only whatever is still unknown; an unknown in the entry keeps its part unknown. So for someone without parents the entry is the whole mix, adding a parent with nothing known above changes nothing, and research that finds real heritage above an entry takes over that part automatically while the entry shrinks to what is still unknown. Only parent links pass heritage on, never a step relationship. An adoptive parent is a parent like any other, so an adopted child inherits the family's heritage: this is the heritage of the family's lines, not genetic ancestry. A mix lists the largest share first, ties going to the surname line: the father's line before the mother's, recursively, then the codes the person's own entry filled in, in the entry's order.
  - **Superseded entries.** Each entry reports how much of it is still in use. Below all of it, research has replaced part of it, and it needs review, because it may have been a guess covering both sides; at none it is dead data to clear. This is advisory, not a `treeProblems` error, and the CLI lists every superseded entry.
  - **Each generation's own answer.** A person's heritage is what they would answer, with reasonably accurate knowledge, to "where is your family from?", so it can change up a line as a family's culture changes. A newly found older origin never overwrites the heritage of the generations below it (see `ideas/heritage-through-time.md`, decision 6).
  - **Entered from evidence.** Research records the origin evidence first, then chooses the people from it: a mother tongue, a recorded people, a religion, a birthplace, the family's lore. Never from a surname, and never a people chosen only because someone was born in a place: where the evidence gives only a state that was home to several peoples, the people stays unknown and the evidence still shows. A region is added only where a record gives it. Entries go on the top person of each known line whose identity the evidence shows; descendants derive theirs. An ancestor found above an entry whose origin differs gets the origin evidence only, no entry, so the descendant's entry keeps the family's identity. The evidence goes in the person's origin fields, with its sources in notes. `treeProblems` rejects unlisted peoples, unlisted regions, a region of another people, repeats, and an entry of only unknown, which fills nothing.
  - **Symbols through time (data only).** Each people has a timeline of the symbols it used for itself through the eras, in `symbol-timelines.ts`: eras with year bounds, each naming the homeland and its symbol, and in a regional era the symbol comes from the share's region, which has a timeline of its own for those years. A people's own symbol in each era, never a regime's imposed on it. The picker takes a heritage and a birth year; a person without a birth date gets a year estimated from their nearest relatives (about 30 years a generation), which picks a symbol only and is never stored or shown as fact. An era is verified once it lists the sources that settle it; the entries start as the design doc's unverified proposal. Symbols are ids with a name until their art is added. The UI does not use the timelines yet: showing era symbols on the cards is the owner's call.
  - **On the cards.** Every card with any known heritage gets a flag centered on each corner, one per share for the largest four, sized by share and drawn whole in its official proportions, and a row of flag-and-percentage chips underneath listing the whole mix. A person whose mix is all unknown gets nothing. The person panel lists the same chips. Flags come from the `svg-country-flags` package (Wikimedia Commons artwork) through `flags.ts`, one per people: today's flag of the people's country, which for every migrated people is exactly the flag its country code showed, and a chip's hover text names that country. A region shows its people's flag. Adding a people means adding its flag there. The flags sit around the card, never inside it: the inside stays reserved for the person's details. The spouse gap is wide enough that a couple's facing flags never touch. Heritage never affects layout or the topology hash. The owner has decided that color blindness is not a design constraint. Living married-in adults get heritage from records like anyone else.
- **Optional string fields** use an empty string to mean "not set". They are always present, never undefined.
- **Schema evolution.** `normalizeTree` migrates saved trees on load, in the viewer and the CLI alike: it backfills new fields and converts old shapes. The viewer can't write, so the migrated shape reaches the row with the CLI's next write. Every new Person field must be handled there.

## Privacy

The public anon key can read the tree row (`supabase/family-tree.sql` gives it a select policy and nothing else), so everything in it, notes included, is effectively public. The private tables beside it (the research document, its history and the change records) are closed to that key; see "The viewer and the only writer". Never store private details about living people. A full birth date plus a name is identity-theft material, so a living person's birth date holds the year only; full dates are for people who have died. For the same reason, for anyone who may be living the birthplace fields hold region and country only, never a town. A living person's religion is a private detail too, so it is recorded only for people who have died. Heritage needs no extra measures: it sits mostly on long-dead origin people, and a derived mix says little the tree doesn't already imply. `research/` is gitignored because it names living people. Never commit it and never quote it in docs.

## The viewer and the only writer

The browser app is a read-only viewer: there is no edit UI. All edits are AI-driven through the CLI, which is the tree's only writer.

- **The private document and the public row.** The tree's source of truth is the research document, one private row (`family_tree_private`). The viewer reads the public row (`family_tree`), which holds the document's projection (`projectTree` in `logic.ts`), and nothing else ever reaches it. Until the document holds claims (`ideas/claims-and-private-data.md`), the projection is the document itself. Both rows are at the same version.
- **The row always holds the tree and an exact layout for it.** The public tree is one row, holding the tree and its fully optimized layout, tagged with the `topologyHash` of the tree it was solved for. A viewer must never see an unsolved or patched layout, so the browser never solves and never patches: `store.ts` loads the row and renders the stored layout as-is. A missing layout, or one whose hash doesn't match the tree, is a bug in whatever wrote the row, and the page shows an error rather than a fallback.
- **Reads and writes.** `persistence.ts` owns the public row's shape and its read, shared by the viewer (anon key) and the CLI. Writes, and every read of the private tables, live in `tools/tree-db.ts`, whose client uses the service-role key (`SUPABASE_SERVICE_ROLE_KEY` in `.env.local`) and never leaves that module. RLS gives the anon key read access to the public row only, and an update RLS refuses matches no rows rather than failing, which would read as a version conflict; that is why no write accepts a client from its caller. Nothing the page imports may reach `tools/`.
- **One commit.** Every tree write is one call to the database function `family_tree_commit`, one transaction: the research document and its projection move to the next version together, with the new layout when the topology changed, and the same transaction records the change (`family_tree_change`: the change file as submitted, the version it was checked against, the change list printed at apply) and a snapshot of the document (`family_tree_history`). So no write can skip its history or change record, and the two rows can't disagree.
- **No lost edits.** Both rows carry a version that counts tree writes. A tree write lands only if both are still at the version the writer loaded, and it bumps both; otherwise nothing lands and the writer must reload rather than overwrite. A database trigger (`supabase/family-tree.sql`) enforces the rule on each row for every writer, the service role included. A layout-only write doesn't move the version, but it too lands only if the public row is still at the version the writer loaded, so the layout always belongs to the tree it was solved for.
- **Private means three locks.** Each private table has row-level security with no policy, and its grants to `anon` and `authenticated` are revoked; the commit function is executable by the service role alone. `verify` proves it: it tries to read each private table and to call the function with the anon key, and fails unless each is refused or empty. It also checks that the public row is the projection of the document at the same version, that the stored layout fits it, and that the history holds that version. The CLI runs it after every write, and a research round runs it first.
- **History.** Every version's research document is kept in the history, which replaces local backups: every version among the latest 200, and beyond that the last version of each calendar week (UTC), forever. The commit thins older snapshots in the same transaction; a thinned version keeps its row and its change record, which are kept forever. The public row isn't kept, since the projection and an exact layout can be rebuilt from the document. `history` lists versions with their change lists, and `restore` commits an earlier version's document again as a new version, with a fresh layout solve; the version counter never goes back. Supabase is the only copy: there is no off-site backup.
- **Shareable links.** The view root and the 2D/3D mode live in the URL (`use-view-url.ts`), so a copied link opens the tree from the same person in the same view. Defaults are left out of it, the selection is not part of it, and changes replace the history entry rather than adding one. Both views open on the view root, never on the whole tree, which is too big to take in: 2D centres their card, and 3D aims at them and frames their immediate family (parents, siblings, partners, children) with room for their labels. Everything else is there to zoom out to.
- **The direct line.** Both views highlight the view root's direct line (`directLine`): the view root, every ancestor and every descendant, never partners, siblings or in-laws, and the parent links running along it (`isDirectLineLink`). Moving the view root moves the highlight.
- **The CLI** (`tools/tree-cli.ts`, pure part in `tools/tree-edit.ts`) is how research gets into the tree: find and show people, list the research gaps family by family with totals per question, list which heritage entries research has superseded, list who has origin evidence but no heritage decision on their line (`undecided`), list the peoples, regions and symbol timelines with what is left to verify (`peoples`), and apply a JSON change file through the `logic.ts` edit functions, validated by `treeProblems`; also list the history, restore a version, and verify. It dry-runs by default, writes through the one commit, and runs `verify` after every write. The `family-tree-research` skill describes the workflow and privacy rules around it.
- **Apply solves, then writes both.** When a change alters the tree's topology (its `topologyHash` no longer matches the stored layout's), `apply` solves the new tree's exact layout before writing anything, then commits the tree and its layout together. When the topology is unchanged (a name, a note, a birth date), it writes the tree alone and the stored layout stays. A solve that fails or can't prove the optimum stops the write, so the row never holds a tree without its layout. A dry run solves too, so it shows what the write would do.
- **Relayout** re-solves the current tree's layout and, with `--write`, stores it as a layout-only write to the public row; the document, its version and the history are untouched. It is for changes to the layout code, which change layouts without changing any tree; the dry run says whether the new layout differs from the stored one.
- **New name fields** flow through the CLI (rename, new people, search) once they are added to its empty-name defaults, which typecheck forces.

## Research edits

The owner wants as few approval requests as possible. When research finds evidence for a change and confidence is high, make the change with the CLI and report it afterwards. High confidence includes a near-certain inference from records: a divorce implied because both spouses remarried while alive, a birth surname from a marriage record that names the parents. Record the evidence in `research/` either way.

Research method, record keeping and the data model are Claude's (see "Who owns what"): decide them and report what changed.

The same goes for modelling and interpretation questions the owner finds hard to call: how heritage is classified, what counts as a line's origin, how the story is structured. Decide them against the two aims above, write the decision and its reasoning into the relevant doc, and report it; the owner judges the result rather than setting the rule up front.

Ask first only when:

- the evidence conflicts, or confidence isn't high. A single mention or a name match without matching relatives isn't enough. A finding like that stays in `research/`, or goes into notes marked "Possible:";
- the change deletes an existing person;
- the change would put a private detail about a living person into the public row (see Privacy).

## Responsive: phone and desktop

The tree must work from 360px phones through ultrawide desktop. Check both whenever you touch components.

- `pan-zoom.tsx` handles pointer events: one-finger or mouse drag pans, a two-finger pinch zooms, and the wheel zooms. The canvas disables browser touch gestures, and a drag suppresses the click that would otherwise select a card. It also glides to a content point on demand (`PanZoomHandle.panTo`); any pan or zoom by the user cancels a glide in progress.
- `person-panel.tsx` is a floating, read-only card: the selected person's full name (middle name, nickname, birth surname), their relation to the view root, birth date, the origin evidence that is set, notes and research record (each question's status, as-of date and sources). On narrow screens it sits at the bottom right with its width capped to the viewport minus a gutter. From `md` up it moves to the top right. It must stay fully usable at 360px.
- The toolbar in `family-tree.tsx` shortens its labels below `sm`. The name search (`name-search.tsx`) sits inline from `sm` up; below that it is a magnifying-glass button that opens the field over the whole toolbar. Jumping to a result selects the person, so below `md` the card lands near the top of the canvas rather than the center, where the person panel would cover it. Arrow-key navigation between cards is desktop-only, so everything it reaches also needs a tap path.
- Size tap targets for fingers, not just for a mouse pointer.

## Layout

Card size is fixed and layout never measures text. Any change to card content (`components/node.tsx`) must keep the text inside the card height.

The layout pipeline (`computeLayout` in `layout/compute-layout.ts`) is a sugiyama layout with an exact crossing minimization (`layout/decross.ts`), solved by OR-Tools CP-SAT in a Python child process (`layout/solver/decross.py`, which also holds the model). It runs only on the desktop, in the CLI; its result is stored in the row beside the tree, identified by `topologyHash`. Nothing the page imports may reach `layout/`: the page's bundle holds neither the sugiyama pipeline nor the solver. Anything short of a proven optimum, including a missing venv, fails loudly; there is no fallback layout.

- **Chains and lines.** Each partner chain (a single person, a couple, or a longer chain like [ex, person, current]) is one layout node, but the solve counts crossings between the lines as drawn: each child's line leaves its parents' marriage midpoint (or a lone parent) and ends at the child's own card, so a line attaches at a port along its chain. Who sits next to whom in a chain is fixed by the partner rules; which way round the whole chain is drawn is a variable of the exact solve, one per chain of two or more. Where flipping a chain costs no crossing, it is drawn whichever way puts its members nearer their own parents.
- **Lines never share a column.** No vertical piece of one family's line (the drop from the parents, or a child's descent from the elbow row) may run down the same column as a vertical piece of another family's line: a married-in spouse under their in-laws' drop reads as one more of their children. Placement keeps any two such pieces that could meet within a row gap at least `LINE_CLEARANCE` apart (`layout/coord.ts`). d3-dag's `coordSimplex` places the chains first; when its placement breaks the rule, CP-SAT (`layout/solver/coord.py`) solves the placement again exactly: the same weighted line length, with the pieces kept apart, then as little movement from `coordSimplex`'s placement as that optimum allows, with tiebreaks that pin most of the answer. It is not fully unique: two solves of the same tree can place some cards differently at the same optimum (see `edge-routing-notes.md`). Stored layouts make that harmless: the viewer always shows the layout solved once for the tree.
- **Connector geometry** lives in `edge-geometry.ts`, shared by the renderer (`components/edges.tsx`) and the layout tests. Two parents joined by a union line drop from the middle of that line, at its height, wherever it runs; a lone parent drops from the bottom center of their card.

The solver needs a project-local Python venv (`layout/solver/.venv`, gitignored) with the pinned `layout/solver/requirements.txt`. `npm run setup:family-tree-solver` creates or updates it, and needs Python 3 on the PATH. The layout tests and the CLI's layout solves need it.

Read these before changing the pipeline:

- `hybrid-decross-notes.md`: exact vs. heuristic decross, why the solve is exact, why the solver is CP-SAT, and how it counts crossings between drawn lines and orients chains.
- `edge-routing-notes.md`: connector geometry, elbow rows, and why two families' lines are kept out of one column in placement rather than rerouted in the renderer.

To prove a change didn't alter layout, use `layout-invariants.test.ts` and the pinned snapshots in `__snapshots__/`. The production-sized fixture runs only in the slow suite (`*.slow.test.ts`, via `npm run test:slow`). Run it after any change to the layout code, then `relayout` (dry run first) so the stored layout follows the new code.
