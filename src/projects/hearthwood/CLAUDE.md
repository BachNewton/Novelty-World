# Hearthwood

A cozy 3D game about a wandering hedge-witch, the cats and dogs they befriend, and the people they help along the way. You walk an endless northern land of forest and mountain made fresh from a seed: by day you wander and forage, meet travellers and help them with your magic, by evening you cook and brew at your campfire, and the animals you have won over follow you wherever you go.

It lives at Games → 3D Games → Hearthwood, in Three.js through `@react-three/fiber`, like the site's other 3D games. The concept and the platform are decided; everything below them is the proposed design, open to the owner's review, with the undecided parts under Open questions.

## Who it's for

Hearthwood is a gift from the owner to their partner, **Elliott**, who uses **they/them** pronouns: always write and speak of Elliott, and of the witch, as they. The witch is **Elliott the Witch**: the player plays as Elliott.

Everything in the game is chosen for what Elliott loves: cats and dogs, and befriending animals that then follow them; nature, forests and mountains; hiking; cooking and campfires; magic, witchy and pagan things; and cozy, relaxing, chill games. Elliott is very social: they like talking to people and helping them, so the land has people in it with stories to tell and help to ask for. And Elliott loves a good mystery, with a soft spot for Sherlock Holmes, so some of those stories are mysteries to solve.

Elliott plays on a Pixel 8 Pro (touch), on a laptop (keyboard and mouse) and on a laptop on the TV with an Xbox controller.

## Pillars

- **Nothing is ever lost.** No fail states, no combat, no hunger or health meter, no timer that punishes. A meal that comes out unexpected is a new dish, not a failure. An animal never runs away, falls ill or dies.
- **Cooking is for joy and for friendship, never for survival.**
- **Every walk finds something.** The world is generated so that wandering always turns up a herb, a view, a cat or a ruin, and never a long empty stretch.
- **Calm by default.** Soft sound, slow light, no notifications nagging for attention. The game waits as long as the player likes.
- **Witchcraft is gentle and earthy**: hedge-witchery, herbs, the moon and the Wheel of the Year, treated with warmth and respect, never as spooky set dressing.

## Target device

Hearthwood's floor is a **Pixel 8 Pro** in Chrome, held upright or sideways. It replaces the site-wide floor of phones up to four years old within this project: layouts are designed and checked at its viewport. Laptops and a laptop on a TV with a controller are above that floor.

**Performance comes last.** While the game is a prototype, the work is making it play right and look right; making it run smoothly on the phone's GPU (Tensor G3's Mali-G715) is a later pass, done once play and look are settled, and only as far as it turns out to be needed. Nothing is held back for a budget before then. The world and the art gallery always show a stats.js frame meter in the top right, to watch the frame rate while playing. Every knob that trades how the world looks for how fast it draws (how far the land reaches and how fine it is with distance, how far plants are drawn and in which detail, thinning, the pixel ratio, the sun's shadow map) lives in one quality profile, `quality.ts`; the game runs on its desktop profile, tuned for looks (plants in full detail out to a long way with no thinning, the native pixel ratio up to 2, 2048 shadow maps), and a phone's profile is another of the same shape, added when the performance pass comes. Haze is never one of those knobs: it belongs to the weather (see The world).

## Art, gameplay and engine are decoupled

The art must be workable on its own, without touching the rules or the engine, and the reverse. So the layers stay apart:

- **The engine** (generating the world, time, input, streaming) is pure where it can be, and knows nothing of how anything looks.
- **Gameplay** (the walker, the cats' behaviour, camp, foraging, cooking) is state and rules, with no Three.js in it, and is tested without drawing anything.
- **Art** (models, materials, palettes, motion, light) takes plain data in (a cat, a tree's seed, the hour) and gives drawing back. It never decides anything the game depends on.
- **The scene** is the thin layer between them that draws the game's state with the art.

A change of look is a change in the art layer alone; a change of rules never needs the art redone.

## The loop

A day has a shape, though nothing forces it:

- **Morning to dusk, the walk.** Out from camp in any direction: forage, meet animals and people, find places, climb for a view. Companions come along and wander near.
- **Evening, the hearth.** At the campfire: cook, brew, feed the companions, sit and look at the stars. Sleeping by the fire starts the next day.
- **Moving on.** Camp goes where Elliott goes: when they choose, it is packed up and pitched again wherever they like, a lake shore, a high valley, a ledge under a summit. Companions and recipes always come along.

## The world

The world is one continuous landscape, endless in every direction, made from a seed with the site's `seeded-random` as pure functions in the project's engine. Any spot always comes out the same, however the world was walked to get there, so it is generated by area as the player goes, and every part of it is unit-testable. `?seed=<text>` opens another world.

**One landscape, never a world of bowls.** The land comes from a few large, slowly varying fields across the whole world: how high the ground broadly lies, how wet it is, how hilly, and where the mountains rise. Nothing has a hand-shaped rim; valleys are simply the land between ranges, in every shape, and a long walk always leads somewhere different.

**Mountains** run as long winding ranges along the lines where a broad field crosses zero, so they wind for kilometres, rise and fall along their crests (where a crest dips there is a pass) and part in wide gaps, leaving the lowlands between them open. A slow field of its own gives each range its character, drawn from the real mountains the game's owner loves, by way of all of Scandinavia:

- **High alpine ranges**, like Norway's Jotunheimen and the Lyngen Alps (and so the Tetons and the Alps): jagged peaks rising steeply out of pine forest to around 900 m above the lowlands, far above the treeline, through alpine meadow and scree to snowfields on the tops. Cirques hold tarns, cascades fall down the valley walls, and the high green valleys hold seter, the turf-roofed summer farms.
- **Fells**, as in Lapland: broad rounded tops rising bare above the trees.
- **Long rounded ridges side by side**, as in central Sweden and Norway or the Appalachians: folded country whose ridges all run roughly one way across a world, layered into the distance and wooded to their tops.
- **Lakeland lowlands**, as in Finland: open country of birch and pine with many lakes.

Big mountains are common enough to stand on most horizons, and a walk can reach them; the walker climbs anything up to about 45° and wades anything shallower than a paddle, so cliffs and lakes turn them aside, passes and valleys lead through, and the world has no wall or edge.

**Bands.** The treeline sits low, as it does at this latitude, so the peaks rise far above the forest: woods below about 255 m, alpine grass above, bare rock from about 420 m (and on any ground too steep to hold soil), snow from about 610 m, clinging to steeper ground the higher it goes. Each band's edge wanders up and down the slopes.

**Water stands on terraces**, so it never needs to know the whole map and never runs uphill. The water table is the broad lie of the land rounded down to steps of a few metres, so every lake and every reach of river is level. Lakes lie wherever the land dips below its water: in the wet lowlands' hollows, and as tarns in cirques up in the ranges, each held in by a lip of moraine. Rivers follow the borders of a pattern of cells laid over the world in a warped frame, so they wind, meet at junctions and part, never close into rings, and start at springs where a border holds no river; each cuts its own channel and a broad valley whose sides rise gently from it, keeps to the lowlands and hills, and fades out as the land climbs high above it. Where a river crosses from one terrace down to the next it drops over a waterfall, and just below each terrace's edge the land rises in a gentle bank that holds the higher water in. Rivers are always shallow enough to wade.

**Regions with a character of their own**, each with its own slowly varying field, nudged by the land, and blending softly across a border a few hundred metres wide, so a long walk is a journey through country after country:

- **Greenwood**, home: mixed oak, fir and birch with open meadow, flowers and bushes. It holds the land round the world's origin.
- **Birch vale**, the lakeland: light birch woods with pine and golden aspen, white and yellow flowers, ferns, chanterelles, fairy rings.
- **Pine highlands**, the mountains: Scots pine and fir up to the treeline, rocks and heather, and a misty air of their own.
- **Heath**: open moorland of golden grass and heather (purple, pink, and gorse in flower), lone pines and birches, tors and boulders, and a clear sky.
- **Maple wood**: dense autumn woods of red, orange and gold maple with golden aspen, bracken, mushrooms and fallen giants.

**Life by area.** The plants and stones of each 64 m square are scattered from that square's own seed. The woods stand in groves and open into glades, cover as much of the land as their region says, thin out to the treeline and keep back from water, camp and the places; each region has its own trees, high and low, and its own undergrowth in the open and under the trees. Fireflies come out over the meadows at night, more by water and in the softer regions.

**Places by area.** The world is cut into cells of about 190 m, and about half of them hold one place, found from the cell's own seed and its land alone, so every walk finds something every few hundred metres: a stone circle, a cairn with a bench on a rise, a tor of weathered slabs, a wayside shrine with a candle and an offering, a fallen giant tree, a fairy ring, a seter, a tarn, a cascade down a valley wall, or a waterfall wherever a river falls from one terrace to the next. Each region has its own mix, each kind is put where it fits best among a scatter of candidates (a stone circle on level ground, a cairn on a rise, a tarn in a hollow), and a place keeps well inside its cell, so what it levels and clears never reaches the next. Trails between places are left for later: they relied on knowing the whole map.

**Camp**, for now, is a level clearing on the greenwood's meadow a short walk from the world's origin, with its fire, two seat logs, a bedroll and a pot on a tripod. A camp pitched anywhere is gameplay for later.

**What is drawn, and how far.** There is no practical limit to how far the player sees: on a clear day the land runs to the horizon, as with Minecraft's Distant Horizons. The far world is the real land at coarser and coarser detail. The land is a quadtree of square tiles, each the same number of vertices, each level twice the width of the one inside it; a tile splits into four finer ones as the camera comes within a fixed number of its widths, so a triangle covers about the same part of the screen at any distance and the cost grows only with the logarithm of how far the view reaches (about a hundred kilometres, past where the curve of the earth has hidden all but the highest peaks). Far tiles sample the world's fields directly at their own spacing, leaving out what is finer than that spacing can show, so distant land never shimmers. Tiles are worked out in workers off the main thread, kept a while once made, and the drawn set changes over all at once when every tile it needs is ready, so no hole ever opens; each tile eases into its parent's shape over the stretch before it is swapped for it, and hangs a skirt from its edges, so levels join with no seam or pop. The land drops away by the curve of the earth. Plants are drawn as instances, in full near by and in a simpler shape further out, with no thinning; past where trees are drawn, the woods are painted into the land as their canopy, mottled crown by crown, fading in before the last trees so no edge shows. Places stream in within about a kilometre. Depth uses a reversed depth buffer, precise from a hand's breadth to the horizon with no change to how the materials shade.

**Haze is weather**, never a way of hiding the edge of the world: the world must hold up on a perfectly clear day on its own. `?haze=<n>` scales it (0 is a perfectly clear day) until the weather drives it.

## Long walks

The journey part. The world has no edge, so a walk can go on as long as Elliott likes: up through the woods to a pass with a view over two valleys, along a ridge, or to a summit for the view and nothing else, then gliding down off it on the broom. A long hike may take more than a day, with a camp on the way: a small fire on a ledge, a meal from what is in the pack, the stars from higher up.

## Companions: cats and dogs

The heart of the game. The woods hold stray **cats and dogs**, every one generated, so no two are the same, and the player befriends them, and once befriended they **follow**: they come along on walks, keep near, nose about, and wait and come running when the witch glides off a ridge. At camp they nap by the fire. Petting one is always available and always answered.

**Cats** look like real cats because their coats are drawn from real cat coat genetics: the base colour and its dilute, orange and the tortoiseshell and calico it makes, tabby patterns (mackerel, classic, spotted, ticked), white markings from none to van, colourpoint, long or short hair, and eye colour.

**Dogs** are the dogs of the north: a mixed population in which spitz types (prick ears, a tail curled over the back, a plush double coat, like a Lapphund, a Finnish Spitz or an elkhound) are common, among all sorts of mixes. Their build varies far more than a cat's (size from small to big, legs, body, muzzle length, ears pricked, folded or dropped, tails curled, plumed or straight), and their coats come from real dog coat genetics, simplified: black, liver and their dilutes, sable, fawn and red, black and tan and tricolour, brindle, a rare merle, white markings, and coat length.

**One animal underneath.** Cats and dogs share one four-legged body, rig, motion and coat-shader approach, each species its own proportions, gait, postures, coat genetics and behaviour, so a third kind of animal later is a variant, not a new system.

**Befriending** differs as the animals do:

- **A cat** is won over gradually: it watches from a distance, then comes closer for food, and follows once it is given the dish it loves. Each cat has a favourite dish and a few it likes.
- **A dog** comes more readily: a friendly one bounds up, a shy one needs a treat held out and patience; a game of fetch with a stick wins most dogs.
- **Each has a personality** from a few traits that show in how it moves and how close it walks: for cats bold or shy, lazy or busy, cuddly or aloof; for dogs bold or shy, calm or playful, cuddly or independent.
- **A name the player gives it.**

Companions, as built:

- **Strays by area** (the engine, pure and unit-tested): the world is cut into the places' cells, and each cell has a few strays or none, from its own seed and its land alone, about one for every cell or two, so animals are met often but each is special. Cats are commoner by a seter, a shrine, a stone circle or a fairy ring and at the edges of the woods, dogs by a seter and out in open country; a seter now and then keeps a second, barn cat, and camp's own cell always has a cat at the woods' edge a short walk from the fire, so the first walk out meets one. Each stray is a cat or a dog generated from its own seed, with a home spot (dry, gentle ground, below the bare rock) that it wanders round.
- **A stray's day** (gameplay, pure and unit-tested): round its home it idles, wanders, sits, lies or loafs, and noses about, each choice seeded and coloured by its nature (a busy one wanders more, a lazy one lies longer), never far from home for long. Near Elliott it reacts by its temper and how warm it is to them: a shy one keeps its distance and watches, and steps back if Elliott comes closer than it likes, further if they come running; a bold one, or a friendly dog, comes to see, a friendly dog at a bound; once warm enough for a pet, any of them lets Elliott come right up. Held out, a cat's favourite treat tempts it nearer.
- **Getting about**: every animal steers over the land by the walking rules (no deep water, no cliffs; a cat keeps its paws out of water a dog would wade), turns as an animal turns, eases its pace up and down, steers round Elliott and the other animals and is never inside them, so none ever walks through Elliott or stands in their way. Its pace picks its gait: a cat walks and, faster, bounds; a dog walks, trots and runs.
- **Befriending a cat** is patience, as the doc says, without cooking yet: Elliott near and calm (walking or still, never running) warms it slowly, so it comes closer; within one visit the warming slows once it has come a good way, so a shy cat takes a few visits or one long, quiet one, and patience alone never quite wins it. Warm enough, it allows a pet, which warms it more, and a treat from the basket (anything foraged but the fly agaric) warms it too; each cat has a favourite treat, which wins it at once. **A cat's favourite dish arrives with cooking**: until then its favourite is one of the forage. Most cats allow a pet within a minute or two of calm company; a shy one takes several.
- **Befriending a dog**: a friendly dog starts a little warm and a pet or a treat wins most at once; a shy dog needs patience first, as a cat does. Fetch is for later.
- **Warmth only grows**: nothing Elliott does cools an animal. It shows without words or bars: in how near it comes and how it holds itself, its tail going up or its wag as it warms to Elliott close by, and a little heart rising over it each time it warms another step (a burst of hearts when it is given what it loves, or won).
- **Petting and treats**: an animal is offered like a ripe berry, by the one rule everything offered shares, so a berry and a cat compete fairly: in first person the one nearest the middle of the view within reach of the eyes, in third the nearest in front within reach. The prompt shows a pet (with the animal's face) and the treat held out; the treat held out goes round the basket, moving on after each one that was only liked. Petted, an animal stops, turns to Elliott and answers: a cat's tail goes up and it slow-blinks, a dog wags and wiggles, and a paw rises over a companion.
- **Naming**: won over, the animal joins and a naming card opens with a name suggested from a cozy list (cats and dogs each their own, many Nordic, and a dog called Watson), the same names for the same animal and never one a companion already has: another suggestion, or keep it, or type one (on a phone, its own keyboard). On a controller the d-pad steps through the suggestions and X keeps one: a controller can't type. The name shows softly over a companion when it is near.
- **Following**: each companion keeps near Elliott at its own distance (a cuddly one close, an independent one further, a cat further than a dog) on its own side behind them, so they spread out rather than queue; walking, it keeps up, now and then noses off to something aside and bounds to catch up; when Elliott stops it comes in and sits, and lies down if Elliott stays; by the campfire they settle round the fire. Left far behind (a long glide on the broom, a lake between them) a companion out of sight is put back on Elliott's trail behind them, out of sight too, and comes running: nothing is ever seen to appear or vanish. **Three follow at once**; when a fourth joins, the one who has walked longest goes back to camp to nap by the fire, and petting one there brings it along again, sending the longest walker back in its place.
- **The companions' row**: a portrait of each in its own coat's colours (from the same genetics as its coat) with its name, a little flame by one resting at camp, in the top left corner; it shows for a few seconds when one joins and whenever it is opened.
- **Art**: the cats and dogs are drawn a little larger than life in the world (about a third), as the small forage is, so they read from a few steps off beside Elliott; their stride follows the ground they cover all the same. They are drawn within about ninety metres. Their coat material is the animals' own standard shader, not yet one of the shared materials the features' chunks reach, so the haze and grade don't reach them (it matters only at a distance).

**How a cat is made** (decided, built in `cats/`):

- **Everything from one seed**, each part (genes, coat, build, personality) from its own seeded stream, so adding to one never changes another.
- **Genes are drawn at street-population frequencies**: black, chocolate and cinnamon, each dense or dilute; orange on the X chromosome (one copy for a tom, two for a queen, so only queens are tortoiseshell or calico, and most orange cats are toms); agouti, which shows the tabby pattern (ticked over mackerel over classic, mackerel broken into spots by a modifier), while orange shows its pattern regardless; white spotting, none or a locket with no copies, low to middling white with one, high white to van with two; a rare dominant white; colourpoint; long hair. A population looks like a real one: mostly black, tabby and their dilutes, about one in six long-haired, colourpoints rare. Eyes follow the coat: points are always blue, high white is often blue or odd-eyed, the rest run green to copper. Silver, smoke and the other breeders' genes are left out.
- **Build** varies size (toms bigger, with broader heads), stockiness (a cobby cat runs to shorter legs), leg, back, tail and ear length, as moved and scaled bones on one shared sculpture, so the build costs nothing per cat.
- **Personality** is three traits from −1 to 1, and shows in motion: a bold cat holds its head and tail higher, a shy one crouches with its ears turned out; a busy cat looks round, flicks its ears and twitches its tail more often; a lazy one breathes slower and lids its eyes; a cuddly cat slow-blinks.
- **The model** is one smooth skinned body: signed-distance forms (the shared toolkit in `src/shared/lib/three/sdf.ts`, which Betrayal's figures also use) blended round a skeleton of pivots, each vertex following the bones of the forms nearest it, so the body bends smoothly where the forms were blended. The head is meshed on a finer grid than the body so the ears keep their edge. Faces are painted, not sculpted: big round eyes with a slit pupil and a glint, a small nose, the inside of the ears.
- **The coat is a shader**: every marking is a pattern function of where a point lies on the cat in its bind pose, so it moves with the body: the tortie's patches (brindled, or bigger and cleaner on a calico), the tabby pattern with its forehead M, cheek lines, leg bars and tail rings, the colourpoint's mask and points, and the white spotting, which grows from the chest and belly through the paws, legs, muzzle and blaze to the sides and back, leaving the head's cap and the tail for last. Long hair is a fuller silhouette (ruff, britches, plume) and a softly lumped surface; no fur cards.
- **Motion** is pure functions of the cat's clock, the distance it has walked and its speed: postures for standing, walking (the lateral-sequence walk, each paw planted while it is down), sitting and loafing, blended over a second as the activity changes before the legs are solved to their paws, with the tail up in greeting over any of them, and breathing, blinks, ear flicks, look-arounds and tail sway laid on top as seeded occasional movements.
- **Budget**: a cat is about 12,000 triangles short-haired, 15,000 long-haired, and two draws (its body and a soft blob shadow), with one compiled coat shader shared by every cat. Meshing a hair length takes about a second on a desktop and runs once a session in a worker. The coat's fragment shader is the heaviest part, a few layers of value noise; if several cats close up ever cost too much on the target phone, the pattern can move to the vertices.

**The shared animal** (decided, built in `animals/`): every four-legged animal is one core with its species as data on it, so a cat and a dog, and the forest's animals after them, are variants rather than systems of their own.

- **One skeleton**: the same bones in the same order for every species (pelvis, chest, neck, head, ears, three-segment legs, a seven-bone tail, and a muzzle, a jaw and a tongue), each species putting the pivots where its own anatomy has them. A bone a species has no use for (a cat's jaw) simply has nothing following it. An animal's build moves its bones and scales them along their own axes; a leg's scale stays equal down and forward, so it still bends without shearing.
- **One sculpting**: a species gives its body as signed-distance forms in layers, each meshed on its own grid, and each form says which bone it follows; a form may also mark a part of its own for the shader (a dog's tongue). A sculpture serves every animal of its kind, meshed once a session in a worker of its own.
- **One motion**: a species gives a posture for each of its activities; the core blends postures over a change of activity, steps the paws through a gait, solves the legs to their paws, and swings the tail (a slow sway, and a wag that is stiff from the root or whips down the tail). **A gait is a table**: when each paw lands in the cycle, how long it is down, how far it sweeps and how high it lifts, so a walk, a trot, a gallop or a hop is a new table, not new code. A dog's paw that is down stays planted: it sweeps back only as far as the body travels while it is down.
- **One coat shader**: the core supplies the noise, a painted eye, an ear's cup, the hollows' shading and the fur's sheen; a species supplies its pattern functions of the bind-pose position.
- **One preview**: `?cats` shows the cats and dogs together in the meadow, lines the dogs up to compare their builds, and focuses one animal to show each of its species' activities.

**How a dog is made** (decided, built in `dogs/`):

- **Everything from one seed**, each part from its own seeded stream, like a cat.
- **Two parents' types.** The north's dogs come from a few working types: spitz (Lapphund, Finnish Spitz, elkhound and Karelian kinds), the commonest by far, then shepherd, hound, retriever, collie and the small farm terrier. A dog has a mother's and a father's type, the same for about a third of dogs (landrace dogs) and different for the rest (mixes). Every gene takes one allele from each parent's type's pool, so a mix inherits as a mix does, and its build lies halfway between the two types with its own variation.
- **Genes**, simplified from real dog coat genetics: E (a mask, normal, or e/e: a clear red, fawn or cream with no eumelanin in the coat), K (dominant black, brindle, or the agouti series showing), A (sable, wolf grey, tan points, recessive black), a saddle modifier turning tan points into a black saddle, B and D (black, liver, blue, isabella), the red's intensity (red, fawn, cream), merle (rare, and hidden on a clear red), white spotting (S solid or a chest star, si Irish white, sp piebald, more white with each step down), a long coat, and a plush double coat. Eyes follow the coat: dark or brown, amber on liver and the dilutes, sometimes blue with merle or much white; the nose is the eumelanin's colour even on a red dog. A coat is named as breeders say it: "black and tan", "red sable", "wolf grey with black mask", "tricolour", "blue merle and white", "white and black"; a lightly shaded sable is called by its red, as a Finnish Spitz is.
- **Build** varies far more than a cat's: size from about 30 cm at the shoulder to over 60, leg length, back length, muzzle length, skull breadth, stockiness, ear and tail size, and a small dog's head is big for its body. Ears are pricked, folded or dropped (a mix's ears fall between its parents': a prick-eared and a drop-eared parent mostly give folded ears); tails are curled over the back (a curl mostly comes through in a mix), sabre, straight, or a feathered plume on a long coat.
- **Personality** is three traits from −1 to 1, each type leaning a little its own way: shy to bold, calm to playful, independent to cuddly.
- **The model** is sculpted at a cat's scale (about 30 cm at the shoulder), so it has the cat's detail, and its size scales it into the world: a deep chest and a tucked-up loin, legs that stand tall with angulated hind legs, a skull with brows, a muzzle with a nose leather and upper lips, a jaw that opens and a tongue that comes out. One sculpture per kind of ear and length of coat, nine in all; a plush or long coat is a fuller silhouette (a spitz's ruff, britches, a brush of a tail, feathering behind the legs and along the belly) and a softly lumped surface.
- **The coat is a shader**: where the eumelanin lies for the pattern (solid; clear; a sable's dark tips along the back, head, ears and tail; a wolf grey's banded saddle and cape with pale legs and cheeks; tan points, with their pips over the eyes, the cheeks, throat, chest, legs and under the tail; or a saddle), with brindle's broken streaks and a mask over it, merle's torn patchwork in the eumelanin, the white spotting growing from a chest star and the toes through the belly, muzzle, tail tip, legs, blaze and an Irish collar to the sides, leaving the head's cap, the ears and the tail's root for last, and fine streaks along the hair. The face is painted: dark round-pupilled eyes with a glint, the nose leather with its nostrils and a wet shine, the line of the mouth, which the jaw stretches into the mouth's dark as it opens, and the tongue.
- **Motion**: standing, a walk (a lateral sequence like a cat's), a trot in diagonal pairs, a bounding gallop with the spine flexing, sitting, and lying down like a sphinx with the forearms out in front; a happy greeting puts the tail up and going, wiggles the hips with it, softens the ears back and opens a grin. The tail's carriage follows its kind and the dog's mood (a curl only loosens when shy; other tails tuck or rise), a curled tail wags stiffly from its root and a straight one whips, and at speed it streams out behind. Idles: panting, ear flicks, the tilted head, a look round, and the nose down to the ground, snuffling. A playful dog bounces, wags and looks about more; a calm one lies with its eyes half shut.
- **Budget**: about a cat's: a dog is under 20,000 triangles and two draws, with one compiled coat shader for every dog; meshing a sculpture takes about half a second on a desktop.

## The animals of the forest

Elliott loves the animals of the forest, so the land is full of them: the animals a Nordic forest really holds, each where and when it really lives. **Nothing is hunted or harmed, and no animal is a threat**: the bear and the lynx are wonders to come across, not dangers.

- **Companions that follow**, beside the cats and dogs: **a familiar**, a raven or an owl, who rides on Elliott's shoulder or pack, flies ahead, perches near and can spot clues from the air, and is met as a story moment rather than as a stray; **a fox**, shy and clever, won over with more patience than a cat; and **a hedgehog**, who trundles behind and curls up in the basket on long walks.
- **Forest friends who stay home but know Elliott**: befriended, they come out to greet Elliott instead of fleeing. Red squirrels darting up trunks; mountain hares, brown in summer and white in winter; roe deer in the meadows at dawn and dusk; reindeer on the fells, sometimes with their herder; a moose wading in a lake; a badger at dusk; beavers with their lodge and dam on a stream; otters playing in a river; a lynx, rare and special; and a brown bear, seen rarely and from a distance, asleep through the winter.
- **Life that makes the forest alive**: birds (a woodpecker drumming, jays, waxwings in winter berry trees, a capercaillie in the pines, ptarmigan that turn white in winter, swans on the lakes, cranes calling overhead in spring and autumn, songbirds), and small things (butterflies and bumblebees in summer meadows, dragonflies over water, frogs and a witch's toad by the ponds, fish rising at dusk, fireflies at night).
- **The seasons move them**: coats turn white, the bear sleeps, the cranes come and go, the deer keep to dawn and dusk. The calendar changes the animals as it changes the land.
- **One animal underneath, two bodies**: the four-legged animal the cats and dogs share becomes the fox, the lynx, the deer, reindeer and moose (long legs, antlers), the hare, the badger, the hedgehog and the squirrel, each a variant with its own proportions, gait and coat. Birds get one winged body of their own, which covers the familiar, the crane, the ptarmigan and the songbirds. Insects and fish are simple effects.
- **First wave**: the fox and the hedgehog as companions, the familiar, squirrels, hares and roe deer as forest friends, and songbirds and butterflies for life; the rest follow.

## People and their stories

The land has people in it, met along the way: travellers on the road, a shepherd on the fell, a beekeeper among the hives, a fisher at a lake, a reindeer herder, a sauna keeper by the shore, an old cottager, a lost hiker. Each has **a story to tell**, and some are **in need of help**, and Elliott the Witch is glad to give it, with magic and with what they know and carry.

- **Help is gentle and kind:** mend a broken bridge with a charm, brew a salve for a sick goat, cook a warm meal for a cold traveller, light a beacon that has gone out, help a lost dog find its way home, find a dropped ring with a companion's nose, make an orchard bloom again.
- **Magic is Elliott's to use**: a growing set of gentle spells (mend, bloom, light, warm, calm, find) alongside the brews of the hearth. New spells, recipes and charms come from the people helped, as gifts and as things they teach.
- **Talking is warm and short.** A conversation is a few lines, the choices are of tone, never of failure, and a person remembers Elliott and what was done for them, so meeting them again is meeting a friend.
- **Rewards are friendship, not currency:** a story, a recipe, a spell, a seedling, a place to stay, a cat or dog who needs a home. There is nothing to grind.
- **Who writes them:** Claude writes the people and their stories as data, a growing library, the way it designs other games' content; where a person stands and how they look come from the world's generation, so the land keeps meeting Elliott with someone new. (Writing conversation live with a language model while playing is possible but needs a server and costs per conversation; it is not planned.)
- **How they look:** storybook figures, rounded and simple, with hats, hoods, scarves and aprons that say who they are, and faces of a few simple shapes (dot eyes, a little blush) that read well even face to face in first person, the way simple faces work in A Short Hike. In conversation the camera eases to frame the person and the words appear in a storybook panel.

## Mysteries

Some of the stories people tell are **mysteries**, and Elliott the Witch solves them the way Holmes would: by noticing, gathering and reasoning, with a witch's tools besides. They are cozy mysteries: nobody is hurt and nothing is grim. Who has been taking the beekeeper's honey? Why does the old mill's bell ring on windless nights? Where did the ferryman go, and why is his boat on the wrong shore? What are the lights moving on the fell?

- **Clues are found in the world**, not handed over: tracks in mud or snow, a tuft of fur on a fence, a dropped button, a door left open, a smell, what the weather and the season say, and what people tell, which is not always the whole truth.
- **Companions help:** a dog follows a scent trail, a cat slips into the places Elliott can't and comes back with something, and either may notice what Elliott walked past.
- **Witchcraft helps without solving it:** a scrying bowl shows a glimpse of the past at a place, a pendulum leans towards what is hidden, a tarot draw offers a hint to think about. Magic opens a way to look; the reasoning stays Elliott's.
- **The casebook** is where the deducing happens, in the spirit of Holmes: every clue goes into it, and Elliott connects clues into deductions ("the tracks are a badger's" plus "the honey jars were opened, not broken" says no badger did it). A deduction is checked when it is made, so the player always knows when they have it right; a wrong guess just isn't accepted, never punished.
- **The reveal** is a small scene with the people involved, where Elliott lays out how they worked it out, and it ends kindly: the honey thief was a lonely child feeding a fox cub, the bell was a jackdaw's nest. Solving a mystery is a friendship made, and often a home found for someone, animal or person.
- **Written like the stories**: Claude writes each mystery as data (its truth, its clues and where they lie, the deductions that join them), placed in the world by its generation, and every mystery is checked for being solvable from its clues alone. A nod to Holmes here and there is welcome: a deerstalker to find, a fiddle by a cottage fire, a dog called Watson.

## Foraging and cooking

Ingredients are found, not bought: herbs, mushrooms, berries, roots, nuts, wild greens, river fish, eggs from a farm a valley might hold. What grows changes with the season.

**The forests have berries and mushrooms to pick**, the ones a Nordic forest really has, each where it really grows:

- **Berries:** bilberries in the shade of pine and spruce, lingonberries on dry heath and under pines, wild strawberries at sunny edges and clearings, raspberries in clearings and along paths, and cloudberries out on the bogs, rarer and prized.
- **Mushrooms:** chanterelles under birch and spruce, porcini in the mixed woods, funnel chanterelles in damp spruce woods late in the year, and the fly agaric under birches, red with white spots, which is never eaten but is the witch's: a brewing ingredient, never a cooking one.
- **By season:** nothing in winter, the first wild strawberries in early summer, the berries through high summer, and the mushrooms from late summer through autumn, with the funnel chanterelles last.
- **Picking:** look at a ripe bush or a mushroom, and a prompt offers to pick it (a key, a controller button, or a tap). It goes into the witch's basket, and the bush shows it has been picked (its berries gone, the mushroom cut). A picked spot grows back after some days, in season.
- **Placement** is part of the world's generation, by region, ground and season, so a spot always grows the same thing; what has been picked is the only thing the game has to remember. Patches cluster as they do in a real forest: a good bilberry slope, a chanterelle spot worth remembering.

Foraging, as built:

- **Nine kinds**: bilberry, lingonberry, wild strawberry, raspberry and cloudberry; chanterelle, porcini, funnel chanterelle and fly agaric (the witch's, for brewing; the basket names it for what it is).
- **Placement** (the engine, pure and unit-tested): each plant chunk tries a dozen spots for a patch from its own seed. At each it reads the ground: how thick the woods are, the trees within ten metres (spruce and fir, pine, birch and aspen, the broadleaves), how wet it is, whether it is bog (wet, open, low ground just above the water) and whether it is above the treeline. Each kind has its own liking for that ground: bilberries the shade of conifers, lingonberries pine woods and dry open heath, strawberries and raspberries the sunny edges of woods and glades, cloudberries the open bog, chanterelles birch and spruce, porcini mixed woods, funnel chanterelles damp spruce, fly agarics birch. Its region makes it more or less plentiful (each region's `forage` in `regions.ts`: the birch vale's chanterelles and cloudberries, the highlands' bilberries, the heath's lingonberries, the maple wood's porcini), and a slow field of its own makes some country richer in it than the rest, so a good slope or a chanterelle spot is worth remembering. The kind the ground suits best grows a patch, thicker towards its middle, clear of trunks, rocks and bushes and off water and steep ground. The plants no longer scatter mushrooms of their own, so every mushroom in the forest can be picked.
- **Seasons** follow the real year, by the real day of the year the calendar's day stands for: strawberries from late June, cloudberries in July, bilberries and raspberries mid-July to early September, lingonberries mid-August to mid-October, chanterelles July to early October, porcini August to early October, fly agarics late August to mid-October, funnel chanterelles September to November; nothing in winter. Each patch's season comes a few days early or late, so a season begins and ends patch by patch. Out of season a berry bush is bare and a mushroom isn't up.
- **Picking** (gameplay, pure and unit-tested): in first person, what is offered is the ripe spot nearest the middle of the view within three metres of the eyes; in third person, the nearest ripe spot in front of Elliott within a couple of metres. A prompt below the middle of the view offers it (F, the controller's X; on touch the prompt is a button, and tapping the thing itself picks it too). Picking puts one of its kind in the basket, strips the bush of its berries or cuts the mushroom to its stumps, and pops it up into the basket with a few sparkles. A picked spot grows back two game days later (about a month of the real year), if it is in season then.
- **The basket** is a row of icons with counts at the top of the screen, no words: it shows for a few seconds after each pick, the kind just picked swelling, and stays while held open (Tab, the controller's Y, or the basket button at the top left on touch).
- **What is kept**: the basket, and each picked spot's id (its chunk and its place in the chunk's list) with the game day it was picked, forgotten once it has grown back. Until saving is built, it is kept on the device under a versioned key of its own; a spot's id holds only as long as the placement does, so a change to placement starts a new key.
- **Art**: each kind is soft, simple forms with their colours in their vertices, from the forage tokens, the small kinds drawn larger than life so they read from a few steps off: blue berries on low clumps of bright leaves, red lingonberry clusters on dark glossy mats, little red strawberries under three-lobed leaves, arching raspberry canes, amber cloudberries on stalks above round leaves, golden wavy funnels, fat brown-capped porcini, dark funnel chanterelles on yellow stems, and the red, white-spotted fly agaric with its ring. A berry bush is two parts, its plant and its fruit; mushrooms are drawn grown or as the stumps they leave. All are instanced, drawn within about a hundred metres.

Cooking happens at the campfire in pans, a pot and on sticks over the flames, and is tactile and short: choose ingredients, cook them, see the dish. Recipes are discovered by trying combinations, and remembered in the player's recipe book once found. Cooking can't fail: a combination with no recipe makes a humble dish of its own, which a cat may still love.

## Witchcraft

- **Brewing** at the hearth: teas, salves and charms from foraged things. A charm does something gentle and visible (makes flowers bloom round camp, calls fireflies, draws a shy cat closer, clears the fog from a view).
- **The moon** goes through its phases night by night; some herbs are gathered only under a full moon.
- **The Wheel of the Year** sits on the calendar as its festivals (see Seasons and the calendar).
- **Rituals** at the festivals and at places of power (a stone circle, a sacred spring): small, quiet set pieces such as lighting a Beltane fire or leaving an offering at Mabon.

## Seasons and the calendar

**Four Nordic seasons**: spring, summer, autumn and winter, and the world changes with them: the trees leaf out, turn and go bare (conifers stay green), the ground goes from snowmelt and new green through summer meadow and autumn gold to snow, the water freezes at the edges in winter, and the light and the colour grade follow. Weather is seeded and gentle: mist, soft rain, snow, golden evenings.

**The sun and moon move as they really do at a latitude**, set once in the game's tuning and easy to change. It starts at **Helsinki's (about 60°N)**: around midsummer the sun is up for about nineteen hours and the short night never gets fully dark; around midwinter it is up for about six, low in the south. Day length, the sun's height and where it rises and sets all come from the latitude and the day of the year, not from hand-set seasons.

**The calendar is the game's own, faster than real time**: a day still passes in minutes of play, a season in a number of days, and the year turns round in an evening or two of play, so a player sees every season. The day's length in minutes, the days in a season and the moon's cycle in days are one tuning each.

**The Wheel of the Year** sits on that calendar as its eight festivals (Samhain, Yule, Imbolc, Ostara, Beltane, Litha, Lughnasadh and Mabon): the solstices, the equinoxes and the four days between, each a small occasion with what can be gathered, brewed and done on it.

As built (the engine's calendar, astronomy and seasons, pure and unit-tested, drawn by the light and the seasons' features):

- **The tunings, in one place** (`CALENDAR`): twelve minutes a day, seven days a season (a year of 28 days, about five and a half hours of play), a lunar cycle of 8.4 days (not a divisor of the year, so the moon's phase on a festival differs from year to year, repeating every three years), Helsinki's latitude, and the start: the seventh day of spring, a late-May morning at nine, two days before midsummer, under a waxing gibbous moon.
- **Each game day stands for about thirteen days of the real year**, spread evenly: the seasons are the Nordic ones by the month (spring from 1 March, summer from June, autumn from September, winter from December), and every date carries the real day of the year it stands for, moving on smoothly through the hours, so anything seasonal keys on real dates. A date gives the day count, year, season, day of the season, progress through the season and the year, the hour, the real day of the year and the moon's age.
- **The festivals fall on the game days that hold their real dates**: the solstices and equinoxes on each season's second day (Ostara, Litha, Mabon, Yule) and the cross-quarter days on its fifth (Beltane, Lughnasadh, Samhain, Imbolc). A day knows its festival, and the next one and how many days off it is.
- **The sun** is worked out from the real day of the year by simplified real formulas (its eccentric orbit along the ecliptic, the declination it gives, local solar time, so it is highest at noon): its direction, elevation and azimuth, and the day's sunrise, sunset, day length and the civil, nautical and astronomical twilights, within a few minutes of Helsinki's almanac. A midsummer night only reaches nautical twilight; real dark comes back in August.
- **The moon** is a body offset east of the sun along the ecliptic by its age through the cycle, inclined a few degrees, so it rises and sets as its phase says (a full moon opposite the sun, rising at sunset and riding low on a summer night and high on a winter one; a new moon beside the sun) and the angle between them is its phase: lit by the sun's real direction, a sphere shows the right phase by itself. It gives its age, elongation, phase angle, the fraction lit, waxing or waning, and the phase's name.
- **The light follows the sun's real height**: the six moments are stops along the sun's elevation, night, dawn and morning as it climbs and golden hour, dusk and night as it sinks, turning from the one set to the other over the hours round noon and midnight. So a midsummer evening is golden at eight and in twilight at half past ten, and a midwinter noon, with the sun six degrees up in the south, is low and warm, between morning and golden hour. The key light is the sun by day and the moon by night, the moon's light by its phase.
- **Dates in the URL and the probe:** `?date=` starts on a date, written as a season and its day (`summer-2`), a festival (`litha`) or a day number, with `?hour=` holding the hour on it; the probe moves the date and reads the date, the sky and today's sun. The art gallery is lit on the autumn equinox, so its day has every moment at its usual hour and a dark night, and so are the world's screenshots, but for their `calendar-` shots.
- **The ~ panel's Calendar section** shows the date (season, day and year, with the season's colour), the real date it stands for, today's festival or the next and its days off, sunrise, sunset and day length, the twilight now, and the moon's phase drawn as it shows with its fraction lit; it moves the date by a slider over the year or a day either way, jumps to any festival, and holds the date while the hours turn (past midnight the same day comes round again). The Quality section beside it shows the quality profile's knobs, read-only: changing one means rebuilding what it shaped, so it is changed in `quality.ts`.
- **The land's year** (`engine/seasons.ts`) is plain numbers on the real day of the year, so it moves on smoothly through the hours and never jumps at midnight. Each season group of plants (birch, aspen, oak, maple, conifer, pine, heather, meadow flowers, fern, bracken, bush, and stone) keeps a phenology of a Nordic year at Helsinki's latitude, a little early in spring so the game's spring reads as one: how much leaf is out, how young and light its green is, how far it has turned (autumn colour; for conifers, winter's darker needles; for heather, its winter rust) and how much it is in flower. Birches, aspens and maples leaf out from late April, are fresh into June, turn from mid-September to their peak round the start of October and are bare by late October; oaks leaf and turn later and keep a few withered leaves all winter; meadow flowers bloom from late April through August and are gone by winter; heather blooms through August; ferns, bracken and bushes brown in autumn. Each plant keeps its own dates a few days either side of its group's, and picks one of its kind's two autumn colours, from a hash of where it stands, so no two trees in a wood turn alike. The ground keeps its own year: the snow line (below the whole land from December to early March, melting up the mountains through spring, above the high snowfields all summer, which stay regardless, and coming down them from October), last year's dormant straw after the melt and again in November, the new green of May, the autumn gold of the grass, the leaves fallen under the broadleaves, and how far the water freezes in from its edges. `seasonWeights` gives how much of each season a day is, handing over across the first of each season's month, for a seasonal grade.

## The night sky

- **The moon is a real moon**: a lit sphere, so its phase is the sun lighting it from where the sun is, never a drawn crescent. It goes through its phases over its cycle, rises and sets at the times its phase gives it (a full moon rises at sunset, a new moon is up with the sun), lights the night by its phase, and shows a faint earthshine on a thin crescent. Its phase matters to witchcraft (see Witchcraft).
- **Northern lights** come on clear nights, mostly in the long dark of autumn and winter, curtains of green with violet edges that drift and ripple, some nights faint and some nights a show, seeded so no two nights are alike.
- **Stars** turn with the night, and form constellations the player can find and name by the fire.

## Controls

The game plays on all three input methods (a controller, touch, and keyboard + mouse), switching to whichever was used last, through the shared input and gamepad libraries and the shared button prompts (see the root `CLAUDE.md`). It plays in **first person** by default, through the witch's own eyes, with **third person** (the camera following behind them) a switch away; nearby things to pick up, pet or use are offered with a prompt.

Walking, as built: the move goes the way the camera looks. In first person the camera is the witch's eyes at their eye height, they face where they look and are not drawn, their head bobs gently with their steps (more at a run, fading as they stop), and the view is wider (about 72° against third person's 55°). In third person they turn to face where they walk.

- **Keyboard and mouse:** WASD or the arrows walk, Shift runs, Q and E turn. In first person a click on the game locks the pointer and the mouse looks (round freely, up and down to just short of straight); Esc, the browser's own, frees it, and a prompt to click shows while it is free. Opening the ~ debug panel frees it too, and only a click locks it again. In third person dragging the mouse turns the camera and the wheel moves it in and out.
- **Touch:** a stick appears under the thumb wherever it lands on the left half of the screen, walking at the push and running at its rim; dragging on the right half looks round in first person or turns the camera in third, where pinching moves it in and out. There is no pointer lock on touch.
- **Controller:** the left stick walks at its push, the right stick looks or turns the camera, and the right trigger runs.
- **Jumping and the broom**, as built: jump (Space, the controller's A, a round button at the bottom right on touch) is a small, soft hop, about 0.6 m high and 0.6 s long, under a gravity a little stronger than the earth's; it keeps the way they were walking or running and can be steered a little in the air. There is no jumping again in the air: a second press takes out the broom and **glides**, a gentle descent at about 2.2 m/s while flying on at about 10 m/s (a glide ratio of about 4.5, so a ridge 200 m above a valley sails some 900 m out into it), turning smoothly towards where the player looks in first person or pushes the stick or keys in third. The broom never climbs, so mountains stay something to hike; gliding down off a ridge into the valley below is the reward for the climb. Touching ground they can stand on lands them softly; pressing jump on the broom puts it away and they fall, which never hurts, and taking it out again catches the fall, easing to the broom's sink: the way down faster. The broom never lets them come down where they couldn't stand: over deep water, or against ground too steep to hold them, it comes out on its own (if it wasn't already) and skims the surface, still steered, sliding down too-steep ground and never up it, until there is ground to land on. The motion is one pure step in gameplay, the same at any frame rate. Through their eyes, the hop is a small arc of the camera and a landing a soft dip; on the broom they sit a little to the left of its handle, whose tip shows low in the view, and the view widens a few degrees in the wind, rocks gently and leans into turns. In third person they ride astride, leaning forward, cloak and scarf streaming. The prompts add jump to each input's controls, and while in the air show what another jump does (the broom out, then put away) until it has been done once; the touch button's own symbol shows the same (a hop, the broom, put away). Real flight may come later as magic that is earned.
- **Using what is offered** (picking a berry, petting an animal): F, the controller's X, or on touch a tap on the thing or on its prompt; **a treat** for the animal offered: R, the controller's B, or its own button on touch; **the basket** opens and closes with Tab, the controller's Y, or its button at the top left on touch (see Foraging and cooking); **the companions' row** with C, the controller's left bumper, or a paw button beside the basket's on touch. While the naming card is up it has the keys: type and Enter on a keyboard, the d-pad and X on a controller.
- **Switching the view:** V, the controller's View button, or a small button at the top left on touch. The choice is kept on the device; without one, first person.
- The third-person camera orbits the witch, within limits of tilt and distance, and comes in along its line where the land rises behind them rather than climbing over it. Both views share the way the player faces, so a switch keeps it.
- How to walk is shown, for the input used last and the view, as its controls beside a symbol of what each does, until that input has walked; on touch, as a ghost stick on the left and a looking or turning symbol on the right. Before anything is used, a device whose main pointer is a finger is taken to be played by touch.

## Look

Hearthwood has its own style: a warm, soft storybook diorama, with golden light and the fire as the brightest thing at night. Its colours are its own tokens, scoped to the project.

**Claude makes all of the art, so the style is chosen for what art written as code does well.** Every model, material, animation and effect is code: no image textures, no models from outside, no hand-keyed animation.

What carries the look, because code does it well:

- **Light and atmosphere** are where the mood comes from: the sun's colour through the day, fog in the valleys, rim light on the forest edge, the fire's flicker on everything near it, and a colour grade per season.
- **Simple, readable forms**: soft low-poly shapes, smooth rounded solids and silhouettes that read from a distance, built from primitives, smooth signed-distance forms and lofts, the way Betrayal's figures are sculpted.
- **Procedural materials**: bark, moss, stone, water and fur patterns are shader functions of position and noise, not painted pictures.
- **Many of a few things**: a forest is a handful of tree designs, each varied by seed (height, lean, branching, colour) and drawn instanced.
- **Particles**: embers, smoke, steam from the pot, fireflies, falling leaves, snow, rain.
- **Procedural motion**: idles, walks, a tail's sway, breathing and ear flicks as functions of time and speed, not keyframes.

What the style stays away from, because code does it badly: realistic or detailed faces, photoreal materials, hand-painted detail, intricate one-off sculpts, and expressive acting. Characters are seen mostly at a distance or from behind; faces are a few simple shapes (eyes, a nose, ears) that read through pose and motion rather than expression.

**Cats and dogs** are the hardest art in the game and its heart, so they get the most care: a stylised cat of a few smooth forms, recognisably a cat by its silhouette, ears and tail and by how it moves (the stretch, the loaf, the tail up in greeting); a dog of the same smooth forms, recognisably a dog by its muzzle, ears and tail and by how it moves (the trot, the wag, the tilted head, the tongue out). Its coat is a shader driven by its genetics (see Cats): a tabby's stripes and a calico's patches are pattern functions, which is exactly what code does well. The witch is a silhouette first: a cloak, a hat, a pack.

Art is reviewed from headless screenshots taken through the repo's own tooling and revised until it is done, the loop Betrayal's art skills follow; once Hearthwood's art has a working method, it becomes a skill of its own.

The world's light, as built, is simple and its own (nothing is shared with Shipwright's physically based light):

- **The day** lasts twelve minutes and blends between six moments, night, dawn, morning, noon, golden hour and dusk, each with its own sky, haze, key light, light from the sky and the ground, and shadow tint, all tokens, chosen by the sun's real height and whether it is climbing or sinking (see Seasons and the calendar). The sun lights the day and the moon the night by its phase, each fading near the horizon so the handover happens in the dark.
- **Every material ends in the world's air:** a haze thickest low down and thinning up the mountains, thicker in the pine highlands' mist and clearer over the heath, warmed towards the sun, then a gentle grade that lifts saturation and tints the shadows towards the moment's own colour. The haze is the horizon's colour, so the land melts into the sky. Tone mapping keeps hues true (Khronos neutral). Anything beyond that (a vignette, and the water, sky, shadows and weather to come) is a feature that can be switched off (see Features).
- **Shapes:** trees are soft primitives with their colours in their vertices, crowns shading as one mass, swaying in a breeze; rocks and stones are lumpy flat-shaded solids; huts are logs under turf. The water lights itself: shallows to deep by the bed's depth, and a river's streaks run the way it flows and whiten over falls and down cascades; everything more (ripples, its colour from depth, reflections, refraction, caustics, ice) is a feature (see Water, under Features).
- **The ground is painted per pixel, not per vertex:** each vertex carries its region's own grounds and the smooth fields the cover is read from (the woods, the water, the river, the bands' wobble), and each ground (the forest floor, alpine grass, rock in weathered bands, snow, the wet shore, the trodden earth round the fire) begins where its field crosses a threshold, softened and set wavering by a little noise, so every edge reads as a soft line at any angle and never follows the mesh's triangles. A fine grain of noise lies over it all. Anything narrow drawn on the land later (a path, a game trail) is drawn the same way, or as a ribbon with a soft edge, never as paint on the vertices.
- **The fire** is soft flame shells, rising embers, a glow and a flickering light, brightest at night, when fireflies come out over the meadows and the stars overhead.
- **Pixel ratio and shadows** come from the quality profile (see Target device): the device's own pixel ratio up to 2, the sun's shadow maps 2048 texels a side, reaching 900 m (60 m in one map without the far shadows), and the sky drawn last, only where nothing covers it.
- The day can be held at an hour with `?hour=<0-24>`, started on a date with `?date=` (`summer-2`, `litha`), or set and held from the debug panel that ~ opens; another world opens with `?seed=<text>`, and `?haze=<n>` scales the haze.

**The art gallery** (`?art`) shows every piece of the art on a plain floor, in rows by kind: each region's plant designs with their variants, rocks, every berry and mushroom to pick, ripe and picked, every kind of place, the camp and its burning fire, fireflies, Elliott (on foot and on the broom), the broom and a few cats. It is lit by the game's own day and air, with the same ~ panel, and can be orbited and zoomed by mouse, touch or pad, with any piece picked (or stepped to with the pad's bumpers) to see it alone on a turntable. It is built from the art layer alone, with no world generated and no rules, which is the proof that the art stands on its own; a new piece appears there by being added to its one list, and a new kind of plant or place appears without even that.

## Features

Every part of the look beyond the plain world is a **feature**: one switch, on unless switched off, and **a feature that is off does no work at all**. Its GLSL is compiled out of every shader by its define, its passes are not run and their render targets not allocated, its per-frame update doesn't run. Nothing is gated by a branch on a uniform or a multiply by zero. Quality (`quality.ts`) is how much of a thing is drawn; features are whether it is drawn at all.

- **The switches** are kept per browser and flipped in the ~ panel's Features section, by group (water, sky and light, shadows and foliage, atmosphere and weather, seasons), each with what it costs the GPU beside it. `?features=` sets them for one visit without keeping them: `-id` off, `+id` on, `none` and `all`, in order, so `?features=none,+sway` shows one feature alone.
- **Adding a feature** takes two entries, both in the group's own files, so no two groups ever edit the same list: its switch in `features/<group>.ts` (a camelCase id, a name, a description), and its art in `art/features/<group>.ts` (any of: GLSL chunks, the uniforms they read, a per-frame update, passes). A feature whose drawing is objects of its own (rain, falling leaves) mounts them only while `useFeature` says it is on.
- **A chunk** is GLSL for the shared materials of some kinds (the land, the gallery's floor, foliage, solid things, water, the sky) at a few named hook points: declarations for each stage, moving the vertex, the surface's colour before light, the normal in world space, the light the surface gives back (from the sun and moon, already shadowed, and from the sky and ground), and the finished colour after the air. The sky takes only declarations and its colour. Each chunk is compiled in under its feature's define, in a block of its own; a hook's setup and write-back exist only while some feature there is on. Switching a feature recompiles the materials it reaches and no others. The hook points and what each can read are listed in `art/feature-art.ts`; every shared material is made through `art/shading.ts`, never patched by hand.
- **A pass** runs at one of three stages, in a declared order: `before` the scene (offscreen renders such as a cloud-shadow map or a reflection), `opaque` (after everything but the late layer is drawn, with the scene's colour and depth to read, for refraction; water belongs on the late layer once anything needs that), and `post` (over the finished frame, the last one drawing to the screen). It is made when its feature is switched on and disposed when it is switched off. With no pass on, the scene draws straight to the screen; with one on, it draws into a target of its own exactly as the screen would be drawn (tone mapped and encoded by the materials, blended as the screen blends), so passes work on the frame as it shows and nothing changes look when the first pass comes on. A linear high-range frame for physically based effects is not built: the storybook look is made in what the screen shows.
- **Depth** is reversed (1 near, 0 far and the sky) and a float, precise to the horizon. Anything that reads depth goes through `art/depth.ts`'s GLSL (linear distance, nothing drawn, the view-space point under a pixel), held by its tests to three's own reversed projection, never its own arithmetic.
- **Costs**: each pass and the scene's draw are timed on the GPU while the Features section is open (and not at all otherwise). A feature that is only chunks costs inside the scene's draw, so its cost is the scene's saving when it is switched off. `tools/feature-shots.mjs` shoots every feature on and off through the day and prints each part's time. The first pass on also costs the scene's own target and its resolve, about half a millisecond on a desktop at 1600×900.
- **Shadows** are cast through `shadowDepth` (`shading.ts`): three's depth material with a kind's vertex hooks (and a shared material's own vertex GLSL, through `shadowDepthOf`) compiled in under `HW_DEPTH`, so whatever a feature moves or shrinks casts its shadow where it is drawn (a bare tree casts its branches, not its summer crown; a swaying tree's shadow sways). The shadow maps draw every caster with it (see Shadows and foliage). A chunk's vertex code reads colour and normals only outside `HW_DEPTH`.
- **The features so far:** the shadows and foliage (below), a soft vignette over the frame (sky and light), and the seasons' two:
  - **Seasonal plants** (`seasonalFoliage`, foliage and solid materials, vertex work only). Plant shapes built while it is on carry what each piece is to the seasons (a crown's mass of leaves, a far tree's one mass, a branch, bark, a flower's head, a small plant's leaves, stone) and its season group, in their vertices; broadleaf trees are built with a bare tree's branches and twigs, hidden inside their crowns in leaf. Built while it is off, a shape is its summer self, with nothing extra to draw, so switching it rebuilds the designs. The groups' years are baked into a small table, a texture a year wide and a row a group, which each vertex reads at the date moved by its plant's own timing: crowns grow in and fall a mass at a time (which masses go first is the tree's own, the last ones smaller and withering), far trees thin to a haze of bare twigs, small plants grow and die back, flowers open and go, the colour goes from the young green through the summer's to the tree's autumn colour, conifers darken, and snow lies where the snow line says on whatever faces up: a dusting on the needles, a line along the twigs, caps on stones and low plants. The maple wood's maples, the aspens and the bracken are painted in their autumn colours, so their summer green is the season's own.
  - **Seasonal ground** (`seasonalGround`, the land, per pixel): over the land's own ground, read from its locals in `terrain.ts`, the dormant straw, the new green, the autumn grass in swathes of gold and russet with green left in the hollows, drifts of fallen leaves under the broadleaves in their region's autumn colour, and snow lying down to the season's line in soft melting patches, clinging to steeper ground the higher it lies, thin under the conifers and kept off the lake beds; the far woods' painted canopy turns, goes bare and takes the snow. Each tile carries its regions' autumn colour and share of broadleaves for it.
  - **What other groups read:** water's ice at its edges and a seasonal grade read the same year from the engine (`groundSeason`'s `ice` and `snowLine`, `seasonWeights`) on the date every feature's frame has (`DayState.date`, the real day of the year).

**Water**, as built: plain on its own (clear shallows to deep blue by the bed's depth, a faint sky, a river's streaks running the way it flows and white over falls), and everything beyond that a feature of the water group, each its own file in `art/water/`, built up in a fixed order on the water's own shader:

- **Ripples**: five waves of a sharp-crested Gerstner-like shape, each about 0.6 times the length of the last and quicker by deep water's dispersion, each turned by the golden angle so they never line up, their phase warped by slow noise and their choppiness swelling and calming over tens of metres. A river's turn downstream and run faster. A wave finer than the pixel fades out, and the ripples ease flat at a grazing look, so the surface never shimmers at any distance; the normal comes from the height by finite difference. **Ripple parallax** steps a few times down through their height along the view.
- **Colour from depth**: water is nearly clear, absorbing red fastest and blue slowest, so its colour comes from how far light goes through it, down to the bed and back up along the bent view, with the water's own colour (what it scatters back of what it doesn't absorb) where the bed is lost. Each region's water has its own absorption, blended across borders: clear and a little green in the greenwood, the lakeland's tea-brown tannin, the highlands' turquoise, the heath's peat, the maple wood's olive. The bed's depth is the engine's, never read from the depth buffer.
- **Shore highlight**: a soft bright edge and a breathing lace of foam where the water is very shallow, only the very edge of a river.
- **Sky reflection**: the sky along the reflected ray, from the sky's own live uniforms, as much as water's Fresnel gives (little looking down, nearly all at a glance), and the key light's glint off the ripples: the sun's by day, the moon's by night.
- **Refraction**: the real bed through the water, from a copy of the scene drawn before it (water is drawn on the late layer, after the passes that read the scene), moved by the ripples further the deeper it is; a moved pixel that lies in front of the water is never pulled in.
- **Screen-space reflections**: the reflected ray marched across a half-size copy of the scene against its distances, in growing steps refined by halving; a ray that hits nothing mirrors whatever is far off along it on screen (the sky with everything in it, the far ranges), and eases back to the sky's reflection where it leaves the screen.
- **Caustics**: a drifting web of light on the bed of shallow water, from the key light only, strongest a hand or two down and fading with depth and with distance; it lights the land itself, so refraction shows it.
- **Underwater**: with the eye under the surface (the scene tells the water its level at the camera from the engine), everything fades into the water's own colour by how far light goes through water, darker the deeper the eye; looking up, **Snell's window** squeezes the whole sky into a bright circle overhead, with the water mirrored outside it.
- **Far water**: lakes in the coarse tiles past where water is drawn in full (a simple shader: colour by depth, the sky by Fresnel, flat), so water reads to the horizon; switching it rebuilds those tiles.
- **Winter ice**: grows out from lake shores into deeper water as winter comes on and melts back in spring, by the seasons' own ice (`engine/seasons.ts`), so the water freezes as the land's snow lies; running water freezes only at its edges and falls never. Pale and matt, dusted with snow, finely cracked, and flat.

The scene copies hold the frame as the screen shows it (see the passes, above), taken back to linear light for the water by undoing the encoding and the exposure; the tone mapping is all but straight below the highlights, so it is left. `tools/water-shots.mjs` shoots a lake near camp from its shore, its shallows, under it, a river, the lake at Yule, far lakes from a height and the glint of the low sun and the moon, every feature on and the water's off, and times each water feature from the shore.

**Shadows and foliage**, as built (`art/shadows/`, `art/foliage/`, one file per effect, gathered by `art/features/shadows-and-foliage.ts`):

- **Shadows** (`sunShadows`): the key light's own shadow maps, drawn before the scene; three's own shadows are not used. Only the key light's share of a surface's light is taken away where a shadow falls (worked out as the same Lambert term three uses), so the campfire still lights what lies in the sun's or the moon's shadow, and a shadow keeps the sky's and the ground's light, the moment's own colour. Land, the gallery's floor, plants, solid things and the water take shadows; the campfire's flames, the fireflies and the sky don't. Every mesh that casts is drawn into the maps with its shadow depth (`shadowDepth`, or `shadowDepthOf` for a material with vertex GLSL of its own), which moves its vertices exactly as the material does (the land's geomorph and the earth's curve, worked out from the real camera; the wind and the parting), so a swaying tree's shadow sways. A receiver is lifted off its surface by about a texel along its normal, more where the light grazes it, which keeps surfaces from shadowing themselves without floating the shadows off their casters. Shadows fade over the light's last few degrees above the horizon, before they could stretch across the land. The maps hold the light's depth twice, compared in hardware for the shadows and as a plain number for knowing how far the light has come, and any feature can read them (god rays): `SHADOW_UNIFORMS` and `SHADOW_GLSL` in `shadow-map.ts`, each map's matrix taking the world to its square with the depth reversed as the world's is.
- **Far shadows** (`shadowCascades`): four maps in place of one, each a slice of the view several times longer than the one before (to about 10, 47, 206 and 900 m), each slice wrapped in the smallest sphere that holds it so its size never changes as the camera turns, and moved only in whole texels, so edges never crawl as Elliott walks or looks round. A point near a map's edge eases into the next, and the last fades out before it ends. The two far maps are drawn on alternate frames. Off, one map covers the first 60 m.
- **Soft shadows** (`softShadows`): a search round each point for how far off its casters are, then a filter as wide as the light's disc seen past them, so a shadow is crisp where it touches its caster and soft where a crown's shadow falls far below. The sun is drawn a little wider than the real one, for a storybook's soft edge. Off, every edge is softened a little and evenly.
- **Moon shadows** (`moonShadows`): at night the moon casts, softer and a little lighter than the sun. The engine hands the key light from the sun to the moon only when both have faded to nothing at the horizon, so the shadows change light in the dark, with no flip. Off, nights draw no shadow maps.
- **Overcast softening** (`overcastShadows`): shadows widen and pale with the cloud cover, to a soft darkening under things beneath a full overcast.
- **Far slopes** (`farShading`): past the shadows' reach (or everywhere with the shadows off), land turned from the light goes over into shade more firmly than the plain lighting gives, from its own normals and the light alone, so far mountains keep a lit side and a shaded side. With every shadow feature off, the world is lit by the plain directional term and this, with no shadow map sampled.
- **Light through leaves** (`leafLight`): leaves and fronds glow, deeper in their own colour, with the key light behind them, brightest looking straight towards it. How much gets through comes from the shadow map's depth, how much of the plant the light has crossed, so the thin rim of a crown and a fern shine and the heart of a crown stays dark; with the shadows off, a leaf turned from the light lets a steady share through.
- **Wind sway** (`sway`): every plant leans downwind and rocks in its own time, more the higher up it, its branches swaying round the trunk and its leaves fluttering; gusts roll downwind across the land as broad patches of stronger air. A berry bush is foliage too, so its fruit moves with its leaves.
- **Parting plants** (`plantParting`): flowers, ferns, heather, bushes and berry bushes (anything under about two metres) bend away from Elliott's feet, each part by where it is, so a bush they stand in opens round them, and spring back behind them along a short trail of their steps, swinging a little past upright before they settle. Elliott high on the broom parts nothing. Cats and dogs could part them the same way later.
- **The weather they answer to**: the cloud cover and the wind's strength and way come from the weather once it has an engine; until then from the ~ panel's Clouds and wind section, or `?clouds=` and `?wind=` (0 to 1) for one visit.
- **Costs**, desktop at 1600×900 with every feature on, at camp at golden hour: the shadow maps about 10 ms of GPU time with the far shadows on and 3 ms without; reading them about 1 to 1.5 ms of the scene's draw, the soft shadows half a millisecond more; the rest a fraction of a millisecond each. Most of the maps' cost is the plants: they are one instanced draw per design and detail for every chunk, so each map runs the vertex work of every plant drawn, culling the ones outside it only in the shader. Drawing plants per chunk, so a map culls whole chunks, is the performance pass's first step here.

## Saving

Companions are kept on the device as built, under a versioned key of their own until the game's saving is built: each companion (its stray's id and seed, its species, its name, and whether it follows or rests at camp) and how warm each stray Elliott has met is to them. Where they stand is never kept: on loading, those who follow are beside Elliott and the rest by the fire. A stray's id holds only as long as the strays' placement does, so a change to that starts a new key.

Single-player and saved on the device, with no account: the world's seed, where camp is pitched, where Elliott is, the companions and their names, the basket, recipes and spells, what has been picked, the people met and what was done for them, and the calendar. The world itself is never saved: any spot regenerates the same from the seed, so only what Elliott has changed is kept.

## First slice

The smallest version that is already the game:

- the endless world, with day and night;
- walking in first and third person on all three input methods;
- a handful of ingredients to forage and a few recipes to cook at the campfire;
- generated cats to meet, befriend with their favourite dish, and bring to camp;
- saving.

Long walks with camp pitched anywhere, brewing, festivals and people follow, one at a time.

## Open questions

- **Companions on screen:** three follow at once for now and the rest nap by the fire at camp; is three the cozy number, and does camp ever outgrow its clearing?
- **The familiar:** a raven or an owl first, and does Elliott choose?
- **Mountain scale:** the highest ranges reach about 900 m above the lowlands; the Rockies and Jotunheimen rise twice that and more.
- **Music:** generated, composed, or ambient sound only.
