# Hearthwood

A cozy 3D game about a wandering hedge-witch, the cats and dogs they befriend, and the people they help along the way, in an endless northern land of forest and mountain made fresh from a seed.

It lives at Games → 3D Games → Hearthwood. **For now it is only its environment**: the land, its water, its plants and grass, and its sky through the day, the year and the night, seen through a free camera. It is drawn with three.js on WebGPU, with no framework between the game and three. The world comes first, simple and fast; graphical features are added one at a time, each only once the owner judges it worth what it costs; props, buildings, people, animals and gameplay come back after that (see Later).

## Who it's for

Hearthwood is a gift from the owner to their partner, **Elliott**, who uses **they/them** pronouns: always write and speak of Elliott, and of the witch, as they. The witch is **Elliott the Witch**: the player plays as Elliott.

Everything in the game is chosen for what Elliott loves: cats and dogs, and befriending animals that then follow them; nature, forests and mountains; hiking; cooking and campfires; magic, witchy and pagan things; and cozy, relaxing, chill games. Elliott is very social: they like talking to people and helping them, so the land has people in it with stories to tell and help to ask for. And Elliott loves a good mystery, with a soft spot for Sherlock Holmes, so some of those stories are mysteries to solve.

Elliott plays on a Pixel 8 Pro (touch), on a laptop (keyboard and mouse) and on a laptop on the TV with an Xbox controller.

## Pillars

- **Nothing is ever lost.** No fail states, no combat, no hunger or health meter, no timer that punishes. A meal that comes out unexpected is a new dish, not a failure. An animal never runs away, falls ill or dies.
- **Cooking is for joy and for friendship, never for survival.**
- **Every walk finds something.** The world is generated so that wandering always turns up a herb, a view, a cat or a ruin, and never a long empty stretch.
- **Calm by default.** Soft sound, slow light, no notifications nagging for attention. The game waits as long as the player likes.
- **Witchcraft is gentle and earthy**: hedge-witchery, herbs, the moon and the Wheel of the Year, treated with warmth and respect, never as spooky set dressing.

## Target device and performance

Hearthwood's floor is a **Pixel 8 Pro** in Chrome, held upright or sideways; laptops and a laptop on a TV with a controller are above it.

**Performance comes from simplicity.** The baseline draws only what the world needs, lit and coloured as simply as reads well, and a graphical feature joins it only when the owner has seen it and judged it worth its cost. There are no feature switches: what is in the game is in it. Every knob that trades how the world looks for how fast it draws (how far the land reaches and how fine it is with distance, how far plants and grass are drawn and in which detail, the pixel ratio) lives in one quality profile, `quality.ts`. The page always shows a stats.js frame meter in the top right.

**WebGPU only.** A browser without WebGPU is told the game needs it.

## How it is built

- **The engine** (`engine/`) is the world and time: pure functions of the seed, a position and the calendar, knowing nothing of how anything looks.
- **The render** (`render/`) is three.js's WebGPU renderer driven directly: one renderer, one scene, one loop (`game.ts`). The page (`components/hearthwood.tsx`) only starts it, stops it and shows what it reports; nothing in the game renders through React.
- **Shaders are TSL**, three's node shaders, which it turns into WGSL. Every surface is lit by Hearthwood's own light and air (`air.ts`: the key light, the sky's and the ground's light, the sky's colour along a way, the haze), read from uniforms the frame sets once, so no material is rebuilt as the day turns; nothing uses three's lights. Each kind of thing is one material: one for all the land, one for all water, one for every plant, one for the grass. Where TSL's typings and its nodes part ways, `tsl.ts` mends it in one place. A shader body TSL makes hard to write may be hand-written WGSL inside TSL (`wgslFn`).
- **Rules WebGPU holds us to**, learnt the hard way:
  - A pipeline reads at most eight vertex buffers: attributes that would pass that share an interleaved buffer.
  - No branching over large expressions: cases are blended by weights of 0 and 1 rather than chosen with `select` or `If`, since three can turn a branch into blocks that leave a value unset, drawing black.
  - A smoothstep's low edge comes first: a reversed one is undefined on Windows, where WebGPU runs on Direct3D, and can draw black; the falling edge is `1 - smoothstep(low, high, x)`.
  - A mesh whose instanced numbers don't exist yet isn't added to the scene: its pipeline would be built without them.
  - A zero-sized canvas (a hidden tab or pane) is not drawn into.
- **The camera** (`camera/`) is the free camera: its maths pure, its input from the keyboard, the mouse (pointer locked) and a controller.

## The world

The world is one continuous landscape, endless in every direction, made from a seed with the site's `seeded-random` as pure functions in the project's engine. Any spot always comes out the same, however the world was walked to get there, so it is generated by area as the player goes, and every part of it is unit-testable. `?seed=<text>` opens another world.

**One landscape, never a world of bowls.** The land comes from a few large, slowly varying fields across the whole world: how high the ground broadly lies, how wet it is, how hilly, and where the mountains rise. Nothing has a hand-shaped rim; valleys are simply the land between ranges, in every shape, and a long walk always leads somewhere different.

**Mountains** run as long winding ranges along the lines where a broad field crosses zero, so they wind for kilometres, rise and fall along their crests (where a crest dips there is a pass) and part in wide gaps, leaving the lowlands between them open. A slow field of its own gives each range its character, drawn from the real mountains the game's owner loves, by way of all of Scandinavia:

- **High alpine ranges**, like Norway's Jotunheimen and the Lyngen Alps, the Tetons and the Alps: massifs some 10 km across rising 1,100 to about 2,700 m above the lowlands, most of their height above the trees, so a range 10 to 20 km off stands over a lowland meadow as the Tetons stand over Jackson Hole. They follow the zero lines of a broader, smoother field of their own, with their own wider gaps, and part in broad lowland valleys between massifs. Glacial valleys run down the flanks from the crest, flat-floored between walls that steepen as they rise; their floors stay low far into the range, then climb to cirques under the crest, where the valleys from either side meet at a col, the pass over. Side valleys come down the main valleys' walls and hang above their floors, so cascades fall from them. The spurs between the valleys rise to narrow rounded crests, the ridges a walker climbs, and up on the crest each col runs on to the peaks either side as an even arête; horns rise where the spurs meet the crest. No two mountains are cut alike. Along a crest the spurs rise to a massif's dominant summit every four kilometres or so and stand up to about a third lower between, while the valley floors and cols keep their heights, so the way up a valley to a pass is as easy beside a great peak as beside a lesser one. Each horn is its own, from slow fields about a valley's spacing across: anything from a knoll to a 400 m tower, a sharp Matterhorn-like horn or a broad dome, some flat-topped, some split into two summits, the lesser massifs carrying the taller ones. Each range has an ice side, wandering slowly across the world, where its faces come nearer the crest and steeper and the snow and ice reach further down, while its other side reaches further out and gentler, so a peak reads differently from each side. Spurs rise straight, steepening under the crest or easing to it, some with a bench partway up; the valleys wander from an even spacing, so the spurs between them are uneven and here and there two valleys merge; and over some massifs the tops level off into high fjell, a plateau standing a little higher, its valleys ending in cirques bitten into its edge and its horns along that edge. From far off, where the valleys are too fine to show, sharp horns broaden into domes and a range keeps its horns' bulk along its crest, so the far land never shimmers. Ribs and gullies flute the faces, running straight down them (stripes turned along the way the land falls, so they roughen a face without steepening the way up it), and crags stand off the high walls in patches, leaving ways up between them. Where a range thins out along its length it narrows as it lowers, so its ends are no steeper than its flanks. Up in the high valley heads the snow and ice reach down into the cirques as snowfields and small glaciers, and tarns lie in the cirques.
- **Fells**, as in Lapland: broad rounded tops rising some 400 m, bare above a low treeline.
- **Long rounded ridges side by side**, as in central Sweden and Norway or the Appalachians: folded country whose ridges, about 2.3 km apart with lesser ribs between, all run roughly one way across a world on a broad swell of upland, rising 400 to 700 m above the lowlands, layered into the distance and wooded to their tops.
- **Lakeland lowlands**, as in Finland: open country of birch and pine with many lakes.

Big mountains are common enough to stand on most horizons, and a walk can reach them; the walker climbs anything up to about 45° and wades anything shallower than a paddle, so cliffs and lakes turn them aside, passes and valleys lead through, and the world has no wall or edge. Every big massif has a way up from the lowlands, along the valley floors to the cols and up the spurs' crests, to a viewpoint at least four fifths of the way to its summit (the summits themselves may be horns, faces to look at rather than climb), and a slow test walks a grid over the highest massifs to hold it so.

**Bands.** The treeline sits low, as it does at this latitude, so the big peaks rise far above the forest. In alpine country, over lowlands at about 30 to 70 m, the woods reach about 780 m, alpine grass lies above them, scree and bare rock from about 1,150 m (and on any ground too steep to hold soil), and snow from about 1,650 m, clinging to steeper ground the higher it goes. The land shifts the bands where it is: about 480 m lower on the fells, so their tops stand bare above the trees as Lapland's do; about 260 m higher in folded country, wooded to its tops; up and down by some 150 m with a slow climate across the world; and down by up to about 420 m in an alpine range's high valley heads, where the snowfields and small glaciers lie. Each band's edge also wanders up and down the slopes by some 30 m. The seasons' snow line moves through the year against the bands too, so the land shifts it with them wherever it is, on the ground, on the plants and in what falls from the sky: the fells and the country round them hold their snow later into spring and take it earlier in autumn, folded country loses it sooner, and deep winter's line lies below the whole land wherever its bands sit.

**Water stands on terraces**, so it never needs to know the whole map and never runs uphill. The water table is the broad lie of the land rounded down to steps of a few metres, so every lake and every reach of river is level. Lakes lie wherever the land dips below its water: in the wet lowlands' hollows, and as tarns in cirques up in the ranges, each held in by a lip of moraine. Rivers follow the borders of a pattern of cells laid over the world in a warped frame, so they wind, meet at junctions and part, never close into rings, and start at springs where a border holds no river; each cuts its own channel and a broad valley whose sides rise gently from it, keeps to the lowlands and hills, and fades out as the land climbs high above it. Where a river crosses from one terrace down to the next it drops over a waterfall, and just below each terrace's edge the land rises in a gentle bank that holds the higher water in. Rivers are always shallow enough to wade.

**Regions with a character of their own**, each with its own slowly varying field, nudged by the land, and blending softly across a border a few hundred metres wide, so a long walk is a journey through country after country:

- **Greenwood**, home: mixed oak, fir and birch with open meadow, flowers and bushes. It holds the land round the world's origin.
- **Birch vale**, the lakeland: light birch woods with pine and golden aspen, white and yellow flowers, ferns, chanterelles, fairy rings.
- **Pine highlands**, the mountains: Scots pine and fir up to the treeline, rocks and heather, and a misty air of their own.
- **Heath**: open moorland of golden grass and heather (purple, pink, and gorse in flower), lone pines and birches, tors and boulders, and a clear sky.
- **Maple wood**: dense autumn woods of red, orange and gold maple with golden aspen, bracken, mushrooms and fallen giants.

**Life by area.** The plants and stones of each 64 m square are scattered from that square's own seed. The woods stand in groves and open into glades, cover as much of the land as their region says, thin out to the treeline and keep back from water and the places; each region has its own trees, high and low, and its own undergrowth in the open and under the trees. Read from far off, the groves and glades finer than the reading can show are left out, so the far woods are their average cover and never speckle.

**Places by area.** The world is cut into cells of about 190 m, and some hold one of the land's own sights, found from the cell's own seed and its land alone: a tor of weathered slabs on a rise, a tarn in a cirque's hollow held in by its lip of moraine, a cascade down a valley wall, or a waterfall wherever a river falls from one terrace to the next. Each region has its own mix, each kind is put where it fits best among a scatter of candidates, and a place keeps well inside its cell, so the clearing round it never reaches the next. Places people made (a stone circle, a cairn, a shrine, a seter) are for when the world is populated.

**What is drawn, and how far.** There is no practical limit to how far the player sees: on a clear day the land runs to the horizon, as with Minecraft's Distant Horizons. The far world is the real land at coarser and coarser detail. The land is a quadtree of square tiles, each the same number of vertices, each level twice the width of the one inside it; a tile splits into four finer ones as the camera comes within a fixed number of its widths, so a triangle covers about the same part of the screen at any distance and the cost grows only with the logarithm of how far the view reaches (about a hundred kilometres, past where the curve of the earth has hidden all but the highest peaks). Far tiles sample the world's fields at their own spacing, leaving out what is finer than that spacing can show, so distant land never shimmers. Tiles are worked out in workers off the main thread, kept a while once made, and the drawn set changes over all at once when every tile it needs is ready, so no hole ever opens; each tile eases into its parent's shape over the stretch before it is swapped for it, and hangs a skirt from its edges, so levels join with no seam or pop. The land drops away by the curve of the earth. Plants are drawn in full near by and in a simpler shape further out, every copy of a design in one draw; past where trees are drawn, the woods are painted into the land as their canopy. Grass blades grow in the open meadows within a few tens of metres. The places stream in within about a kilometre and a half. Depth uses a reversed depth buffer, precise from a hand's breadth to the horizon.

**The haze** is light and even, thinner up high so the peaks stand clearer than the valleys, and the horizon's colour, so the land melts into the sky: the world holds up on a perfectly clear day on its own. Weather (mist, rain, snow, clouds) is for later.

## Seasons and the calendar

**Four Nordic seasons**: spring, summer, autumn and winter, and the world changes with them: the trees leaf out, turn and go bare (conifers stay green), the ground goes from snowmelt and new green through summer meadow and autumn gold to snow, the water freezes at the edges in winter, and the light and the colour grade follow.

**The sun and moon move as they really do at a latitude**, set once in the game's tuning and easy to change. It starts at **Helsinki's (about 60°N)**: around midsummer the sun is up for about nineteen hours and the short night never gets fully dark; around midwinter it is up for about six, low in the south. Day length, the sun's height and where it rises and sets all come from the latitude and the day of the year, not from hand-set seasons.

**The calendar is the game's own, faster than real time**: a day still passes in minutes of play, a season in a number of days, and the year turns round in an evening or two of play, so a player sees every season. The day's length in minutes, the days in a season and the moon's cycle in days are one tuning each.

**The Wheel of the Year** sits on that calendar as its eight festivals (Samhain, Yule, Imbolc, Ostara, Beltane, Litha, Lughnasadh and Mabon): the solstices, the equinoxes and the four days between, each a small occasion with what can be gathered, brewed and done on it.

As built (the engine's calendar, astronomy and seasons, pure and unit-tested):

- **The tunings, in one place** (`CALENDAR`): twelve minutes a day, seven days a season (a year of 28 days, about five and a half hours of play), a lunar cycle of 8.4 days (not a divisor of the year, so the moon's phase on a festival differs from year to year, repeating every three years), Helsinki's latitude, and the start: the seventh day of spring, a late-May morning at nine, two days before midsummer, under a waxing gibbous moon.
- **Each game day stands for about thirteen days of the real year**, spread evenly: the seasons are the Nordic ones by the month (spring from 1 March, summer from June, autumn from September, winter from December), and every date carries the real day of the year it stands for, moving on smoothly through the hours, so anything seasonal keys on real dates. A date gives the day count, year, season, day of the season, progress through the season and the year, the hour, the real day of the year and the moon's age.
- **The festivals fall on the game days that hold their real dates**: the solstices and equinoxes on each season's second day (Ostara, Litha, Mabon, Yule) and the cross-quarter days on its fifth (Beltane, Lughnasadh, Samhain, Imbolc). A day knows its festival, and the next one and how many days off it is.
- **The sun** is worked out from the real day of the year by simplified real formulas (its eccentric orbit along the ecliptic, the declination it gives, local solar time, so it is highest at noon): its direction, elevation and azimuth, and the day's sunrise, sunset, day length and the civil, nautical and astronomical twilights, within a few minutes of Helsinki's almanac. A midsummer night only reaches nautical twilight; real dark comes back in August.
- **The moon** is a body offset east of the sun along the ecliptic by its age through the cycle, inclined a few degrees, so it rises and sets as its phase says (a full moon opposite the sun, rising at sunset and riding low on a summer night and high on a winter one; a new moon beside the sun) and the angle between them is its phase: lit by the sun's real direction, a sphere shows the right phase by itself. It gives its age, elongation, phase angle, the fraction lit, waxing or waning, and the phase's name.
- **The light follows the sun's real height**: the six moments are stops along the sun's elevation, night, dawn and morning as it climbs and golden hour, dusk and night as it sinks, turning from the one set to the other over the hours round noon and midnight. So a midsummer evening is golden at eight and in twilight at half past ten, and a midwinter noon, with the sun six degrees up in the south, is low and warm, between morning and golden hour. The key light is the sun by day and the moon by night, the moon's light by its phase.
- **Dates in the address:** `?date=` starts on a date, written as a season and its day (`summer-2`), a festival (`litha`) or a day number, and `?hour=` holds the hour on it.
- **The land's year** (`engine/seasons.ts`) is plain numbers on the real day of the year, so it moves on smoothly through the hours and never jumps at midnight. Each season group of plants (birch, aspen, oak, maple, conifer, pine, heather, meadow flowers, fern, bracken, bush, and stone) keeps a phenology of a Nordic year at Helsinki's latitude, a little early in spring so the game's spring reads as one: how much leaf is out, how young and light its green is, how far it has turned (autumn colour; for conifers, winter's darker needles; for heather, its winter rust) and how much it is in flower. Birches, aspens and maples leaf out from late April, are fresh into June, turn from mid-September to their peak round the start of October and are bare by late October; oaks leaf and turn later and keep a few withered leaves all winter; meadow flowers bloom from late April through August and are gone by winter; heather blooms through August; ferns, bracken and bushes brown in autumn. Each plant keeps its own dates a few days either side of its group's, and picks one of its kind's two autumn colours, from a hash of where it stands, so no two trees in a wood turn alike. The ground keeps its own year: the snow line (below the whole land from December to early March, melting up the mountains through spring, above the high snowfields all summer, which stay regardless, and coming down them from October), last year's dormant straw after the melt and again in November, the new green of May, the autumn gold of the grass, the leaves fallen under the broadleaves, and how far the water freezes in from its edges. `seasonWeights` gives how much of each season a day is, handing over across the first of each season's month.

## The sky

- **The sky** is drawn from the shared air: the horizon's haze up to the zenith's blue, each moment's colours blended by the sun's real height, with a glow round the sun and its disc.
- **The moon is a real moon**: a lit sphere, so its phase is the sun lighting it from where the sun really is, never a drawn crescent; faint by day, with earthshine on its dark side by night, rising and setting as its phase says.
- **Stars** turn with the night on the engine's celestial frame: a seeded field worked out per pixel, so it never shimmers. A star shows only once it is brighter than the sky behind it, so a summer night shows few.
- **Northern lights** come on some nights, seeded by the night and mostly in the long dark of autumn and winter (`engine/aurora.ts`): curtains of green with violet tops across the northern sky, drifting slowly. `?aurora=on|off` holds them on or off.
- **The light** is the sun by day and the moon by night by its phase, each fading near the horizon so the handover is in the dark, with light from the sky above and the ground below, tone mapped to keep hues true.

## Look

**Open.** The aim is cozy, low-poly, colourful and beautiful, starting from something natural rather than growing into a style, and with nothing that has to be paid for in performance yet. Its colours are its own tokens, scoped to the project. Claude makes the art as code for now; models from outside (poly.pizza's, say) may come with the props, credited as the site requires.

What is drawn: the land painted per pixel by its bands (meadow and forest floor, alpine grass above the trees, scree and rock up high and on steep ground, sand at the shore, snow down to the season's line); soft low-poly plants, a few designs per kind varied by seed; tufts of grass blades swaying in the meadows; flat water coloured by its depth, with the sky in it at a glance and its falls white; the tors' slabs and the cascades' white water.

## Controls

**The free camera** only, for now: the world is for looking at and flying over. A click on the world locks the pointer and the mouse looks; Esc frees it. WASD or the arrows fly the way it looks, E or Space rises, Q or C sinks, Shift is five times faster, and the wheel steps the speed from 1 m/s to 5 km/s. On a controller the left stick flies, the right stick looks, A rises, the left trigger sinks, the right trigger is faster and the bumpers step the speed. It never goes below the ground. There are no touch controls yet.

The address sets the world for a visit: `?seed=` another world, `?hour=` a held hour, `?date=` a date, `?aurora=on|off`.

## No saving

Nothing is saved: the world regenerates the same from its seed.

## Later

The game around the world was designed and built once (companions, people and their stories, mysteries, foraging, cooking and brewing at the campfire, camp moving, walking and the broom, weather, and a Proton-inspired stack of graphical features) and taken out to rebuild on a simple base. Its design and code are in git history, in the commits before the WebGPU baseline, to bring back piece by piece once the world looks right: first props and buildings, then people and animals, then the gameplay.

## Open questions

- **Seas and oceans**: the world has lakes, tarns, rivers and waterfalls but no sea yet; a coast is a change to the world's generation.
- **Plants on far, coarse land** may float or sink a little where a tile is eased into its parent's shape.
- **Touch controls** for the free camera, to look at the world on the phone.
- **The look**, all of it, waits on the owner's eye: the colours, the grass's density and reach, the haze.

- `camera/`: `flight.ts` (the free camera's maths), `fly-controls.ts` (what each input means), `fly-input.ts` (the page's keys, mouse, wheel and controller).
