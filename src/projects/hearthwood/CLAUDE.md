# Hearthwood

A cozy 3D game about a wandering hedge-witch and the cats they meet. You live outdoors in a valley of forest and mountain made fresh from a seed: by day you walk and forage, by evening you cook and brew at your campfire, and the stray cats of the woods come round one by one, each won over by the dish it loves. When the valley has given what it has, you pack up camp and walk over the ridge into a new one.

It lives at Games → 3D Games → Hearthwood, in Three.js through `@react-three/fiber`, like the site's other 3D games. The concept and the platform are decided; everything below them is the proposed design, open to the owner's review, with the undecided parts under Open questions.

## Pillars

- **Nothing is ever lost.** No fail states, no combat, no hunger or health meter, no timer that punishes. A meal that comes out unexpected is a new dish, not a failure. A cat never runs away, falls ill or dies.
- **Cooking is for joy and for friendship, never for survival.**
- **Every walk finds something.** The world is generated so that wandering always turns up a herb, a view, a cat or a ruin, and never a long empty stretch.
- **Calm by default.** Soft sound, slow light, no notifications nagging for attention. The game waits as long as the player likes.
- **Witchcraft is gentle and earthy**: hedge-witchery, herbs, the moon and the Wheel of the Year, treated with warmth and respect, never as spooky set dressing.

## Target device

Hearthwood's floor is a **Pixel 8 Pro** in Chrome, held upright or sideways. It replaces the site-wide floor of phones up to four years old within this project: layouts are designed and checked at its viewport. Laptops and a laptop on a TV with a controller are above that floor.

**Performance comes last.** While the game is a prototype, the work is making it play right and look right; making it run smoothly on the phone's GPU (Tensor G3's Mali-G715) is a later pass, done once play and look are settled, and only as far as it turns out to be needed. Nothing is held back for a budget before then. The world and the art gallery always show a stats.js frame meter in the top right, to watch the frame rate while playing. Every knob that trades how the world looks for how fast it draws (how far the land reaches and how fine it is with distance, how far plants are drawn and in which detail, thinning, the pixel ratio, the sun's shadow map) lives in one quality profile, `quality.ts`; the game runs on its desktop profile, tuned for looks (plants in full detail out to a long way with no thinning, the native pixel ratio up to 2, a 2048 shadow map), and a phone's profile is another of the same shape, added when the performance pass comes. Haze is never one of those knobs: it belongs to the weather (see The world).

## Art, gameplay and engine are decoupled

The art must be workable on its own, without touching the rules or the engine, and the reverse. So the layers stay apart:

- **The engine** (generating the world, time, input, streaming) is pure where it can be, and knows nothing of how anything looks.
- **Gameplay** (the walker, the cats' behaviour, camp, foraging, cooking) is state and rules, with no Three.js in it, and is tested without drawing anything.
- **Art** (models, materials, palettes, motion, light) takes plain data in (a cat, a tree's seed, the hour) and gives drawing back. It never decides anything the game depends on.
- **The scene** is the thin layer between them that draws the game's state with the art.

A change of look is a change in the art layer alone; a change of rules never needs the art redone.

## The loop

A day has a shape, though nothing forces it:

- **Morning to dusk, the walk.** Out from camp along trails and off them: forage, meet cats, find places. Cats of the camp come along and wander near.
- **Evening, the hearth.** Back at the campfire: cook, brew, feed the cats, sit and look at the stars. Sleeping by the fire starts the next day.
- **Moving on.** When the player chooses, camp is packed and the walk over the ridge begins (see Walks between valleys). Camp, its cats and the player's recipes always come along.

## The world

The world is one continuous landscape, endless in every direction, made from a seed with the site's `seeded-random` as pure functions in the project's engine. Any spot always comes out the same, however the world was walked to get there, so it is generated by area as the player goes, and every part of it is unit-testable. `?seed=<text>` opens another world.

**One landscape, never a world of bowls.** The land comes from a few large, slowly varying fields across the whole world: how high the ground broadly lies, how wet it is, how hilly, and where the mountains rise. Nothing has a hand-shaped rim; valleys are simply the land between ranges, in every shape, and a long walk always leads somewhere different.

**Mountains** run as long winding ranges along the lines where a broad field crosses zero, so they wind for kilometres, rise and fall along their crests (where a crest dips there is a pass) and part in wide gaps, leaving the lowlands between them open. A slow field of its own gives each range its character, drawn from the real mountains the game's owner loves, by way of all of Scandinavia:

- **High alpine ranges**, like Norway's Jotunheimen and the Lyngen Alps (and so the Tetons and the Alps): jagged peaks rising steeply out of pine forest to around 900 m above the lowlands, far above the treeline, through alpine meadow and scree to snowfields on the tops. Cirques hold tarns, cascades fall down the valley walls, and the high green valleys hold seter, the turf-roofed summer farms.
- **Fells**, as in Lapland: broad rounded tops rising bare above the trees.
- **Long rounded ridges side by side**, as in central Sweden and Norway or the Appalachians: folded country whose ridges all run roughly one way across a world, layered into the distance and wooded to their tops.
- **Lakeland lowlands**, as in Finland: open country of birch and pine with many lakes.

Big mountains are common enough to stand on most horizons, and a walk can reach them; the walker climbs anything up to about 45° and wades anything shallower than a paddle, so cliffs and lakes turn them aside, passes and valleys lead through, and the world has no wall or edge.

**Bands.** The treeline sits low, as it does at this latitude, so the peaks rise far above the forest: woods below about 255 m, alpine grass above, bare rock from about 420 m (and on any ground too steep to hold soil), snow from about 610 m, clinging to steeper ground the higher it goes. Each band's edge wanders up and down the slopes.

**Water stands on terraces**, so it never needs to know the whole map and never runs uphill. The water table is the broad lie of the land rounded down to steps of a few metres, so every lake and every reach of river is level. Lakes lie wherever the land dips below its water: in the wet lowlands' hollows, and as tarns in cirques up in the ranges, each held in by a lip of moraine. Rivers follow the borders of a pattern of cells laid over the world in a warped frame, so they wind, meet at junctions and part, never close into rings, and start at springs where a border holds no river; each cuts its own channel and a broad valley whose sides rise gently from it, keeps to the lowlands and hills, and fades out as the land climbs high above it. Where a river crosses from one terrace down to the next it drops over a waterfall, and just below each terrace's edge the land rises in a gentle bank that holds the higher water in. Rivers are always shallow enough to wade.

**Regions with a character of their own**, each with its own slowly varying field, nudged by the land, and blending softly across a border a few hundred metres wide, so a long walk is a journey through country after country:

- **Greenwood**, home: mixed oak, fir and birch with open meadow, flowers and bushes. It holds the land round the world's origin.
- **Birch vale**, the lakeland: light birch woods with pine and golden aspen, white and yellow flowers, ferns, chanterelles, fairy rings.
- **Pine highlands**, the mountains: Scots pine and fir up to the treeline, rocks and heather, and a misty air of their own.
- **Heath**: open moorland of golden grass and heather (purple, pink, and gorse in flower), lone pines and birches, tors and boulders, and a clear sky.
- **Maple wood**: dense autumn woods of red, orange and gold maple with golden aspen, bracken, mushrooms and fallen giants.

**Life by area.** The plants and stones of each 64 m square are scattered from that square's own seed. The woods stand in groves and open into glades, cover as much of the land as their region says, thin out to the treeline and keep back from water, camp and the places; each region has its own trees, high and low, and its own undergrowth in the open and under the trees. Fireflies come out over the meadows at night, more by water and in the softer regions.

**Places by area.** The world is cut into cells of about 190 m, and about half of them hold one place, found from the cell's own seed and its land alone, so every walk finds something every few hundred metres: a stone circle, a cairn with a bench on a rise, a tor of weathered slabs, a wayside shrine with a candle and an offering, a fallen giant tree, a fairy ring, a seter, a tarn, a cascade down a valley wall, or a waterfall wherever a river falls from one terrace to the next. Each region has its own mix, each kind is put where it fits best among a scatter of candidates (a stone circle on level ground, a cairn on a rise, a tarn in a hollow), and a place keeps well inside its cell, so what it levels and clears never reaches the next. Trails between places are left for later: they relied on knowing the whole map.

**Camp**, for now, is a level clearing on the greenwood's meadow a short walk from the world's origin, with its fire, two seat logs, a bedroll and a pot on a tripod. A camp pitched anywhere is gameplay for later.

**What is drawn, and how far.** There is no practical limit to how far the player sees: on a clear day the land runs to the horizon, as with Minecraft's Distant Horizons. The far world is the real land at coarser and coarser detail. The land is a quadtree of square tiles, each the same number of vertices, each level twice the width of the one inside it; a tile splits into four finer ones as the camera comes within a fixed number of its widths, so a triangle covers about the same part of the screen at any distance and the cost grows only with the logarithm of how far the view reaches (about a hundred kilometres, past where the curve of the earth has hidden all but the highest peaks). Far tiles sample the world's fields directly at their own spacing, leaving out what is finer than that spacing can show, so distant land never shimmers. Tiles are worked out in workers off the main thread, kept a while once made, and the drawn set changes over all at once when every tile it needs is ready, so no hole ever opens; each tile eases into its parent's shape over the stretch before it is swapped for it, and hangs a skirt from its edges, so levels join with no seam or pop. The land drops away by the curve of the earth. Plants are drawn as instances, in full near by and in a simpler shape further out, with no thinning; past where trees are drawn, the woods are painted into the land as their canopy, mottled crown by crown, fading in before the last trees so no edge shows. Places stream in within about a kilometre. Depth uses a reversed depth buffer, precise from a hand's breadth to the horizon with no change to how the materials shade.

**Haze is weather**, never a way of hiding the edge of the world: the world must hold up on a perfectly clear day on its own. `?haze=<n>` scales it (0 is a perfectly clear day) until the weather drives it.

## Walks between valleys

The journey part. Leaving a valley is a hike over a generated mountain trail to the next one: the trail climbs, crosses a pass with a view down over both valleys, and descends. A longer hike may take more than a day, with a camp on the way: a small fire on a ledge, a meal from what is in the pack, the stars from higher up. Some walks go to a summit for the view and nothing else.

## Cats

The heart of the game. Every cat is generated, so no two are the same, and every one looks like a real cat because its coat is drawn from real cat coat genetics: the base colour and its dilute, orange and the tortoiseshell and calico it makes, tabby patterns (mackerel, classic, spotted, ticked), white markings from none to van, colourpoint, long or short hair, and eye colour. On top of the look, each cat has:

- **A personality**, from a few traits (bold or shy, lazy or busy, cuddly or aloof) that show in how it moves, where it naps and how close it walks.
- **A favourite dish**, and a few it likes. A stray is won over gradually: it watches from a distance, then comes closer for food, and joins the camp when it is given the dish it loves.
- **A name the player gives it.**

Cats of the camp follow on walks, nap by the fire, sit on the player's pack and sometimes lead the way to something worth finding. Petting a cat is always available and always answered.

**How a cat is made** (decided, built in `cats/`):

- **Everything from one seed**, each part (genes, coat, build, personality) from its own seeded stream, so adding to one never changes another.
- **Genes are drawn at street-population frequencies**: black, chocolate and cinnamon, each dense or dilute; orange on the X chromosome (one copy for a tom, two for a queen, so only queens are tortoiseshell or calico, and most orange cats are toms); agouti, which shows the tabby pattern (ticked over mackerel over classic, mackerel broken into spots by a modifier), while orange shows its pattern regardless; white spotting, none or a locket with no copies, low to middling white with one, high white to van with two; a rare dominant white; colourpoint; long hair. A population looks like a real one: mostly black, tabby and their dilutes, about one in six long-haired, colourpoints rare. Eyes follow the coat: points are always blue, high white is often blue or odd-eyed, the rest run green to copper. Silver, smoke and the other breeders' genes are left out.
- **Build** varies size (toms bigger, with broader heads), stockiness (a cobby cat runs to shorter legs), leg, back, tail and ear length, as moved and scaled bones on one shared sculpture, so the build costs nothing per cat.
- **Personality** is three traits from −1 to 1, and shows in motion: a bold cat holds its head and tail higher, a shy one crouches with its ears turned out; a busy cat looks round, flicks its ears and twitches its tail more often; a lazy one breathes slower and lids its eyes; a cuddly cat slow-blinks.
- **The model** is one smooth skinned body: signed-distance forms (the shared toolkit in `src/shared/lib/three/sdf.ts`, which Betrayal's figures also use) blended round a skeleton of pivots, each vertex following the bones of the forms nearest it, so the body bends smoothly where the forms were blended. The head is meshed on a finer grid than the body so the ears keep their edge. Faces are painted, not sculpted: big round eyes with a slit pupil and a glint, a small nose, the inside of the ears.
- **The coat is a shader**: every marking is a pattern function of where a point lies on the cat in its bind pose, so it moves with the body: the tortie's patches (brindled, or bigger and cleaner on a calico), the tabby pattern with its forehead M, cheek lines, leg bars and tail rings, the colourpoint's mask and points, and the white spotting, which grows from the chest and belly through the paws, legs, muzzle and blaze to the sides and back, leaving the head's cap and the tail for last. Long hair is a fuller silhouette (ruff, britches, plume) and a softly lumped surface; no fur cards.
- **Motion** is pure functions of the cat's clock, the distance it has walked and its speed: postures for standing, walking (the lateral-sequence walk, each paw planted while it is down), sitting and loafing, blended over a second as the activity changes before the legs are solved to their paws, with the tail up in greeting over any of them, and breathing, blinks, ear flicks, look-arounds and tail sway laid on top as seeded occasional movements.
- **Budget**: a cat is about 12,000 triangles short-haired, 15,000 long-haired, and two draws (its body and a soft blob shadow), with one compiled coat shader shared by every cat. Meshing a hair length takes about a second on a desktop and runs once a session in a worker. The coat's fragment shader is the heaviest part, a few layers of value noise; if several cats close up ever cost too much on the target phone, the pattern can move to the vertices.

## Foraging and cooking

Ingredients are found, not bought: herbs, mushrooms, berries, roots, nuts, wild greens, river fish, eggs from a farm a valley might hold. What grows changes with the season.

Cooking happens at the campfire in pans, a pot and on sticks over the flames, and is tactile and short: choose ingredients, cook them, see the dish. Recipes are discovered by trying combinations, and remembered in the player's recipe book once found. Cooking can't fail: a combination with no recipe makes a humble dish of its own, which a cat may still love.

## Witchcraft

- **Brewing** at the hearth: teas, salves and charms from foraged things. A charm does something gentle and visible (makes flowers bloom round camp, calls fireflies, draws a shy cat closer, clears the fog from a view).
- **The moon** goes through its phases night by night; some herbs are gathered only under a full moon.
- **The Wheel of the Year** is the calendar (see Seasons).
- **Rituals** at the festivals and at places of power (a stone circle, a sacred spring): small, quiet set pieces such as lighting a Beltane fire or leaving an offering at Mabon.

## Seasons

The game's year is the Wheel of the Year: its eight festivals (Samhain, Yule, Imbolc, Ostara, Beltane, Litha, Lughnasadh and Mabon) divide the seasons, and each changes the light, the weather, what grows and what can be brewed. Weather is seeded and gentle: mist, soft rain, snow, golden evenings. A season lasts long enough to enjoy and passes on its own.

## The night sky

Each valley's sky is generated, and the stars form constellations the player can find and name by the fire. The moon's phase is shown truly.

## Controls

The game plays on all three input methods (a controller, touch, and keyboard + mouse), switching to whichever was used last, through the shared input and gamepad libraries and the shared button prompts (see the root `CLAUDE.md`). It is a third-person game: the player walks, the camera follows and can be turned, and nearby things to pick up, pet or use are offered with a prompt.

Walking, as built: the move goes the way the camera looks, and the witch turns to face where they walk.

- **Keyboard and mouse:** WASD or the arrows walk, Shift runs, dragging the mouse turns the camera, Q and E turn it too, and the wheel moves it in and out.
- **Touch:** a stick appears under the thumb wherever it lands on the left half of the screen, walking at the push and running at its rim; dragging on the right half turns the camera, and pinching there moves it in and out.
- **Controller:** the left stick walks at its push, the right stick turns the camera, and the right trigger runs.
- The camera orbits the witch, within limits of tilt and distance, and comes in along its line where the land rises behind them rather than climbing over it.
- How to walk is shown, for the input used last, as its controls beside a symbol of what each does, until that input has walked; on touch, as a ghost stick on the left and a turning symbol on the right.

## Look

Hearthwood has its own style: a warm, soft storybook diorama, with golden light and the fire as the brightest thing at night. Its colours are its own tokens, scoped to the project.

**Claude makes all of the art, so the style is chosen for what art written as code does well.** Every model, material, animation and effect is code: no image textures, no models from outside, no hand-keyed animation.

What carries the look, because code does it well:

- **Light and atmosphere** are where the mood comes from: the sun's colour through the day, fog in the valleys, rim light on the forest edge, the fire's flicker on everything near it, and a colour grade per season.
- **Simple, readable forms**: soft low-poly shapes, smooth rounded solids and silhouettes that read from a distance, built from primitives, smooth signed-distance forms and lofts, the way Betrayal's figures are sculpted.
- **Procedural materials**: bark, moss, stone, water and fur patterns are shader functions of position and noise, not painted pictures.
- **Many of a few things**: a forest is a handful of tree designs, each varied by seed (height, lean, branching, colour) and drawn instanced.
- **Particles**: embers, smoke, steam from the pot, fireflies, falling leaves, snow, rain.
- **Procedural motion**: idles, walks, a tail's sway, breathing and ear flicks as functions of time and speed, not keyframes.

What the style stays away from, because code does it badly: realistic or detailed faces, photoreal materials, hand-painted detail, intricate one-off sculpts, and expressive acting. Characters are seen mostly at a distance or from behind; faces are a few simple shapes (eyes, a nose, ears) that read through pose and motion rather than expression.

**Cats** are the hardest art in the game and its heart, so they get the most care: a stylised cat of a few smooth forms, recognisably a cat by its silhouette, ears and tail and by how it moves (the stretch, the loaf, the tail up in greeting). Its coat is a shader driven by its genetics (see Cats): a tabby's stripes and a calico's patches are pattern functions, which is exactly what code does well. The witch is a silhouette first: a cloak, a hat, a pack.

Art is reviewed from headless screenshots taken through the repo's own tooling and revised until it is done, the loop Betrayal's art skills follow; once Hearthwood's art has a working method, it becomes a skill of its own.

The world's light, as built, is simple and its own (nothing is shared with Shipwright's physically based light):

- **The day** lasts twelve minutes and blends between six moments, night, dawn, morning, noon, golden hour and dusk, each with its own sky, haze, key light, light from the sky and the ground, and shadow tint, all tokens. The sun lights the day and the moon the night, each fading near the horizon so the handover happens in the dark.
- **Every material ends in the world's air:** a haze thickest low down and thinning up the mountains, thicker in the pine highlands' mist and clearer over the heath, warmed towards the sun, then a gentle grade that lifts saturation and tints the shadows towards the moment's own colour. The haze is the horizon's colour, so the land melts into the sky. Tone mapping keeps hues true (Khronos neutral), and there are no post passes, for the target phone's fill rate.
- **Shapes:** trees are soft primitives with their colours in their vertices, crowns shading as one mass, swaying in a breeze; rocks and stones are lumpy flat-shaded solids; huts are logs under turf. The water lights itself: shallows to deep by the bed's depth, foam at the shore, the sky at a glance and glints of sun; a river's streaks run the way it flows and whiten over falls and down cascades.
- **The ground is painted per pixel, not per vertex:** each vertex carries its region's own grounds and the smooth fields the cover is read from (the woods, the water, the river, the bands' wobble), and each ground (the forest floor, alpine grass, rock in weathered bands, snow, the wet shore, the trodden earth round the fire) begins where its field crosses a threshold, softened and set wavering by a little noise, so every edge reads as a soft line at any angle and never follows the mesh's triangles. A fine grain of noise lies over it all. Anything narrow drawn on the land later (a path, a game trail) is drawn the same way, or as a ribbon with a soft edge, never as paint on the vertices.
- **The fire** is soft flame shells, rising embers, a glow and a flickering light, brightest at night, when fireflies come out over the meadows and the stars overhead.
- **Pixel ratio and shadows** come from the quality profile (see Target device): the device's own pixel ratio up to 2, the sun's shadows a 2048 map round the walker, and the sky drawn last, only where nothing covers it.
- The day can be held at an hour with `?hour=<0-24>`, or set and held from the debug panel that ~ opens; another world opens with `?seed=<text>`, and `?haze=<n>` scales the haze.

**The art gallery** (`?art`) shows every piece of the art on a plain floor, in rows by kind: each region's plant designs with their variants, rocks, every kind of place, the camp and its burning fire, fireflies, Elliott and a few cats. It is lit by the game's own day and air, with the same ~ panel, and can be orbited and zoomed by mouse, touch or pad, with any piece picked (or stepped to with the pad's bumpers) to see it alone on a turntable. It is built from the art layer alone, with no world generated and no rules, which is the proof that the art stands on its own; a new piece appears there by being added to its one list, and a new kind of plant or place appears without even that.

## Saving

Single-player and saved on the device: the current valley's seed, camp, cats, recipes, the season and the day. No account needed.

## First slice

The smallest version that is already the game:

- the endless world, with day and night;
- walking in third person on all three input methods;
- a handful of ingredients to forage and a few recipes to cook at the campfire;
- generated cats to meet, befriend with their favourite dish, and bring to camp;
- saving.

The walks between valleys, brewing, seasons, festivals and the night sky follow, one at a time.

## Open questions

- **Who is the player?** A fixed witch, or one the player designs?
- **The cats on screen:** how many can camp hold before it stops being cozy, and does camp ever outgrow its clearing?
- **Do valleys ever repeat?** A valley left behind could be revisited by its seed, or every walk could lead somewhere new.
- **Other people:** a valley could hold a few gentle characters (a beekeeper, a travelling tinker), or the game could be the witch and the cats alone. Since faces are the art's weak point, characters would have to work at a distance and through silhouette.
- **Music:** generated, composed, or ambient sound only.

## Code map

- `cats/`: the cats, pure where it can be. `genetics.ts` (the genotype, drawn at population frequencies, and the coat it makes; `coatName`), `cat.ts` (`generateCat`: genes, coat, build and personality from a seed), `coat-look.ts` (a coat as the shader's colour tokens and numbers), `rig.ts` (the skeleton's pivots and proportions; `rigOf` a cat's build), `sculpt.ts` (the body's signed-distance forms and their skinned mesh), `motion.ts` (`catPose`: postures, the walk, blends and fidgets, and the two-segment reach the legs are solved with).
- `components/cats/`: `cat-body.tsx` (`CatBody`, one cat drawn and moving from props: activity, speed, greeting and an optional clock; whoever places it moves and turns its group), `skinned-cat.ts` (a cat's skinned mesh and skeleton, posed each frame), `coat-material.ts` (the coat shader), `cat-geometry.ts` and `cat-mesh.worker.ts` (the shared sculpture, meshed once per hair length in a worker), `cat-preview.tsx` (the `?cats` preview: a meadow of nine cats, reroll, and one cat on its own to stand, walk, sit, loaf, greet and turn).
- `tools/cat-shots.mjs`: headless screenshots of the preview through its control surface (`window.__hearthwoodCats`), on a frozen clock: the field on desktop and on the target phone, and each chosen cat by activity and view, with a sheet of them all.
- `engine/`: the world and time, pure and unit-tested, knowing nothing of looks. `world.ts` (`createWorld`: the land with camp and the places levelled in, regions, woods, places and plants by area, kept a while once worked out), `land.ts` (the broad fields, the ranges and their characters, hills, lakes, the water's terraces and the bands' altitudes, sampled band-limited from far off), `rivers.ts` (where rivers run), `regions.ts` (the regions, the kinds of plant and place, and each region's character as plain data), `places.ts` (camp, and each cell's place), `life.ts` (the woods' density and each chunk's plants and fireflies), `tiles.ts` (a tile of land as plain arrays for the art), `land-tile.worker.ts` (tiles worked out off the main thread), `streaming.ts` (which tiles and plant chunks the camera wants, and plant detail by distance), `day.ts` (the hour, the sun and moon, and each moment's light), `noise.ts`.
- `gameplay/`: rules, with no Three.js. `walking.ts` (where a walker may go).
- `quality.ts`: every look-for-speed knob in one profile.
- `input/`: walking's input. `walk-controls.ts` (each input's meaning, pure) and `use-walk-input.ts` (all three inputs into one state, and the input used last).
- `art/`: the art, plain data in and drawing out, deciding nothing. `palette.ts` (the tokens, each region's grounds and its air), `atmosphere.ts` (the haze and grade every material ends in), `daylight.tsx` (the day's light from the hour), `sky.tsx`, `terrain.ts` (a tile's mesh and the per-pixel ground, the far canopy, the geomorph and the earth's curve), `water.ts` (water's shader, a tile's water, a still pool), `plant-shapes.ts` and `plant-instances.ts` (the plant designs, and a plant's placing and colour), `place-shapes.ts` (the places, and falling water), `campfire.tsx`, `fireflies.tsx`, `witch.tsx` (Elliott, a placeholder silhouette), `painted.ts` (vertex-coloured shapes from pieces).
- `components/valley/`: the world's scene, gluing the engine to the art. `world-view.tsx` (the canvas, its overlay, the clock and the air), `world-land.tsx` with `land-workers.ts` (the land streamed in from the workers), `world-plants.tsx` (plants streamed and instanced), `world-places.tsx`, `clock.ts`, `walker.tsx` and `follow-camera.ts` (walking and the camera), `walk-hints.tsx` (the prompts and the touch stick), `debug-panel.tsx`, `frame-stats.ts`, `world-probe.tsx` (`window.__hearthwoodWorld`: ready and settled, frames, what was drawn, switches for the hour, motion, haze, camera, a free camera and the walker, readings of the world at a point, and a top-down map of any area drawn from the world).
- `components/gallery/`: the `?art` gallery. `pieces.tsx` (the one list of pieces) and `art-gallery.tsx` (the floor, the rows, the orbit and turntable, and `window.__hearthwoodArt`).
- `tools/world-shots.mjs`: headless screenshots of the world through its probe, on a held hour and motion clock: top-down maps of 6 and 16 km (by region and by relief), camp through the day, a walk in each region at morning, golden hour and night, a range from afar, clear-day views from a valley floor, a ridge and a summit, each kind of place, and the touch controls on the target phone, with what each frame drew.
- `tools/art-shots.mjs`: headless screenshots of the gallery: the whole of it through the day, each row, and chosen pieces alone.
