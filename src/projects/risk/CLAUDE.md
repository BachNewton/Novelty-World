# Risk

The board game Risk: its rules, its engine, and a game screen playable on touch, keyboard and mouse, and a controller, played online with people and bots.

**Risk is online-only for now.** Every game is a table on the server, joined by its code. Local play (a game passed round one device, or several controllers on one TV) may come later; the screen already plays whatever feed it is given, so it would be a new feed, not a new screen.

**One engine for every real game.** `play-engine.ts` is the single place the engine real games play on is chosen: the reference engine (`engine/`). The server route and a phone applying its own moves ahead of the server both take it from there. There are no stand-in engines.

## Rules

Classic Risk, with escalating card values for set trades, set up as RISK: Global Domination's Auto Placement does: the territories are dealt and the starting armies placed at random, so nobody makes a setup decision and a new game begins at the first player's first turn (`newGame` plays the whole setup from the seed; the screen shows the board already set up, its deal playing as it mounts). `RULES.md` in this folder is the source of truth for the rules: the engine, its tests and the bots follow it, and a question about a rule is settled there, not in code.

Ambiguities are settled the way Betrayal settles them: a note beside the rule, and a resolution labelled with its authority (official, official digital, community, project or unresolved). Where the rulebooks are unclear, the official digital game, RISK: Global Domination, is deferred to before community practice or a project ruling. `RULES.md` opens with the method. Claude makes the project rulings as a game designer would, and brings one to the owner only when it would fork the engine or the architecture.

## The engine contract

`contract.ts` is the boundary between the engine and everything that uses it: the UI, the server route that plays real games, and the bots. They never read the engine's state; they ask for a seat's **view** (what that seat may see), its **options** (the legal moves, grouped so a screen can highlight targets), and **apply** a move, getting back the new state and the **events** that happened, each naming the rule behind it. `map.ts` is the board as data, shared by both sides. The contract is the same whichever language the fast engine ends up in, so the UI and the engine are built in parallel against it.

- **One source of legality.** A move is legal exactly when `apply` accepts it; the options are a convenience a test holds equal to that. A step the rules leave no choice over is taken by the engine, not offered.
- **Hidden information is kept by good faith, as in Betrayal.** The whole state, every hand and the deck order included, lives in one game row every player can read, and the UI shows each seat only its own view and events with other players' drawn cards removed. Players are trusted to look only at what the UI shows them; there is no per-player secret storage.
- **Bots are never trusted: they see only what their seat may legally see.** That is enforced by construction, not good faith: a bot is given its seat's view and options, never the game state, so hidden information is unreachable from bot code. A searching bot that needs whole states to simulate builds them from its view, filling in what it can't see (other hands, the deck order) by sampling what is consistent with it, never by reading the real ones.
- **Moves are sized for a network.** Every move is a round trip to the server, so a whole assault is one move (blitz: attack until captured, or down to a chosen number of armies), alongside the single battle.
- **The reference engine runs real games; the fast engine runs bots.** Real games, on the server and for legality on the phone, use the reference engine, which is TypeScript and obviously correct. The fast engine exists for bot speed, and the differential tests keep the two identical.

## Visual style and map

Risk has its own style, separate from Novelty World's bright, quirky one: a war room, campaign maps, brass and parchment. Its colours come from its own design tokens, scoped to the project. The board is our own, never Hasbro's board art. It is drawn as a graph of territories and borders, made to be read; geographic art may come later.

**Ultrawide monitors are a primary device too** (21:9, such as a 3440×1440 panel at 125% scaling, which a browser sees as 2752×1152 CSS pixels; and 32:9, such as 5120×1440). The extra width is spent on information, not on stretching the board: the board stays at its most readable size, and panels beside it show what a narrower screen keeps behind a tap (the battle log, every player's standing, continent control, the odds of a planned attack).

**The iPad is a primary device**, in both orientations (iPad about 820×1180, iPad mini 744×1133), with touch as its input. Every iPad browser runs on WebKit, Safari's engine, so a change to the screen is looked at in WebKit as well as Chromium (Playwright's WebKit, headless). A screenshot of the board must wait for its territories to mount and their deal to finish: the deal fades them in, so a shot taken early shows a board that looks empty.

**Risk's width floor is 375px**, raising the site's 360px floor for this project only: the whole board has to stay readable and tappable, and 42 territories are its hardest layout problem.

- **The board is a graph with a fixed layout** (`board/graph-layout.ts`), so players learn it: each territory a node, each border an edge, each continent a tinted region with its name and bonus, outlined in a seat's colour while that seat holds it all. `board/layout_graph.py` makes it offline, once, from a hand sketch that keeps the continents where Risk players expect them (North America left, South America below it, Europe and Africa in the middle, Asia right, Australia lower right) and places territories within a continent for the graph, not geography; a gentle relaxation then evens the spacing without letting any border cross another. It is tuned by editing the sketch and running the script again, never at runtime. Borders between continents are drawn brighter than those inside one. Alaska–Kamchatka runs off both sides of the board, as on Risk's own board, each end's line leading to a ghost of the territory across the edge, which can be tapped. Tests hold the layout to `map.ts` (every territory and border, nothing more), with no crossings and every territory a fingertip apart on a 375px phone.
- **Nodes** show the owner's colour and shape, the armies large in the middle and a short name (`ui/short-names.ts`); the full name is the tooltip. The territories on offer light in brass, the chosen source and target more strongly, with the borders between them, and a fortifying army's march through its owner's territories is drawn as a lit path.
- **The geographic board is kept, unused**, for when the board gets art: `board/generate.py` draws it from Natural Earth's admin-1 provinces (downloaded into a directory of its own, never committed), and only its simplified output, `board/board-data.ts`, is in the repo. It is stylised as Risk boards are: projected with the Bering Strait as the board's edge, magnified smoothly round the crowded places (Europe, Central America, the Malay archipelago, Japan) by lenses that only stretch the plane, archipelagos closed up into chunkier land, small islands dropped and a few scaled up. The generator checks the result against `map.ts`: territories that touch must be neighbours, and neighbours that don't touch get a sea line.
- **Colour is never the only cue.** Each seat has an ink and a shape, on its territories on the board and beside its name, and is named by its colour (`ui/seats.ts`).
- **The tokens** are `components/theme.ts`, applied on the game's root; the display face is Cinzel.
- **The map pans and zooms on every input.** It starts on the whole board while that is readable, and otherwise zoomed to a readable size: a phone in portrait fills the map's height and is panned across rather than shrunk to a strip.

## The game screen and input

The screen is built against the contract alone. It shows a seat's `GameView`, lights the territories its `Options` offer and never works out legality for itself (`ui/selection.ts`), sends `Move`s, and tells the events. The rules are well known, so the screen doesn't quote them; events keep their rule ids for tracing a game. It takes a feed (the view, the options, the events, who plays each seat and a way to move), so it never knows where the game lives.

- **How a game reaches the screen.** `components/risk.tsx` opens on the online lobby (`RiskOnline`, on the shared `GameLobby`): create a table or join one by its code, add bots, start with 3 to 6 seats. Once the table starts, the online store's snapshot (`{ snapshot, act }`) is turned into the screen's feed by `components/use-online-feed.ts`: the seat's view and options, the events shown so far, which of them the latest move caused, and who plays each seat.

- **Places are picked on the map; everything else is one status box**: what to do now, the amounts and dice, the latest news, and ending the phase. Cards, the log and the menu are dialogs. The card dialog shows everything public (`public-cards`): every hand's size, the sets traded, the next set's value and the discard pile.
- **Three input methods, each complete on its own**, following Betrayal's model and controller map wherever the two games share a meaning, so a player moving between them finds the same controls doing the same things. Hints follow the last-used input, and before anything is used, the device (none on a touch screen), drawn with the site's prompt glyphs from one map of meanings to controls (`ui/bindings.ts`).
- **One meaning per control.** The d-pad, left stick and arrow keys move the focus: between the territories on offer while a territory is to be picked (spatially, by screen direction, the board wrapping east to west so Alaska's west is Kamchatka), round the status box's buttons while it asks for something, and round a dialog's while one is open. A, Enter and Space press the focus; B and Esc back out. Tab steps through the territories on offer. Unlike Betrayal, whose left stick pans its camera round a reticle, a flat map is navigated by focus, so the right stick pans and the triggers zoom (Betrayal's zoom), View and C show the whole board (Betrayal's Follow, the camera's home), Y and I open the cards (Betrayal's explorer), Menu and M the menu, LB and RB (or the bracket keys) step an amount down and up, and ending the attack or the turn is a hold of X or Backspace, as Betrayal's End turn is. The number keys roll that many dice and B blitzes.
- **Touch and the mouse** pick a territory with a tap or click, a territory reaching half way to its neighbours (a fingertip at least); a drag pans, two fingers pinch, the wheel zooms. Every button is at least 44px.
- **A couch-distance layout** at 1920 wide and up: larger type, markers and buttons (sized in ems, so they scale with the type), a margin clear of the screen's edges, and a focus that shows plainly.
- **Screens step up with their room.** A tablet held upright gives the board the height its shape needs and shows the players below it. From 2400px wide (an ultrawide), panels beside the status box show what narrower screens keep behind a tap: every player's standing as bars, continent control, the exact odds of a planned blitz (`ui/odds.ts`; each target's chance while one is being picked) and the battle log as dice and loss pips (`components/intel.tsx`). From 3600px (32:9) the log gets a column of its own.
- **Each player sees their own seat**: its view and cards, their name and the bots' beside each seat, and "Waiting…" while another seat moves. A phone shows only the events since it loaded the game, so its log starts there.

## Engine plan

The engine is built in stages, each one earning the next:

1. **The rules spec** (`RULES.md`).
2. **A reference engine**: slow and written to be read, one rule at a time, with a test for each rule and invariant checks (army counts, ownership, card counts, turn order) run on every state it produces. Its job is to be obviously correct, not fast. It lives in `engine/`: `invariants.ts` checks `RULES.md`'s INV-* on every step, and `test-kit.ts` holds scripted positions, scripted dice, a model of what the options allow, and the random-legal player the fuzz tests use.
3. **TypeScript for the fast engine and the bots** (`ideas/language-decision.md`, made by an independent review; `ideas/speed-spike.md` holds the partial measurements). One bounded exception: a single hot, pure kernel (position scoring, say) may later move to Rust compiled to WASM, the same binary everywhere with the TypeScript kernel kept as its differential oracle, and only when all three hold: the bot measurably keeps gaining from more work, real bot turns on the server use their allowed time after the TypeScript is tuned, and a profile puts most of a turn inside that kernel. Bots are never written in Rust.
4. **The fast engine**, TypeScript, built when gauntlets need it: flat typed-array state, allocation-free hot loops, benchmarks pinning each kernel's throughput, and differential tests against the reference engine over many seeded games, move for move. It is for simulation only; real games use the reference engine.

## Bots

The bot direction is a rules-based bot. `ideas/risk-bot-ai.md` is the research behind that choice and the survey of stronger methods.

- **One build, laptop to server.** A bot ships as the exact code that was tuned on the laptop. There is no separate "tuning" bot and "shipping" bot to drift apart.
- **Bots run on the server.** Game state lives in the database and a server function plays the bot seats, as in Monopoly, so a phone only shows the game and sends its player's moves. In a real game a bot may take a few seconds per turn: a better decision in a competitive game is worth the wait. Thinking time is spent once per turn, planning it, not on each of a turn's many small decisions.
- **Bots may later think on a player's device.** If a bot's strength keeps climbing with its budget well past what a server function can give it in a few seconds (measured by rating the same bot at several budgets), a modern phone's faster cores may run it instead. The design keeps that open: a bot is a pure function of its seat's view, and its move goes through the same route as any move, named by its decision id. A device could then volunteer an answer, with the server still the single writer, still validating, and still playing the bot itself when no device answers. Parallel search would use Web Workers with their own copies of the game, not shared-memory threads, which need site-wide cross-origin isolation.
- **Strength comes first.** The goal is the most challenging bot; the need for fast gauntlets never caps its strength.
- **Gauntlets must be fast.** Measuring bots takes thousands of games, so iteration speed is set by the engine and the bots' cost per move.
- **More thinking is earned, not assumed.** Whether a bot gets stronger with more work per turn is a measurement, made by rating the same bot at several amounts of work, not an assumption built into the bot interface. Only a bot with an expensive part that measurably gains from more work gets a work budget. Such a budget is then a fixed amount of work (nodes, rollouts, iterations), never a wall-clock limit, so the same seed and position always give the same move on any machine; and gauntlets may screen it at a small budget and confirm a change at its play budget, where rankings can differ.

## Online play

`online/` holds online play, built on Betrayal's design for it (`src/projects/betrayal/design/engine.md`, sections 8 and 9), which records the lessons from Monopoly's sync bugs.

- **One game row, one writer.** A game is a row of `risk_games` (`supabase/risk.sql`): the seats, the status, and the game once it starts, hidden information included. Phones read and subscribe to it with the anon key; `/api/risk` is its only writer, with the service role. Every request goes through one commit routine: read the row, work out the next one, write it only if its version is unchanged, and start again from a fresh read when another write got there first.
- **Answers name their decision.** The game beside the engine's state counts the answers it has taken; that count is the pending decision's id (`DecisionId` in the contract). An answer to a decision no longer pending is rejected, unless it repeats one that landed, which is accepted as it is. So a slow, repeated or reordered answer is harmless, and a phone hears only "accepted, here is the row" or "rejected, here is the row".
- **A phone shows its own moves at once.** Its sync is the shared client sync (`src/shared/lib/client-sync.ts`): the row's game as the server confirmed it, with its player's unconfirmed answers applied on top through the same engine. The random source lives in the state and only one seat acts at a time, so a phone's dice are the server's. The row is reloaded whenever the subscription (re)connects and whenever the tab wakes.
- **The server drives the bots.** A seat's controller is a person or a bot. When a write leaves the decision with a bot, the route plays the bot seats once its response has gone, until a person is to move; a drive stops after a bounded number of decisions and hands on to a fresh request. Any phone that loads a game waiting on a bot nudges the route, which restarts a stalled drive. A drive acts only on the decision pending and names it, so drives that overlap land one answer per decision. Overlapping drives are harmless but duplicate the work; a claim on each decision, so only one drive computes it, is added once bot turns are expensive. A bot's answer goes through the same validation as a person's, so a bot's move could later come from a player's device as well, with the server's drive as the fallback. A bot's illegal move fails the drive loudly, naming the bot, seat, decision and move: a bot is deterministic, so asking it again gives the same move, and playing another bot's move instead would hide the bug while the game plays on.
- **Every accepted answer is logged.** `risk_moves` keeps each answer of each game (its decision, seat, who gave it, the move), written by one database function in the same transaction as the row, so the log and the row never disagree. The row's game keeps its seed and player count, so the log replays any game. The engine's state carries no log, and bots and gauntlets never touch the table.
- **A bot is a pure function of what its seat may see** (`bots/bot.ts`, the one seam every Risk bot plays through, online and in the harness): its view, its options, and the latest events redacted for it. The driver holds the state and hands a bot only that (`online/drive-input.ts`); lint keeps bot code (`bots/`) from importing anything that holds a state, and a test holds a bot's input the same whatever its seat can't see. With no server-only dependencies, a bot could run in a browser worker too.
- **Online play is tested as Monopoly's is: by unit tests, with the database and realtime behind their seams** (the row store, `GameIo`), never against the live project. The database layer itself (`supabase/risk.sql`: the tables, the function that writes a row and its move together, the access rules, realtime on the table) is checked by a manual run against the live project whenever it changes, never by a test in the suite.
- **A page that stops hearing its game is an open bug.** Once, on a freshly started dev server, a page's subscription said it was subscribed and then heard no write again, while the bots' moves reached the database. It hasn't been reproduced since. With `?risk-diag` in the address, a development build traces the subscription's status changes, Realtime's system messages, each row heard and each row the page takes (`riskDiag` in `online/client.ts`); keep it until the cause is found.

## Measuring bots

Every Risk bot lives in `bots/`, measured or played: `bot.ts` is the seam (a seat's view, options, redacted events and a seed in, a move out), `index.ts` the registry the harness seats by label, `online/seat-bots.ts` the bots a table can seat, by the id its row keeps and the name players see, and two baselines that exist so the harness has something real to measure: `random`, a random legal move, and `greedy`, a one-ply player that stacks, attacks where it is bigger and pulls idle armies to the front. Neither is v1, which is designed in `ideas/rule-bot-v1.md`. The harness is `eval/`, built on the shared bot-lab core (`src/shared/lib/bot-lab/`, the root `CLAUDE.md`'s Bot measurement), with `npm run risk:gauntlet`, `risk:ratings` and `risk:verify`.

- **A game is a pure function of its seed and seating.** The runner holds the state and hands each bot only what its seat may see; a bot's seed is derived from the game's through a hash, so nothing about the dice or the deck can be read from it. The worker pool is bit-identical to the single-threaded runner (`risk:verify` proves it, and reports throughput). An illegal move is a bug: the game is an error, counted and printed, never replaced by another move.
- **The turn cap is a draw.** A game past its cap of player-turns has no winner; capped games are discarded from the test and reported as the cap rate, a health metric, as in Monopoly.
- **Two mixtures, every seat, the same luck.** A pairing is a candidate against one opponent at a table of 3 to 6, either **one-in** (the candidate alone among copies of the opponent) or **one-out** (the reverse). Strength inside one population says nothing about another (DORA, `ideas/risk-bot-ai.md`), so a gauntlet tests both. Games come in groups that share a seed, the lone seat rotated through every seat, so each bot meets the same deal, the same seat order and the same random stream from every position (the dice are drawn in the same order, though who rolls which diverges once play does).
- **The test statistic is the candidate side's win, against its par.** Winning is the goal, so each decisive game is one trial: did a candidate seat win. Its hypotheses are an Elo edge per seat, turned into the side's win probability by the Luce model (a seat wins in proportion to its strength), so a candidate holding k of n seats has par k/n, and one margin means the same edge at every table size and in both mixtures. The SPRT is Monopoly's pair of one-sided tests, checked only between whole rotation groups. Treating a group's games as independent overstates their variance (the rotation cancels seat luck), so the test is conservative: its error rates hold, and it plays more games than a paired statistic would. Two identical bots show how much: every group lands on exactly par, yet calling it "even" took over a thousand games. A statistic on each group's total (fishtest's pentanomial idea) is the refinement to make when gauntlet time starts to matter.
- **Placement is reported, not tested.** Finishing order carries more evidence per game, but it rewards outlasting as well as winning, and in Risk those differ: the random bot never wins against greedy, yet finishes above par among greedy copies, because greedy fights itself. So the ladder is the **win Elo** (Bradley–Terry over who beat whom for the win, which recovers the same Luce strengths the SPRT tests), with the **finishing-order Elo** printed beside it and the lone seat's mean place in each pairing.
- **The crown gate is Monopoly's**: the candidate must be better than its base at every table tested and worse than no field member at any. Iterate on the `train` seed stream and confirm on `holdout`.
- **`random` is a sanity floor, never in a field.** Games with no planner in them almost never end (random-only tables run to the cap), so they would cost the most and say nothing. Every real bot must crush it (greedy wins every game against it at 3, 4 and 6 players), and that check is run by naming it: `risk:gauntlet -- greedy --field random`.
- The default indifference margin is 30 Elo per seat; it is provisional until real bots show what edges matter.

**Throughput decides the fast engine.** On the reference engine, greedy games average about 35, 46 and 57 player-turns at 3, 4 and 6 players (500 to 800 moves), at about 54, 31 and 22 games a second on one core and 300, 210 and 150 on a 14-worker pool, which makes a gauntlet pairing seconds to a minute. A profile of 6-player greedy games puts about 90% of the time in the engine: over half in `apply` (most of it the copy of the whole state each move makes), about a quarter building views, and under a tenth in the bot. The speed spike's fast engine ran about 100 times faster per game. So the fast engine is not needed for gauntlets of greedy-cost bots; it is needed when a bot's own planning or rollouts sit on the reference engine, which a profile of that bot will show.
