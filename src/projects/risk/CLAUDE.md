# Risk

The board game Risk: its rules, its engine, and a game screen playable on touch, keyboard and mouse, and a controller, played online with people and bots.

**Risk is online-only for now.** Every game is a table on the server, found in a live lobby that lists them all. Local play (a game passed round one device, or several controllers on one TV) may come later; the screen already plays whatever feed it is given, so it would be a new feed, not a new screen.

**One engine for every real game.** `play-engine.ts` is the single place the engine real games play on is chosen: the reference engine (`engine/`). The server route and a phone applying its own moves ahead of the server both take it from there. There are no stand-in engines.

## Rules

Classic Risk played as RISK: Global Domination (GD) plays it in its ranked games: Fixed card values, Balanced Blitz, and Auto Placement, where the territories are dealt and the starting armies placed at random, so nobody makes a setup decision and a new game begins at the first player's first turn (`newGame` plays the whole setup from the seed; the screen shows the board already set up, its deal playing as it mounts). GD's rules are the ones its players know, and its bots are ported here as the yardstick for ours, so the two games must be the same. `RULES.md` in this folder is the source of truth for the rules: the engine, its tests and the bots follow it, and a question about a rule is settled there, not in code.

Ambiguities are settled the way Betrayal settles them: a note beside the rule, and a resolution labelled with its authority (official, official digital, community, project or unresolved). What GD does is the rule wherever it is known; the rulebooks settle what it leaves open. `RULES.md` opens with the method. Claude makes the project rulings as a game designer would, and brings one to the owner only when it would fork the engine or the architecture.

## The engine contract

`contract.ts` is the boundary between the engine and everything that uses it: the UI, the server route that plays real games, and the bots. They never read the engine's state; they ask for a seat's **view** (what that seat may see), its **options** (the legal moves, grouped so a screen can highlight targets), and **apply** a move, getting back the new state and the **events** that happened, each naming the rule behind it. `map.ts` is the board as data, shared by both sides. The contract is the same whichever language the fast engine ends up in, so the UI and the engine are built in parallel against it.

- **One source of legality.** A move is legal exactly when `apply` accepts it; the options are a convenience a test holds equal to that. A step the rules leave no choice over is taken by the engine, not offered.
- **Hidden information is kept by good faith, as in Betrayal.** The whole state, every hand and the deck order included, lives in one game row every player can read, and the UI shows each seat only its own view and events with other players' drawn cards removed. Players are trusted to look only at what the UI shows them; there is no per-player secret storage.
- **Bots are never trusted: they see only what their seat may legally see.** That is enforced by construction, not good faith: a bot is given its seat's view and options, never the game state, so hidden information is unreachable from bot code. A searching bot that needs whole states to simulate builds them from its view, filling in what it can't see (other hands, the deck order) by sampling what is consistent with it, never by reading the real ones. `sim.ts` builds them: a **world** from a seat's view, its redacted history, the bot's guess at the cards it can't see and the bot's own seed for the dice. The guess is refused unless the seat's knowledge allows it (every hand's size, its own hand, each card once, and a traded card kept out of reach of the draws since it went back below the deck's top); the public facts the view doesn't carry (the round, for the first-turn bonus, and the reinforce phase's bookkeeping) are read exactly from the history. A world offers only views, options and moves on the reference engine, and nothing converts it to or from a real game, so search learns nothing its seat can't see.
- **Moves are sized for a network.** Every move is a round trip to the server, so a whole assault is one move (blitz: attack until captured, or down to a chosen number of armies), alongside the single battle.
- **The reference engine runs real games; the fast engine runs bots.** Real games, on the server and for legality on the phone, use the reference engine, which is TypeScript and obviously correct. The fast engine exists for bot speed, and the differential tests keep the two identical.

## Visual style and map

Risk has its own style, separate from Novelty World's bright, quirky one: a war room, campaign maps, brass and parchment. Its colours come from its own design tokens, scoped to the project. The board is our own, never Hasbro's board art. It is drawn as a graph of territories and borders, made to be read; geographic art may come later.

**The board and the two panels are one composition, designed by hand for each orientation, and a screen changes only its scale.** The board is never stretched, and its scale is never the player's to choose (there is no zoom). A screen wider than it is tall gets the landscape layout: the board as large as fits, centred, with the players panel in its open strip across the top-left and the action panel in its open slot between Africa and Australia; spare height grows the panels to the screen's top and bottom edges, and spare width stays as sea either side, so an ultrawide gains a bigger board, never more panels. A landscape screen whose slots would come out smaller than the panels (a phone held sideways) instead fits the board to its height, whole, with the panels as columns in the gutters either side; a screen too narrow for that shrinks the board to leave the columns their width, and below the smallest scale a fingertip can use (the closest two territories 44px apart) the board pans across between them. A portrait screen puts the panels in bands across the top and bottom and fills the height between them with the board, panning across, so a phone or tablet held upright always pans east and west round the world. The board never pans up and down; across, it wraps as a globe does. What isn't needed for the decision at hand stays behind a tap or appears only where it counts.

**Risk's phone floor is 375×640 CSS pixels in portrait.** It replaces the site-wide 360 width within this project: layouts are designed and checked from 375 wide, and the base Galaxy S models' 360 is below it, where the game still plays but isn't designed for.

**The iPad is a primary device**, in both orientations (iPad about 820×1180, iPad mini 744×1133), with touch as its input. Every iPad browser runs on WebKit, Safari's engine, so a change to the screen is looked at in WebKit as well as Chromium (Playwright's WebKit, headless). A screenshot of the board must wait for its territories to mount and their deal to finish: the deal fades them in, so a shot taken early shows a board that looks empty.

- **The board is a graph with a fixed layout** (`board/graph-layout.ts`), so players learn it: each territory a node, each border an edge, each continent a tinted region, outlined in a seat's colour while that seat holds it all. Continents carry no names or bonus labels: an experienced player knows them, and the bonus shows where it counts, in the reinforcements' news. It is the **clean map**, placed by hand: every continent a compact, regular shape on a 60° lattice, in the places Risk players expect (North America top-left, South America below it, Europe top-middle, Africa below Europe, Asia right, Australia bottom-right), with two slots left open for the landscape screen's panels (`SLOTS`: a strip across the top-left above North America and Europe, and the room between Africa and Australia under Asia). It is changed by editing its positions, never generated and never laid out at runtime; only the continents' regions are drawn at runtime, round the territories as the screen places them. Its one spacing rule: **nothing a territory draws covers another's**, so no lit ring overlaps another ring or another territory's name, and no two names meet. The names' widths are measured in the board's type (`board/label-widths.ts`); a stretch only spreads territories apart, so it keeps the rule. Borders between continents are drawn brighter than those inside one. Alaska–Kamchatka runs across the board's edge: a screen that pans across draws the board again either side, so the border joins the two, and a screen that shows the whole board shows each end running off its edge. Tests hold the layout to `map.ts` (every territory and border, nothing more), with no crossings, no clash at any stretch, every territory clear of the borders it isn't on, the slots clear of every territory, border and region, every territory a fingertip apart on the smallest phone with it panning across only, and a 16:9 desktop showing the whole board.
- **Nodes** show the owner's colour and shape, the armies large in the middle and a short name (`ui/short-names.ts`) always beneath (the board is never shown small enough to drop it); the full name is the tooltip. The territories on offer light in brass, the chosen source and target more strongly, with the borders between them, and a fortifying army's march through its owner's territories is drawn as a lit path.
- **The geographic board's data is kept, unused**, for when the board gets art: `board/generate.py` draws it from Natural Earth's admin-1 provinces (downloaded into a directory of its own, never committed), and only its simplified output, `board/board-data.ts`, is in the repo. It is stylised as Risk boards are: projected with the Bering Strait as the board's edge, magnified smoothly round the crowded places (Europe, Central America, the Malay archipelago, Japan) by lenses that only stretch the plane, archipelagos closed up into chunkier land, small islands dropped and a few scaled up. The generator checks the result against `map.ts`: territories that touch must be neighbours, and neighbours that don't touch get a sea line. Showing it again means drawing it on the same camera as the graph (`ui/camera.ts`).
- **Colour is never the only cue.** Each seat has an ink and a shape, on its territories on the board and beside its name, and is named by its colour (`ui/seats.ts`).
- **The tokens** are `components/theme.ts`, applied on the game's root; the display face is Cinzel.
- **The camera** is `ui/camera.ts`, pure and tested: from the screen's size and the composition (`COMPOSITION` in `board/graph.ts`: the board, the part its territories draw, its slots, the panels' least sizes, the column's least width and the portrait bands' heights) it lays the screen out, the board's place and the panels' boxes, and pans and wraps a board that pans. The panels' least sizes are measured from their content at a six-seat table (the action panel's tallest step, a battle, included), each a few alternatives, since a panel with more width needs less height. Once placed, the board moves only when the player pans it or the screen changes size; the panels' content never moves it.

## The game screen and input

The screen is built against the contract alone. It shows a seat's `GameView`, lights the territories its `Options` offer and never works out legality for itself (`ui/selection.ts`), sends `Move`s, and tells the events. The rules are well known, so the screen doesn't quote them; events keep their rule ids for tracing a game. It takes a feed (the view, the options, the events, who plays each seat and a way to move), so it never knows where the game lives.

- **How a game reaches the screen.** `components/risk.tsx` opens on the online lobby (`RiskOnline`, on the shared `GameLobby`): create a table or open one from the list of every game (take a seat, come back to your own, or watch), add bots, each picked from a list of every seatable bot (a seated bot can be removed, never changed), start with 3 to 6 seats. Once the table starts, the online store's snapshot (`{ snapshot, act }`) is turned into the screen's feed by `components/use-online-feed.ts`: the seat's view and options, the events shown so far, which of them the latest move caused, and who plays each seat.

- **Places are picked on the map; everything else is the action panel** (`components/status-box.tsx`), which fills the box the layout gives it: the latest news, what to do now with going back and ending the phase beside it, and the amounts and dice (a blitz shows its chance of capture, `ui/odds.ts`). Its shape follows its box. In a landscape slot each control has a row of its own (a battle's Back and dice, then its slider, then its blitz; an amount's slider, then its button, with Back before the news; Back always leads its row), and a step short enough to fit under Australia widens along the bottom (the board's `actionWide` slot) so its news stays on one line. In a portrait band the controls share rows where there is room, the slider keeping room to travel; in a sideways phone's column the buttons stack beside a slider that runs up. A choice of an amount (placing, moving in after a capture, marching, and how many a blitz keeps back) has no words at all: the map lights where it goes, and a slider (`Slider` in `components/bits.tsx`) shows how many, its number on the thumb and the most at its end, so "all" is a drag to the end; a tap either side of the thumb nudges it by one, finer than a finger drags, and LB / RB or the bracket keys step it from anywhere. Its button says what it does ("Place"), never the number. Only picking a territory offers to end the phase: beside a fight or an amount on hand, ending would read as ending that one move, so Back comes first. A battle's dice stay buttons, each a move of its own. A selection carries on where a move leads (`nextSelection` in `ui/selection.ts`): placing again keeps the same territory, a battle that didn't capture keeps its pair, and the attack phase starts from the territory last placed on, as a capture's attack goes on from the territory just moved into (or the source it came from), whenever that can attack. Back steps out of it to picking a source, and a target's focus starts beside its source. Only the viewer's own moves carry a selection: another seat's playback offers no options. The players panel (`components/players-panel.tsx`) shows whose turn it is and the phase, every seat in turn order with its colour's name beside its player, so a colour in the news finds its player at any moment, and its territories and armies, and the war room's button with the hand drawn as cards. A bot's name always shows and its rank where its row has room; the full line is the row's tooltip. It is a strip in landscape, a band in portrait and a column beside a sideways phone's board, its roster in as many columns as fit. The war room is the game's one menu: the hand and the trades open to it (a set's worth shows only beside a set the hand can trade), everything public about the cards (`public-cards`: every hand's size drawn as cards, the deck's size, every trade once there is one), and the way to the battle log and out of the game.
- **Other seats' moves play out at the viewer's speed** (`ui/playback.ts`, pure and tested; `components/use-playback.ts`). The server drives bots as fast as the engine goes, so a phone hears a bot's turn in a burst. The screen plays it back an event at a time, each holding the screen for a beat, with the board stepped from the redacted events. Each batch the feed delivers ends with the view it leads to, so the board snaps to the engine's own view at every batch: a blitz's capture, whose initial move-in the events don't carry, and a batch missed while the page wasn't listening, are put right there. The viewer's own moves are never held back; their effects only flash. Nothing is asked of the player until the screen has caught up. A hidden tab plays everything at once. The speed is the viewer's, kept in this browser: a slider in the War room of ten steps, Very slow to Instant, which LB / RB or the bracket keys step while it is open. The default is the third, Slow, where each beat lasts about a second or more, long enough to find where it happened and see what came of it, so a bot's typical turn takes about twenty seconds. Choosing Instant catches up at once. Playback is presentation only: the engine, the server's drive and the bots never wait on it.
- **The map shows each move as it plays** (`graph-board.tsx`'s effects, timed to the beat): armies arriving, a fight's border lit with each side's losses, a capture's burst in the new owner's colour, armies marching border by border, a continent's outline flashing for its new holder, and a fallen seat's skull where its last territory fell.
- **Three input methods, each complete on its own**, following Betrayal's model and controller map wherever the two games share a meaning, so a player moving between them finds the same controls doing the same things. Hints follow the last-used input, and before anything is used, the device (none on a touch screen), drawn with the site's prompt glyphs from one map of meanings to controls (`ui/bindings.ts`).
- **One meaning per control.** The d-pad, left stick and arrow keys move the focus: between the territories on offer while a territory is to be picked (spatially, by screen direction, the board wrapping east to west so Alaska's west is Kamchatka), round the action panel's buttons while it asks for something, and round a dialog's while one is open. A, Enter and Space press the focus, and Enter on the action panel's slider presses its main action; B and Esc back out, and a Back button, with no glyph, shows beside an amount or a fight and while picking where to fortify, where nearly every other territory of the player's is a target, so a tap can't switch the source as it does in an attack. Tab steps through the territories on offer. Unlike Betrayal, whose left stick pans its camera round a reticle, a flat map is navigated by focus, so the right stick (or A and D) pans the board across when it pans, and moving the focus brings a territory into view. Menu and M open the war room, LB and RB (or the bracket keys) step an amount down and up, and X or Backspace ends the attack or the turn at a press: unlike Betrayal's End turn, it is no hold, because a hold to confirm proved more friction than safeguard. The number keys roll that many dice and B blitzes.
- **Touch and the mouse** pick a territory with a tap or click, a territory reaching half way to its neighbours; a drag or the wheel pans the board across when it pans. Every button is at least 44px.
- **A couch-distance layout** on a landscape screen 1800 wide and up whose panels fit at it (the camera picks the panels' type with the layout, so a wide but short window keeps the normal type rather than cutting a seat off): larger type, markers and buttons (sized in ems, so they scale with the type), panels clear of the screen's edges, and a focus that shows plainly.
- **Sound is off in the game until its designs are ready**: the game screen plays nothing and the war room has no mute, while `?sounds` keeps the audition, every sound with its trigger and its parameters. Its cues and `use-risk-sounds.ts` stay; turning it back on is calling that hook from the game screen again, with the select and refused-pick sounds and the war room's mute, and it is built on these rules. **Sound follows the presentation** (`ui/sounds.ts` the designs, `ui/sound-cues.ts` when each plays, on the shared `sound/` module): an event sounds when the screen shows it, so another seat's moves are heard a beat at a time at the viewer's playback speed and the viewer's own at once, and each event sounds once, by its id, so the history a page loads with, a reload and a resync are silent. What the screen presents at once (a burst at the instant speed, a hidden tab catching up) is merged into its most telling two sounds, each once, and a hidden tab sounds only the news that should bring the player back: their turn, victory and defeat. Only news has a sound: dice, a blitz, armies placed, a march, a capture (the viewer's, one of the viewer's lost, or between others), a continent taken, a trade, the viewer's card, an elimination, the viewer's turn, victory and defeat; in the UI only picking a territory and a tap or move refused. Moving the focus and the steps of a move are silent, and a move needs no confirm: its own sound is the confirmation. The palette is the war table's: wooden dice and counters, a field drum, brass calls and a gong. Mute is kept in this browser.
- **Each player sees their own seat**: its view and cards, whose turn it is by name (every seat's player in the players panel, a bot's rank with it where there is room and on its tooltip), and "Waiting…" while another seat moves. A phone shows only the events since it loaded the game, so its log starts there.

## Engine plan

The engine is built in stages, each one earning the next:

1. **The rules spec** (`RULES.md`).
2. **A reference engine**: slow and written to be read, one rule at a time, with a test for each rule and invariant checks (army counts, ownership, card counts, turn order) run on every state it produces. Its job is to be obviously correct, not fast. It lives in `engine/`: `invariants.ts` checks `RULES.md`'s INV-* on every step, and `test-kit.ts` holds scripted positions, scripted dice, a model of what the options allow, and the random-legal player the fuzz tests use.
3. **TypeScript for the fast engine and the bots** (`ideas/language-decision.md`, made by an independent review; `ideas/speed-spike.md` holds the partial measurements). One bounded exception: a single hot, pure kernel (position scoring, say) may later move to Rust compiled to WASM, the same binary everywhere with the TypeScript kernel kept as its differential oracle, and only when all three hold: the bot measurably keeps gaining from more work, real bot turns on the server use their allowed time after the TypeScript is tuned, and a profile puts most of a turn inside that kernel. Bots are never *designed* in Rust: the owner has since decided on a **native Rust port** of the engine and the frozen bots for the measuring harness (`native/`, `ideas/native-port.md`), held exact to the TypeScript by differential tests. The TypeScript stays the source of truth of every rule and every bot; the port follows it.
4. **The fast engine**, TypeScript, built when gauntlets need it: flat typed-array state, allocation-free hot loops, benchmarks pinning each kernel's throughput, and differential tests against the reference engine over many seeded games, move for move. It is for simulation only; real games use the reference engine.

## Bots

The bot direction is a rules-based bot. `ideas/risk-bot-ai.md` is the research behind that choice and the survey of stronger methods.

- **One build, laptop to server.** A bot ships as the exact code that was tuned on the laptop. There is no separate "tuning" bot and "shipping" bot to drift apart.
- **Bots run on the server.** Game state lives in the database and a server function plays the bot seats, as in Monopoly, so a phone only shows the game and sends its player's moves. In a real game a bot may take a few seconds per turn: a better decision in a competitive game is worth the wait. Thinking time is spent once per turn, planning it, not on each of a turn's many small decisions.
- **Bots may later think on a player's device.** If a bot's strength keeps climbing with its budget well past what a server function can give it in a few seconds (measured by rating the same bot at several budgets), a modern phone's faster cores may run it instead. The design keeps that open: a bot is a pure function of its seat's view, and its move goes through the same route as any move, named by its decision id. A device could then volunteer an answer, with the server still the single writer, still validating, and still playing the bot itself when no device answers. Parallel search would use Web Workers with their own copies of the game, not shared-memory threads, which need site-wide cross-origin isolation.
- **A seated bot has a name, a version, an Elo and a rank.** The bots a table can seat (`online/seat-roster.ts`, ranked by `online/seat-bots.ts`) are each a name and version (a rule bot's version, or one of GD's personas). Their Elo is measured, never set by hand: one ratings run fits every seatable bot together, and the result is kept with the bots and refreshed whenever one is added or changed. The rank is a military title given by that order alone: the weakest seatable bot is Recruit, the next Private, and so on up a long ladder to Field Marshal, with most rungs left for bots to come. The gap in Elo between neighbours plays no part, so adding a bot can move the others' ranks. Each rank has an insignia, our own drawing in the war room's brass (`components/insignia.tsx`, `RankInsignia`): stripes for the enlisted ranks, a warrant's bar, bars, leaves and wings for the officers, stars for the generals and crossed batons for the field marshal, with the rank's name as its accessible name and tooltip; `?insignia` shows the whole set at its real sizes. The lobby shows each bot's insignia, its title spelled out beside it where there is room, and its strength among the seatable bots as rising bars, and its bot picker lists them strongest first, starting on the strongest. A row that names a bot no longer seated fails loudly rather than seating another in its place, so retiring a bot means dealing with the rows that seat it.
- **GD's Expert bots stay seatable.** The four personas RISK: Global Domination deals at its Expert difficulty (Defensive, Continental, Aggressive, Stacker) are ported faithfully, strengths and flaws alike, as the yardstick for ours, and kept as seatable bots whatever their measured rank.
- **Strength comes first.** The goal is the most challenging bot; the need for fast gauntlets never caps its strength.
- **Gauntlets must be fast.** Measuring bots takes thousands of games, so iteration speed is set by the engine and the bots' cost per move.
- **More thinking is earned, not assumed.** Whether a bot gets stronger with more work per turn is a measurement, made by rating the same bot at several amounts of work, not an assumption built into the bot interface. Only a bot with an expensive part that measurably gains from more work gets a work budget. Such a budget is then a fixed amount of work (nodes, rollouts, iterations), never a wall-clock limit, so the same seed and position always give the same move on any machine; and gauntlets may screen it at a small budget and confirm a change at its play budget, where rankings can differ.

## Online play

`online/` holds online play, built on Betrayal's design for it (`src/projects/betrayal/design/engine.md`, sections 8 and 9), which records the lessons from Monopoly's sync bugs.

- **The lobby lists every game, from the rows, not by code.** As in Monopoly, the lobby is every row of `risk_games` (gathering, in play and over), most recently active first, kept live by one subscription to the whole table; the rows are the only source of truth, so a player always finds their own game again. The list is built from a load and the writes and deletes heard since, each game kept at the newest version seen (`online/lobby.ts`), so there is no refetch and no debounce. Tapping a game takes a seat at a gathering table with one free (the route's `join`), comes back to a game the player is seated in, and otherwise watches (`openAction`). Holding a game (as in Monopoly's lobby), or pressing Delete on it from the keyboard, opens a confirm screen, then deletes it for everyone through the route's `delete` (its move log cascades; the table's access rules stay read-only); a page at a deleted game hears the delete and says the table is gone. A gathering table nobody else has joined doesn't outlive its maker: pressing Leave while they are its only person (bots don't count) deletes it through the route's `leave`, the delete conditional on the row's version so a guest joining at that moment keeps it. Anyone else leaving, or anyone leaving a started game, keeps their seat to come back to. Closing the tab or losing the connection is not a leave: nothing on the page or the server hears it, so such a table stays until someone deletes it. Each entry is shown in symbols: its status, each seat's mark with the player's name or a bot, the open seats of a gathering table, and when it was last active. Row ids are internal and never shown; the page keeps the game it is at in the address (`?game=`) so a reload comes back to it, but nothing offers the address for sharing.
- **One game row, one writer.** A game is a row of `risk_games` (`supabase/risk.sql`): the seats, the status, and the game once it starts, hidden information included. Phones read and subscribe to it with the anon key; `/api/risk` is its only writer, with the service role. Every request goes through one commit routine: read the row, work out the next one, write it only if its version is unchanged, and start again from a fresh read when another write got there first.
- **Answers name their decision.** The game beside the engine's state counts the answers it has taken; that count is the pending decision's id (`DecisionId` in the contract). An answer to a decision no longer pending is rejected, unless it repeats one that landed, which is accepted as it is. So a slow, repeated or reordered answer is harmless, and a phone hears only "accepted, here is the row" or "rejected, here is the row".
- **A phone shows its own moves at once.** Its sync is the shared client sync (`src/shared/lib/client-sync.ts`): the row's game as the server confirmed it, with its player's unconfirmed answers applied on top through the same engine. The random source lives in the state and only one seat acts at a time, so a phone's dice are the server's. The row is reloaded whenever the subscription (re)connects and whenever the tab wakes.
- **The server drives the bots.** A seat's controller is a person or a bot. When a write leaves the decision with a bot, the route plays the bot seats once its response has gone, until a person is to move; a drive stops after a bounded number of decisions and hands on to a fresh request. Any phone that loads a game waiting on a bot nudges the route, which restarts a stalled drive. A drive acts only on the decision pending and names it, so drives that overlap land one answer per decision. Overlapping drives are harmless but duplicate the work; a claim on each decision, so only one drive computes it, is added once bot turns are expensive. A bot's answer goes through the same validation as a person's, so a bot's move could later come from a player's device as well, with the server's drive as the fallback. A bot's illegal move fails the drive loudly, naming the bot, seat, decision and move: a bot is deterministic, so asking it again gives the same move, and playing another bot's move instead would hide the bug while the game plays on.
- **A person has an Elo, kept on their own device** (`online/rating.ts`, stored by `online/player-elo.ts` in the project's local storage beside the profile), on the bots' scale (greedy = 1000), starting at 1000. It is the same win Elo as the bots' ladder: a seat's chance of winning a table is its strength over the table's (γ = 10^(Elo/400), the Luce model), and a game moves the rating by K × (won − expected), K = 32 (reasoned in `rating.ts`: a settled spread of about 50 Elo, a newcomer at their level in a few dozen games). A seat carries the Elo its player brought when they took it, so every device rates the table the same way: bots at their measured Elo, people at the Elo on their seat (a seat taken without one counts as a newcomer's). The device scores a game once its seat's game is over, when it is eliminated, or when it wins and the server has the game over; the ledger keeps each scored game by its row id, so a reload or a second look never scores it twice, and keeps what it did to the rating, which the action panel shows (from, to and the change) for the rest of the game. A game is scored only on a device that sees its end: one never opened again is never scored. The lobby shows the player's name and Elo above the games, and a gathering table each person's seat Elo where it shows each bot's.
- **Every accepted answer is logged.** `risk_moves` keeps each answer of each game (its decision, seat, who gave it, the move), written by one database function in the same transaction as the row, so the log and the row never disagree. The row's game keeps its seed and player count, so the log replays any game. The engine's state carries no log, and bots and gauntlets never touch the table.
- **A bot is a pure function of what its seat may know** (`bots/bot.ts`, the one seam every Risk bot plays through, online and in the harness): its view, its options, the game's whole history redacted for it (every event since the game began, so it can remember anything its seat saw without keeping state of its own), and which seats people play. The driver holds the state and hands a bot only that (`online/drive-input.ts`): it replays the history from the seed and the move log once per drive, failing loudly if the replay doesn't reach the row's game, and grows it by each answer it lands, so the cost is per request, not per decision. What bots read from the history (this turn's attacks, captures and placements) is derived once, in `bots/history.ts`. Lint keeps the secrets out of bot code (`bots/`), any real game's state and anything that deals a game from a seed: from Risk it may import only the contract, the map, the cards, the blitz odds and `sim`, and the run-time ways round an import (a dynamic import, the global object, Node's process and built-in modules, the Function constructor) are banned. A test holds a bot's input, history included, the same whatever its seat can't see. With no server-only dependencies, a bot could run in a browser worker too.
- **Online play is tested as Monopoly's is: by unit tests, with the database and realtime behind their seams** (the row store, `GameIo`, `LobbyIo`), never against the live project. The database layer itself (`supabase/risk.sql`: the tables, the function that writes a row and its move together, the access rules, realtime on the table) is checked by a manual run against the live project whenever it changes, never by a test in the suite.
- **A page that stops hearing its game is an open bug.** Once, on a freshly started dev server, a page's subscription said it was subscribed and then heard no write again, while the bots' moves reached the database. It hasn't been reproduced since. With `?risk-diag` in the address, a development build traces the subscription's status changes, Realtime's system messages, each row heard and each row the page takes (`riskDiag` in `online/client.ts`); keep it until the cause is found.

## Measuring bots

Every Risk bot lives in `bots/`, measured or played: `bot.ts` is the seam (a seat's view, options, redacted history, which seats people play and a seed in, a move out), `index.ts` the registry the harness seats by label, `online/seat-roster.ts` the bots a table can seat, by the id its row keeps and the name players see (apart from their ranking, so the ratings run that measures a newly seated bot can read it before the bot has an Elo), and two baselines that exist so the harness has something real to measure: `random`, a random legal move (the harness's alone: it never wins, so no table seats it), and `greedy`, a one-ply player that stacks, attacks where it is bigger and pulls idle armies to the front. **A new version may come from anywhere.** It may be written from scratch, copied from any existing bot (a GD persona included), combine pieces of several, retune one, or try something entirely different (a search bot, say): its author reads the Elo ladder, the existing bots' code and their games, and takes the next version in whatever direction the evidence points, never only as a tweak to the last one. Each version is a folder of its own in `bots/` (`rule-v1`, `rule-v2`, and on) with a design doc in `ideas/` that says where it came from and why; it may import code from a frozen version (a seated version is frozen, so what it shares can't drift), as a convenience, never as a constraint. `ideas/bot-log.md` records every measurement, the changes tried and dropped included; `risk:review` replays any measured game as a readable record, a rule bot's plans with their odds and scores included, which is how v2's changes were found, and `risk:trace` follows a game's trajectory turn by turn (attacks, captures, trades, losses, the armies on the board and the biggest stack), an online game's from its move log too, and surveys a seating's game lengths, capped games and shape (the board's armies at turn 100, losses over income; `ideas/stalled-games.md`, `ideas/rule-bot-v6.1.md`). `risk:agreement` asks a base bot and candidates the same positions (an online game's or the base's own games') and reports how often each decides as the base does, by decision kind (`ideas/rule-bot-v6.1.md`). The harness is `eval/`, built on the shared bot-lab core (`src/shared/lib/bot-lab/`, the root `CLAUDE.md`'s Bot measurement), with `npm run risk:gauntlet`, `risk:ratings` and `risk:verify`, and `risk:opponents`, which scores a rule bot's opponent model against the turns it predicts (`ideas/rule-bot-v4.md`).

- **A game is a pure function of its seed and seating.** The runner holds the state and hands each bot only what its seat may see; a bot's seed is derived from the game's through a hash, so nothing about the dice or the deck can be read from it. The worker pool is bit-identical to the single-threaded runner (`risk:verify` proves it, and reports throughput). A gauntlet plays all its pairings at once, each its own SPRT over its own rounds, and a ladder all its matchups, so the pool is never left waiting on one table's slowest game; games a pairing had in flight past its stop point are discarded, so the report is the one a pairing at a time would give. An illegal move is a bug: the game is an error, counted and printed, never replaced by another move.
- **The turn cap is a draw.** A game past its cap of player-turns has no winner; capped games are discarded from the test and reported as the cap rate, a health metric, as in Monopoly.
- **Two mixtures, every seat, the same luck.** A pairing is a candidate against one opponent at a table of 3 to 6, either **one-in** (the candidate alone among copies of the opponent) or **one-out** (the reverse). Strength inside one population says nothing about another (DORA, `ideas/risk-bot-ai.md`), so a gauntlet tests both. Games come in groups that share a seed, the lone seat rotated through every seat, so each bot meets the same deal, the same seat order and the same random stream from every position (the dice are drawn in the same order, though who rolls which diverges once play does).
- **The test statistic is the candidate side's win, against its par.** Winning is the goal, so each decisive game is one trial: did a candidate seat win. Its hypotheses are an Elo edge per seat, turned into the side's win probability by the Luce model (a seat wins in proportion to its strength), so a candidate holding k of n seats has par k/n, and one margin means the same edge at every table size and in both mixtures. The SPRT is Monopoly's pair of one-sided tests, checked only between whole rotation groups. Treating a group's games as independent is close to exact between different bots: their games diverge too soon for the rotation to cancel much seat luck, and a group's total varied 0.88 to 1.18 times as much as independent games would (`ideas/sim-speed.md`), so a paired statistic (fishtest's pentanomial idea) would save at most about a tenth of the games and isn't used. Only near-identical bots differ: two copies land every group on par, yet calling them "even" takes over a thousand games.
- **Placement is reported, not tested.** Finishing order carries more evidence per game, but it rewards outlasting as well as winning, and in Risk those differ: the random bot never wins against greedy, yet finishes above par among greedy copies, because greedy fights itself. So the ladder is the **win Elo** (Bradley–Terry over who beat whom for the win, which recovers the same Luce strengths the SPRT tests), with the **finishing-order Elo** printed beside it and the lone seat's mean place in each pairing.
- **There are bots and the rankings, nothing else.** No version is a champion or a parent: the Elo ladder (`bots/ratings.json`, one ratings run over every seatable bot, greedy at 1000) is the only standing of any bot, and it sets the lobby's ranks and default. What to build next is Claude's call, made from the ladder, the bots' code and their games. The gauntlet is an instrument, not a gate: its per-table verdicts (better, worse or even against a chosen opponent, on `train` seeds, confirmed on `holdout`) are the fast way to tell whether an idea pays while iterating. **A version that has run the full suite and clears its seating bar is seated at once**, without waiting on the owner: the suite is its gauntlets on train and holdout seeds and the ladder with every seatable bot, and the bar is the one its design doc fixes before the runs. Seating adds it to the seatable bots and reruns the ratings. A seated version stays seated when a newer one beats it, so players keep every step of strength. GD's four personas stay seatable whatever they rate.
- **`random` is a sanity floor, never in a field.** Games with no planner in them almost never end (random-only tables run to the cap), so they would cost the most and say nothing. Every real bot must crush it (greedy wins every game against it at 3, 4 and 6 players), and that check is run by naming it: `risk:gauntlet -- greedy --field random`.
- **rule-v4 and rule-v5 are not in a suite's field.** A new bot's gauntlets name their field, and it is rule-v6 (or what replaces it), GD Defensive, GD Stacker and greedy: v4 and v5 stall a bot-only table worst (`ideas/stalled-games.md`), so their 6-player pairings were the slowest of every suite while measuring an edge over bots v6 already beats. They stay seated and on the ladder. A suite already fixed with them in its field (v7's) runs as written.
- The default indifference margin is 30 Elo per seat; it is provisional until real bots show what edges matter.
- **Seatable bots' Elo** is `bots/ratings.json`: one ratings run over every seatable bot, greedy anchored at 1000, rerun whenever a seatable bot is added or changed (a test fails until it is). The rank is read from it by order.
- **A seated bot is frozen, and a test holds it so** (`bots/frozen.test.ts`): every seatable bot's moves over two fixed games are pinned by a fingerprint. Each game is kept as its moves (`bots/__fixtures__/frozen/`, by `eval/replay.ts`), the fingerprint is taken from them, and the bot is asked its own decisions again a slice at a time, each slice a test of its own, with the game replayed from the kept moves up to it. A test that plays games is cut this way, or into smaller games, so it takes well under a second alone: the full suite runs a test about four times slower than it runs alone, and one that takes 1.3 seconds alone misses the 5-second timeout there. Work on a seated bot's speed (a cache, a faster loop) must leave them bit-identical; a change to its play is a new version. Float results must come out the same too, so a speed change keeps the order of every sum and product.
- **The ladder plays to a precision, not a count** (`eval/ratings.ts`). Its pairs play a seed group at a time, every table and both ways round alike, and after each group one win-Elo fit over every group so far says how precisely it knows each pair's gap. A pair stops once its gap's standard error is within margin / z(1 − α): with the gauntlet's 30 Elo a seat and α = 0.05, 18.2 Elo, so any two bots a margin apart are ordered right with probability at least 95%, the same edge and error rate the gauntlet's verdicts use. The error is the fit's sandwich (Huber–White) error, not its textbook one: the fit counts a lone win among n − 1 copies as n − 1 comparisons, which would overstate the evidence up to n − 1 times, and the sandwich counts each game once and reads the observed outcomes, so a field that isn't one strength scale shows as the extra spread it causes (`eloGapVariance` in `bot-lab/elo.ts`). Precision comes from the whole fit, not from a pair's own games: a lopsided matchup (a weak bot alone among strong ones) says little about its gap, but the pair stops as soon as the bots between them pin it, rather than playing to a cap. A pair that never comes into focus (a bot that never wins) stops at `--max-groups` (100). Every pair of a bot keeps all its tables and both mixtures at equal weight, so the ladder measures the same average over populations it always did; playing only the most informative matchups would be cheaper, but would change what is measured. Simulated on the seatable ladder's real matchup rates: from nothing, 8,700 games instead of 198,000 (3 to 7 groups a matchup), gap errors 19 Elo RMS against 12 at 100 groups, of which about 11 is the fit's own small-sample bias shared by both, and 7 of 1,560 pairs at least 30 apart misordered; v7 (+150 a seat) joining the cached ladder, 3,100 games and a thirteenth of the compute, its gaps 17 Elo RMS; the 5-player budget ladder of v6 and v7 at 2 and 8 replies, 1,350 games instead of 3,000.
- **The ladder remembers its matchups** (`eval/ratings-cache.json`, committed). A matchup keeps its first seed groups, each group's record apart (its tally and its Elo comparisons summed, which fit exactly as its games do), with a hash of the code its games ran on: both bots' modules and everything they import, and the engine, runner and dealing (`eval/ratings-cache.ts`). A record answers only while that code is unchanged, so a new bot costs only its own matchups, and a gauntlet's field games are played once, not on every run. Because it holds groups, not a stop point, it only saves playing: a ladder that finds a matchup's groups there reads what it would have played, so it rates the same whichever earlier run left them, stricter or looser. Records from before the ladder played to a precision summed a fixed 100 groups and can't say what their first groups were, so the cache was emptied when it changed; the next seatable ratings run replays the few groups each old pair needs. Any edit in a bot's import closure, a comment included, replays its matchups; `--fresh` replays everything. A registered bot names its module in `BOT_MODULES` (`bots/index.ts`).
- **Long runs play on GitHub Actions** (`docs/actions.md`): the gauntlet and the ladder, with the same arguments, in rounds split over up to twenty machines, and a report identical to a local run's, which a test holds them to. A gauntlet's undecided pairings play more games each round until their tests decide, a ladder's undecided pairs more groups until their gaps are known, and one pairing's games may be split across machines. The results come back as an artifact, which `risk:import` brings into the checkout.
- **`risk:tune`** proposes a rule bot's preferences by SNES (the shared `bot-lab/snes.ts`) against a fixed field of one or more opponents, its fitness the candidate's worst table (an opponent at a table size), so an edge at one table can't hide a loss at another. Against several opponents a baseline bot plays the same seeds, and each table is read as the candidate's edge over the baseline's score there: preferences tuned against one strong bot can give back to weak ones what every strong bot beats them by, and a raw worst table would always be the strongest opponent's. Its candidates are seated by tuned labels (`rule-v1~{"borderWeight":0.7}`), which may move preferences and the work knob, never the opponent model. It proposes; the gauntlet and the ladder measure.
- **`risk:record`** records positions for fitting an evaluation to outcomes (`eval/positions.ts`), the data for asking whether outcomes predict better from rule-v6's value, a fit on its terms, or a small network on them and the raw board. It plays 5-player games from a mixed field (by default v6, v5, v4 and GD's four personas, a field it takes as an argument) on the harness's runner and worker pool, seated in rotation so every bot sits in every seat, each game a pure function of its seed (`hybrid-data-<n>`) and seating. For every seat at the end of each of its turns it keeps one record: the board as that seat sees it (owners and armies, every hand's size, its own cards, who is alive, whether the turn earned a card), its turn order, and v6's evaluation of it term by term with v6's own settings at that table, plus every seat's power. The board is the one v6's planner scores for a turn's end: after the fortify, the turn's card not yet drawn. It is built from the seat's view alone, as a bot builds its board, and a test holds it the same whatever the seat can't see. Each record carries its game's outcome, the winner or capped; capped games are kept, flagged, and every seat's record at every turn, so later targets (out before my next turn, my share some turns on) are derived offline. The output is gzipped JSON lines in `eval/positions/`, never committed, one file per run of consecutive games, so a recording splits across machines by shard and the files are simply read together. `risk:features` turns a recording into rule-v8's training set by reading every board through v8's own feature function (`bots/rule-v8/features.ts`), so the trainer (`bots/rule-v8/train/train.py`) never computes a feature itself (`ideas/rule-bot-v8.md`).
- **`risk:record -- --teacher rule-v7`** records a teacher, for distilling v7's placement search into a cheap student (`eval/distill.ts`, `ideas/distilled-v7.md`). It plays 5-player games with v7 in one or more seats (`--teachers`) among a mixed field (by default v6, v5 and GD's Stacker and Aggressive), seated in rotation, each game a pure function of its seed (`distill-v7-<n>`), on the same pool and shards as the position recorder. At each turn-start search v7 runs over more than one candidate it keeps one record: v6's candidate plans (v6's own choice first), each with its placement, its success chance, v6's value and the two boards v6 valued it on (the turn's end if the plan takes everything and if it falls short in its likeliest way) with v6's breakdown of each, and v7's mean score for every candidate and its pick. v7 plays through the recorder's own copy of its control flow, on rule-v7's exported search, so each search runs once; a test holds it to v7's moves, a record to v7's own search, and a record the same whatever the seat can't see. Any search setting may be the teacher's (`rule-v7~{"replies":32}`), since the teacher is paid for once. `risk:features -- --teacher` reads every candidate's boards through v8's feature function, a row per candidate grouped by decision, and `bots/rule-v8/train/distill.py` trains a small network to rank a decision's candidates as v7's scores do (a listwise loss, held out by game) and reports the kill test: how often v7 overrules v6, the student's agreement with v7 against always taking v6's choice and taking v6's highest value, and the share of v7's edge in its own scores the student keeps. The student that plays is `student-v1` (`bots/student-v1/`, `ideas/student-v1.md`): v6 with the turn's placement chosen among v7's candidates by the network, its rows built by the same functions as the training rows; `npm run risk:student-weights` turns the trainer's export into a weight set it plays (`student-v1~{"weights":"<name>"}`).

**Throughput decides the fast engine.** On the reference engine, greedy games average about 35, 46 and 57 player-turns at 3, 4 and 6 players (500 to 800 moves), at about 54, 31 and 22 games a second on one core and 300, 210 and 150 on a 14-worker pool, which makes a gauntlet pairing seconds to a minute. A profile of 6-player greedy games puts about 90% of the time in the engine: over half in `apply` (most of it the copy of the whole state each move makes), about a quarter building views, and under a tenth in the bot. The speed spike's fast engine ran about 100 times faster per game. So the fast engine is not needed for gauntlets of greedy-cost bots; it is needed when a bot's own planning or rollouts sit on the reference engine, which a profile of that bot will show.
