# Risk bot: which AI method fits

Research, not a decision: the investigation behind the Risk project's bot. The bot direction itself, a rules-based bot, is decided in the project's `CLAUDE.md`; this survey stays as the evidence for that choice and for anything stronger later. The question it answers: which game-AI method suits a Risk bot (3 to 6 players, dice, alliances, playable in the browser), built by Claude, trained on the owner's laptop (Ryzen 7 7840U plus an RTX 4070 eGPU) in about a week of overnight runs, aiming for expert play. The sources are linked inline; the stage-gate thresholds are proposals to retune once the engine's speed is measured.

## The answer: build a searching Risk bot, not AlphaZero

Expert play against good humans is not a goal this budget can promise. No Risk AI has ever shown it, at any compute level, and the best evidence says compute is not what stopped them. The strongest published Risk agent, Carr's 2020 "D.A.D.", trained its evaluator in **about one GPU-hour** on a GTX 1080. Combined with a turn-level search over exact battle odds, it won **35.3% of 6-player games against the hardest Lux Delux bots**, where chance is 16.7% ([Carr 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06355)). Carr never tested it against humans. The only Risk system with any human result, Wolf's 2005 hand-planned bot with TD-tuned weights, "won regularly versus human beginners" and only occasionally beat experienced players ([Blomqvist 2020, summarising Wolf](https://forums.triplea-game.org/assets/uploads/files/1637032761173-fulltext01.pdf)). Every from-scratch self-play attempt at Risk failed or plateaued, including one on a 100-core server. On the merits, the best-suited method for this laptop is therefore **search at play time over a cheaply learned evaluation**, built in this order: a fast engine with exact attack-odds tables; hand-written heuristic bots tuned by evolutionary search; a Carr-style end-of-turn search; then a small graph network value function trained by TD(λ) and refined by self-play. Fifty-six overnight hours is more compute than that path needs. What the week can realistically deliver is a bot that measurably beats every hand-written Risk bot in 1v1 and 6-player play, runs in a browser, and is probably strong against casual and intermediate humans. "Expert against good humans" stays a stretch goal that only human games can verify, and it is weakest in free-for-all, where alliances and kingmaking decide games and no Risk human-game data exists to learn them from.

## Risk breaks the assumptions behind the famous methods

Risk is hard in a specific way, and that way decides which methods fit. One turn is a chain of decisions: card trade, army placement, an unlimited series of attacks, then fortification. Wolf estimated the branching factor of a whole turn at **10^33 to 10^85**, against about 31 for chess and 250 for Go, with placement the dominant term. Restructuring placement brings it down to around 10^6 ([Blomqvist 2020](https://forums.triplea-game.org/assets/uploads/files/1637032761173-fulltext01.pdf)). Games are long: Carr's 6-player Lux games averaged about 100 player-turns ([Carr 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06355)), and an untrained learning agent once dragged a game out to **21,324 turns** ([Lütolf 2013](https://ai.dmi.unibas.ch/papers/theses/luetolf-bachelor-13.pdf)). Dice add chance after every attack. With 3 to 6 players the game is general-sum, so alliances, ganging up on the leader and kingmaking all matter ([Gnecco Heredia & Cazenave](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~cazenave/papers/RiskConferencePaper.pdf)).

Two of these properties turn out to be cheap, and the attempts that failed mostly failed by not exploiting them. Hidden information is mild: card counts are public and only card types are hidden. Dice are exactly computable: a battle is a small Markov chain. A single 3-versus-2 roll costs the defender two armies with probability 2890/7776 (37.2%), and a 5-versus-3 battle fought to the end goes to the attacker 76.9% of the time ([bin.re, implementing Osborne's chain](https://bin.re/blog/probability-of-winning-battles-in-risk/)). Carr precomputed every battle's terminal distribution into a lookup table and planned against a single outcome at a "Risky" quantile: 0.3 worked best, higher made the bot over-optimistic and lower made it passive. After each real roll he re-planned ([Carr 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06355)). Gnecco Heredia and Cazenave sampled dice inside MCTS instead. Their attack-phase learning never got off the ground, and they name the missing outcome table as a main reason ([Expert Iteration for Risk](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~cazenave/papers/RiskConferencePaper.pdf)).

The famous methods carry a 2-player zero-sum assumption that Risk violates. In AlphaZero and MuZero it is structural: a scalar value whose sign flips at every backup. In CFR, NFSP, PSRO, R-NaD and ReBeL it lives in the theory, whose Nash guarantees hold only for two players. In Elo and exploitability it lives in the metric. AlphaZero-style search extends mechanically to N players with a value vector and max-n backups, where each mover maximises its own entry ([Petosa & Balch 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13012)). The guarantees do not survive the extension. With 3+ players there is no single optimal policy to converge to, and self-play optimises against copies of itself. Diplomacy, the closest well-studied analogue, shows the cost. DORA, trained by self-play from scratch, was superhuman in 2-player Diplomacy, but in the 7-player game it converged to "a strategy incompatible with agents bootstrapped from human data," and its authors concluded "self play alone may be insufficient" ([Bakhtin et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02924)). On this axis Risk looks like Diplomacy, not poker.

## Every method family, judged against this laptop and this goal

The comparison below weighs each family on Risk's properties and the owner's limits. It does not weigh popularity. Three hardware facts shape it. The Ryzen 7 7840U is a 15 to 28 W mobile chip, so it will throttle on sustained all-core overnight runs. The eGPU sits on a Thunderbolt link with roughly PCIe x4 bandwidth and higher per-call latency; that penalty is inferred from the interface, not measured. And the browser has to run the play-time search on a phone. Together they reward designs where the GPU trains a small network on large stored batches and the search runs on CPU threads. NNUE in chess is the existence proof: a small, sparsely updated network that runs millions of times per second on CPUs, worth at least +80 Elo over Stockfish's classical evaluation ([Chessprogramming wiki](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Stockfish_NNUE)). The same constraints punish AlphaZero-style self-play, which calls the network once per search node.

| Family | Good at | Bad at, for Risk | Verdict for ~56 h on this laptop |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Search with hand evaluation** (MCTS with max-n vectors, expectimax over bucketed battle outcomes, Carr-style end-of-turn search) | Needs no training. Uses exact odds directly. Runs in WASM. Fails visibly (visit counts, missed moves). | Only as good as its evaluation. Full-turn trees explode, so placement must be pruned or chunked. | **Best-suited core.** Proven: 35.3% in 6-player games against strong Lux bots. |
| **Small learned value function by TD(λ)** (TD-Gammon, Carr) | Cheap. One loss to watch. Learns strategic trade-offs a linear evaluation cannot. Tiny enough for the browser. | Needs a fast engine and decent games to learn from. A linear model blamed unrelated events and failed to improve (Lütolf). | **Plausible and recommended** as the evaluator inside search. |
| **Evolutionary tuning** (CMA-ES or genetic search over heuristic weights; RHEA) | Embarrassingly parallel on CPU. Readable weights. Diagnosable fitness curves. Recommended by Lütolf. | Capped by the heuristic's feature set. No published Risk success. | **Plausible.** The right first optimiser for heuristic bots. |
| **AlphaZero / Expert Iteration from scratch** | Strong where the value can be learned and the engine is fast. A coding agent built a Connect Four pipeline in 3 hours. | Every Risk attempt failed or plateaued. Calls the network per node, which the eGPU link punishes. | **Implausible** as the main path. A low-simulation Gumbel ExIt step is an optional late stage. |
| **Model-free PPO / IMPALA self-play** | Easy to scale; masking and autoregressive heads handle structured actions. | Sample-hungry over long horizons. No lookahead for tactics. Fails silently through implementation details. | **Plausible** for "beats scripted bots"; implausible for expert play. |
| **MuZero variants** | Plan without known rules. | Learning a model of rules you already have exactly is wasted work. Most moving parts of any family. | **Not worth it.** Borrow its ideas (afterstates, chance nodes) inside search instead. |
| **Game-theoretic equilibrium** (CFR / Pluribus, R-NaD / DeepNash, PSRO, NFSP) | Unexploitable play in 2-player zero-sum games; Pluribus beat pros at 6-player poker. | Guarantees vanish for 3+ players. No known abstraction tames Risk's turns. "Balanced against everyone" is wrong when you should coordinate against the leader. | **Implausible and ill-fitting.** Keep only PSRO's payoff-table evaluation. |

| **LLM agents** | No training. Can reason about the game in words and could add diplomacy chat. | Seconds of latency per call across hundreds of sub-decisions. Costs money per game. Needs a server key. Results driven by execution reliability, not strategy. | **Poor move engine.** At most an optional chat or plan-selection layer. |

### Search fits Risk's shape, and the multiplayer search literature says which kind

Among multiplayer search schemes, Best-Reply Search beats max-n by 65 to 88% in alpha-beta settings across Chinese Checkers, Focus and Rolit ([Schadd & Winands 2011](https://dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/m.winands/documents/BestReplySearch.pdf)). That advantage comes from alpha-beta pruning, which Risk's dice and branching factor largely erase. Inside MCTS, by contrast, **the max-n tree structure is clearly best**, and MCTS-max-n "gets relatively stronger as thinking time increases in every game" tested ([Nijssen & Winands](https://dke.maastrichtuniversity.nl/m.winands/documents/Multi_Overview.pdf)). BRS also lets opponents "pass", producing positions that cannot occur. That flaw hurt in Rolit, where every move reshapes the board, and would hurt more in Risk, where a single opponent turn does. Every Risk search agent found used max-n-style value vectors ([Gibson et al. 2010](https://cspages.ucalgary.ca/~richard.zhao1/publications/2010aiide-UCT.pdf); [Carr 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06355)).

Within a turn, the evidence favours pruning to plausible end-of-turn states over searching every atomic decision. Carr placed armies only on territories that border enemies, in groups of three on at most two territories, and searched breadth-first. Depth-first search "regularly miss[ed] obvious moves" ([Carr 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06355)). Gibson's UCT drafting lifted Lux's Quo bot from about 42% to about 77% wins in 3-player games. It ran 3,000 simulations per pick in under a second on a 2010 Core 2 Duo, which shows how much the opening territory draft matters and how cheap searching it is ([Gibson et al. 2010](https://cspages.ucalgary.ca/~richard.zhao1/publications/2010aiide-UCT.pdf)). Combinatorial-bandit search (NaiveMCTS) handles joint actions in the 10-million range at about 1,000 playouts per decision ([Ontañón 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.04805)), but on larger action spaces it stops scaling without action abstractions ([Moraes et al. 2018](https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~santanad/papers/2018/moraesMLN18.pdf)). It is a candidate for placement, not a full-turn solution. Carr's search had two costs. It ran in Python and sometimes took 1 to 2 minutes per turn. And it needed hand patches: a territory-count evaluation to finish off nearly dead opponents, and a cap on a feature that once led it to pile "hundreds of units in Australia" ([Carr 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06355)). Both costs are engineering, and both are fixable.

### Self-play RL failed at Risk for reasons a week does not fix

The Risk self-play record is uniformly poor. Blomqvist ran Expert Iteration on a computing cluster: 1v1 with a neutral player, open fixed cards, automatic trades, 12 to 14 thousand games per iteration, 10,000-simulation MCTS experts. Validation loss went flat after 6 iterations. Network 1 versus network 6 scored 49.5%, so later networks added nothing. The value head degenerated into a winner classifier: agents using it **lost 76 to 89% to agents that did not** ([Blomqvist 2020](https://forums.triplea-game.org/assets/uploads/files/1637032761173-fulltext01.pdf)). Gnecco Heredia and Cazenave used a graph network and 4,000-simulation experts on a **100-core EPYC with no GPU**. After 100 iterations their policy was **worse than random** in the main game, though it learned the draft on a toy 6-territory map ([Expert Iteration for Risk](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~cazenave/papers/RiskConferencePaper.pdf)). A hobby PPO agent ran at about 250 environment steps per second on a laptop GPU and after 250k steps was still "well short of" a rule-based bot ([SdSarthak/risk-game-bots](https://github.com/SdSarthak/risk-game-bots)). Across these failures the diagnoses converge. A flat state vector loses the map graph. A single ±1 outcome at the end of a long, dice-heavy, multiplayer game gives almost no credit-assignment signal. And the decision of when to stop attacking was learned badly: agents either attacked everything or sat passive.

The scale of successful self-play elsewhere puts the budget in perspective. Pgx's Gumbel AlphaZero reached its Go 9x9 level in about 8 hours on an A100, over roughly 105 million frames ([Pgx](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.17503v4)). Scaled to a 4070, that is perhaps 1 to 2 days, and only for a 2-player game with a GPU-vectorised simulator that Risk does not have. DORA used 192 V100s for about a week per stage ([Bakhtin et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.02924)). Ataraxos, the cheap recent Stratego champion, used about 3,000 GPU-hours and 163 million self-play games ([Slashdot/Ars summary](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/26/10/02/2129251/)). Fifty-six hours on one 4070 is one to two percent of that. The one encouraging data point is about implementation, not strength: a frontier coding agent built a working AlphaZero pipeline for Connect Four in 3 hours and beat the solver as first mover in 7 of 8 trials ([Sherwood et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25067)). Claude can build the machinery. The machinery is the wrong tool for Risk at this scale.

### Equilibrium methods solve the wrong problem, and human-data methods lack data

Pluribus is the existence proof of superhuman 6-player play. Its blueprint took 8 days on a 64-core server, and its authors state that CFR-family algorithms "are not guaranteed to converge to a Nash equilibrium outside of two-player zero-sum games" ([Meta AI](https://ai.meta.com/blog/pluribus-first-ai-to-beat-pros-in-6-player-poker/)). It worked because poker has no lasting alliances. Risk does, and the right play is often to coordinate implicitly against the leader rather than to be balanced against everyone. The methods that reached top human level in an alliance game all anchored their search to human play. Diplodocus, for example, regularises search toward a human-imitation policy. It is trained on about 46,000 webDiplomacy games filtered to the top half of rated players, and in a 200-game tournament against 62 humans its two copies ranked 1st and 3rd by Elo ([Bakhtin et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.05492)). Carr "could not find an existing database of human risk games" ([Carr 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06355)), and this research found none either. Human data is therefore the missing ingredient for expert free-for-all play. Novelty World can start generating it, but it cannot be bought within a week.

### LLMs can drive the build, not the moves

The one Risk study of frontier LLM players, gemini-3.1-pro-preview, gpt-5.1, claude-opus-4-7 and kimi-k2.6, found that Gemini won 20 of 32 games in a cross-provider championship. When execution was standardised on a cheaper scaffold and only the planner varied, the planners were statistically indistinguishable (p ≈ 0.82). The spread came from execution behaviour, not strategy, and no LLM was tested against strong bots or humans ([Ekne 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22238)). A comparable Diplomacy harness cost $15 to $250 per model to benchmark 20 games ([Duffy et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07485)). The cost per game, seconds of latency on each of hundreds of sub-decisions, and the need for a server-side key make an LLM a poor engine for a self-contained browser bot. An LLM layer for chat or table talk could come later, with an engine choosing the moves.

## The recommended path, and why its order matters

The plan follows the one documented success, Carr's, and fixes his known weaknesses. Each stage produces the opponent pool and training data the next stage needs, so every gate is measured against something fixed. It also back-loads machine learning: Stages 0 to 3 contain no neural network at all. The owner can verify each stage with ordinary software-engineering judgement (tests, throughput numbers, win rates), and Claude can debug each stage visibly.

The engine comes first, and it decides everything downstream. Every prior attempt was throttled by a slow engine: Python, or Java talking to Lux over a socket ([Carr 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06355); [Blomqvist 2020](https://forums.triplea-game.org/assets/uploads/files/1637032761173-fulltext01.pdf)). One engine should serve both training and the browser, with battles resolved by exact outcome tables rather than simulated dice. Whether TypeScript on 16 worker threads is fast enough, or the hot core must become Rust compiled to native and WASM, is for the Stage 0 throughput gate to decide, not to assume. That choice is an inference from the budget, not from a source. Start with the rules most measurable and least political, then widen. Prior Risk bots used fixed card values with automatic trades, and Blomqvist also made cards open ([Blomqvist 2020](https://forums.triplea-game.org/assets/uploads/files/1637032761173-fulltext01.pdf)). Evaluate both 1v1 and 6-player from the start, because the value-vector design handles any player count. 1v1 removes the social layer and gives clean ratings; 6-player is the real target.

The heuristic bots matter more than they look. They are the yardstick, the training-data generators and the fallback product. Lux's and Domination's rule bots encode the community's consensus strategy. That consensus covers the "common threat" response to a runaway leader, continent ratings by bonus and border count, breaking enemy bonuses, cluster consolidation, and kills for cards ([Lütolf 2013](https://ai.dmi.unibas.ch/papers/theses/luetolf-bachelor-13.pdf)). It is also the only practical way to give the bot leader-aware multiplayer behaviour before human data exists. Evolutionary tuning of their weights uses the CPU as an embarrassingly parallel tournament runner, which is exactly what the laptop is good at.

The learned evaluator comes only after search works, and it starts from bot games, not self-play. Carr's network was a 2-layer GCN with layers of 60 and 30 units over 14 features per territory, plus a 66-dimensional global input, outputting each seat's win probability. It was trained by TD(λ = 0.8) on 200,000 turn-end states from about 2,000 bot games, in 3 epochs ([Carr 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06355)). That is tiny by any standard, trains in minutes to hours on the 4070 in PyTorch, and exports to the browser. Two tested improvements are worth adding. First, KataGo-style auxiliary targets, such as each seat's territory or army share a few turns ahead, give a denser learning signal than win or loss alone; they were a major part of KataGo's 50x efficiency gain ([Wu 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)). Second, self-play should draw opponents from a pool of past checkpoints plus the heuristic bots. Naive self-play can cycle without improving ([Balduzzi et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08106)), and a fixed pool is the cheap version of AlphaStar's league.

## Stage gates an engineer can check without ML expertise

Every gate below is a pass/fail number. All game-playing gates use the same harness. Each matchup is played in all seat rotations with **duplicate dice seeds**, so luck is mirrored across seats. A sequential probability ratio test (SPRT) stops each comparison once it is decisive, as the site's Monopoly bot ladder already does. Each gate reports win rate, mean placement and a confidence interval. In 6-player games, chance is **16.7%**. Gates include mixed-population cross-play, one candidate among five copies of the pool bot and the reverse, because DORA showed that strength inside one population says nothing about strength in another ([Bakhtin et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.02924)). The thresholds are proposed values drawn from the cited benchmarks. Retune them once Stage 0 measures real throughput.

| Stage | What gets built | Pass gate (measurable) | Rough compute |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Engine** | Full rules; exact battle-outcome tables; seedable dice; headless batch runner; game logs | Battle tables match the exact Markov values (3v2 single roll: 37.2 / 33.6 / 29.3%; 5v3 full battle: 76.9%). Rule tests pass. Every game ends under a turn cap. Measured throughput of **≥ 15 full heuristic-bot games per second** across 16 threads, the rate that makes about 1M games feasible in one night. | Day work, no overnight run |
| **1. Heuristic pool** | 3 or 4 rule bots of different styles (continent-turtle, cluster-expander, card-killer, leader-basher) | Each beats random-legal bots ≥ 95% in 1v1. Pool round-robin shows no bot strictly dominated, so the pool is diverse. | Minutes |
| **2. Evolved heuristic** | CMA-ES or genetic tuning of evaluation weights by self-tournament | SPRT-significant gain over the untuned bot. ≥ 25% wins in 6-player games against the pool. Weights stable across two independent runs. | 1 to 2 nights |
| **3. Turn search** | Carr-style end-of-turn search over the Stage 2 evaluation: frontier-only chunked placement, quantile battle outcomes, re-plan after each real attack, max-n values, card determinisation | **≥ 30% in 6-player games against the pool** (Carr reached 35.3% against strong Lux bots). SPRT win over Stage 2 in 1v1. A move fits the browser budget on a 4-year-old phone. | Tuning tournaments, 1 night |
| **4. Learned value net** | GCN value vector plus auxiliary targets, trained by TD(λ) on Stage 3 games, swapped in as the search evaluation | At an **equal time budget per move**, an SPRT win over Stage 3. Value predictions better calibrated than the hand evaluation's (lower Brier score on held-out games). No feature driven out of its training range (the Australia-turtle check). | Data generation 1 night; training about 1 to 2 h on GPU |
| **5. Self-play refinement** | TD self-play against a pool of past checkpoints plus heuristic bots; optional low-simulation Gumbel ExIt policy prior | Each checkpoint beats its predecessor by SPRT, and still beats every pool bot at or above Stage 4's rate (no forgetting). Cross-play in both mixtures stays above 16.7%. Stop when two successive checkpoints fail the gate. | Remaining 2 to 3 nights |
| **6. Humans (stretch)** | Ship to the site; log every human game decision by decision | Against good human players over about 100 or more 6-player games: **≥ 25 to 30% wins** (clearly expert; par 16.7%). In 1v1, a sustained majority over a similar sample. | Calendar time, not compute |

The human gate needs more games than intuition suggests. At a true 6-player win rate around 25%, 100 games give a standard error of about 4.3 points, so a 95% interval spans roughly ±8.5 points. That is enough to separate 30% from par, not enough to separate 25% from 30%. This is arithmetic, not a sourced figure, and fewer games give a verdict only for dramatic results. The same logic explains why the bot-versus-bot gates need hundreds to thousands of games each, and why engine speed governs the schedule. The week's compute goes mostly to evaluation tournaments, not to training: Carr's whole training run fit in an hour.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question. The binding constraint on a strong Risk bot was never GPU time. It is representation (graph-structured state, exact dice, a pruned turn), engine speed, and the absence of human data for the social game. Those are engineering problems a strong software engineer can judge, which makes this budget more promising than the "expert in a week" framing suggests, while the expert label stays out of reach for a reason no amount of training fixes. The bot's free-for-all weakness will sit exactly where humans differ from bots: whom to attack, when to stop being the leader, whom to let win. Hand-written leader-aware heuristics are the only stand-in until real games exist.

The most valuable thing to build alongside the bot may therefore be the game log. Every human game on Novelty World yields hundreds of labelled decisions. Within months that corpus could support what no Risk AI has had: a human-anchored, piKL-style search regulariser, the technique that took Diplomacy bots to the top of human ladders ([Jacob et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07544)). The same games would also yield an honest measurement of whether the bot has become expert. A week of overnight runs can buy a bot at or beyond the best published Risk agent's level against bots; logging human games is the step that could make it expert.
