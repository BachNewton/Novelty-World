# Citizens of the Federation

A growing collection of stories about the everyday lives of ordinary people in the Star Trek universe, on Earth and other Federation planets, across the centuries. The workshop's `CLAUDE.md` (`../../CLAUDE.md`) says how the producer and the writer work, and the `write-fiction` skill is how a chapter is written. Beside this file: the narrator in `voice.md`, each chapter's job and the threads between them in `outline.md`, the people in `people.md`, every date in `timeline.ts`, and what's still open in `open-questions.md`.

## The idea

Star Trek's stories usually happen on a starship. This one looks at the people who never board one: what life is like for an ordinary citizen as society, technology, culture, values and politics change around them. It starts in the 21st century and moves forward, and the hard part it has to balance is the facts of the universe against a personal, relatable touch.

## The reader

Written for people whose values are Star Trek's: loved by its fans, and just as readable by someone who has never seen an episode. The book gives the reader everything they need about its universe, its history and its places. It doesn't write to an American, and assumes no one knows Ohio or anywhere else it goes.

The cold read reads as: someone who shares Star Trek's values, has seen little or none of it, isn't American, and has never been to Ohio.

## The core

The book is about these. They aren't a checklist but a test for each chapter: does it speak to enough of the core, and is there something more it could say from it?

- Timeline
- Geography and planet
- Values
- Culture
- Society
- Politics and government
- Conflict: how people approach it and resolve differences and disagreements
- Technology: medicine and health (physical and mental), power, resources, labor, education, transport and transit, construction
- Mortality, ethics and philosophy
- Economy and money
- Faith and religion
- Other species: fear, trust and living side by side
- Law and justice
- Family, gender and sexuality
- Environment: the planet's damage and repair
- Art, food and leisure
- How the past is remembered: each era's story about the ones before it

## The values

Each chapter tests its subjects against these:

1. Every person has dignity, without earning it. Worth doesn't depend on species, class, body, mind or usefulness.
2. Difference is a gift, not a threat: infinite diversity in infinite combinations.
3. The first move toward the unknown is curiosity.
4. Understanding is stronger than force. Violence is a failure, sometimes necessary, never a victory to celebrate.
5. Freedom is real only when it's for everyone, and power answers to principle.
6. Principles matter most when they're costly. Frightened people deciding those rules are a luxury is how things go wrong.
7. We're responsible for each other. A society is measured by how it treats those who can't manage alone.
8. Knowledge and work are for everyone's good.
9. People can change. Humanity's past is terrible, and the hope is that it grew and could again.

**The book has an opinion, and it's Star Trek's.** Compassion, empathy, togetherness, love, understanding and liberty. It holds these strongly and criticizes those who violate them, on any side. Having no villains doesn't mean having no view: it condemns what people do and what ideas do, while still seeing them as people. The Federation's own blind spot is fair game: it can be too sure of its goodness, and ordinary citizens can test whether it lives up to these values for the people who never leave home.

**The book's truth:** people are flawed and can do terrible things, but humanity is good, and over time it finds a way. There's hope, and through technology and people working together we can build a better future. However dark a chapter gets, it sits inside this.

**Serious subjects are met head on.** Star Trek takes on hard, uncomfortable subjects, and this book does too, for the sake of the story. The producer sets no content limit: the focus is truth, honesty, reality and life. Claude's instinct to soften or avoid them is something to push against here. If Claude won't write something, it says so plainly, and never quietly waters it down.

## Standing decisions

- **The world is what holds it together.** It doesn't need one family, one place or a continuing cast. It is closer to a collection of short stories, each a lens into one life, and stories may overlap: a minor character can step forward later, and a name, place or tune can echo across centuries.
- **It grows over time.** There's no finished plan. The producer brings ideas, Claude helps turn them into stories that work, and the outline keeps up.
- **No villains, and no trouble for its own sake.** The conflict is life itself (people grow, change and lose things) and how the world of Star Trek deals with it.
- **Star Trek's spirit:** the utopia, the humanistic optimism, and using the future to comment on our own society. What it comments on comes out of the stories, not chosen in advance.
- **Its tone is Star Trek's:** hopeful and optimistic, serious and grave when the times are. The first chapters are dark because their times are.
- **Each chapter thinks through one idea,** named in its outline entry, and its people and plot serve it: something open at the start has moved by the end, however small, while the book as a whole rolls on, with some things resolved, some left open and new ones opening. Every scene earns its place against the idea. The idea is felt through the story, never stated.
- **A chapter is about 2,500 to 4,500 words**, fifteen to thirty minutes read aloud.
- **It's fiction.** The story may sit close to the producer's life, but invention leads, and Claude doesn't mine the producer's life for material.

## Real history and Star Trek's

The book's 2020s are our real 2020s: the phones, video calls, apps and machines people really use, and real places as they really are. Star Trek's events of the period happen inside that world. Invented technology appears only where canon or the story needs it. How a reader tells canon from real history without being told is open (`open-questions.md`).

## Canon

Stay as close to canon as possible, filling gaps only where needed and only where nothing contradicts it. The producer knows the universe well.

**Canon is checked online while working, never from memory.** Memory Alpha's pages don't fetch directly: use search results, TrekCore's episode pages, Wikipedia and Ex Astris Scientia. When canon disagrees with itself, take the modern retcon where it fits. Where it doesn't fit, or an idea contradicts canon, stop and talk it over with the producer before writing around it. A ruling goes in this table.

| Event | Canon so far | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Bell Riots | San Francisco, first week of September 2024 | Solid |
| Irish unification | 2024, cited in TNG "The High Ground" as a case where terrorism achieved its aim | Open: how the book holds it (`open-questions.md`) |
| Sanctuary Districts | American cities in the 2020s, in every major city by the early 2020s. | Second Civil War | American, some time after 2024. Pike's speech, the only on-screen mention found: "Our conflict also started with a fight for freedoms. ## Thinking about it like code

The producer is a software engineer, and tradeoffs land best in those terms: the core is the spec, the outline's test is the test suite, and canon is the external API. Where the analogy breaks, say so: a novel's chapters are meant to depend on each other (the workshop's `CLAUDE.md`).
