## Telling canon from real history

Agreed: the book's 2020s are our real ones, with Star Trek's events happening inside them. Open: how a reader tells which is which without being told.

**Recommended:** what the reader lived through gets a mention; what they didn't gets the story. A real event, place or device can be named in passing, because the reader brings it with them. A canon event or an invented thing is new to the reader, so it arrives the way it arrives to the people in the scene, with what it is and what it does to them. The reader never needs a label, because the difference shows as familiarity: one is remembered, the other is news. The past-tense companion can also place an era in a sentence ("That was the autumn when…").

## Irish unification

Canon's only source is Data in TNG's "The High Ground" (1990), who cites the Irish unification of 2024 as a case where terrorism achieved its aim; the line kept the episode off British and Irish television for years. The producer's reading is the 1980s one: in the middle of the Troubles, a peaceful, united Ireland was a hopeful thing to imagine, and that hope is lost when it appears as a passing fact.

**A possible reconciliation, for later:** our 2024 unification comes by a vote, as the Good Friday Agreement allows, and Data's line is one later historian's judgement about the decades of violence before it, which people of the time would argue with. The book could carry both: the hope, and the argument over what bought it. Needs proper research before deciding.

## A narrator voice per era

When the book is performed, each era (chapters 1 to 3 are one) could get its own narrator voice from the same description, so a new voice marks a jump in time. The cost is the sense of one companion across all of it, which the voice the producer chose leans toward.
