# Outline

What each chapter is for, so the writing can be free while the book holds its direction. The core itself is in `CLAUDE.md`. This says which parts of it each chapter is responsible for.

## Using it

Before a chapter is written, fill in its entry, starting with its idea: the one concept the chapter thinks through, which its people and plot exist to serve. Before it goes to the producer, test it against that entry:

1. **Does it answer its question?** Something open at the start has moved by the end.
2. **Does it go deep on its deep subjects?** Each one must matter to what happens, not only be mentioned.
3. **Is there more it could say from the core?** Look at what it barely touches, and add only what fits.
4. **Does it sit inside the book's truth, and test its values?** However dark it gets, humanity finds a way, and the chapter has something to say about at least one of the nine values.
5. **Is the idea the subject, and the people and plot its case?** Every scene works the idea through or shows what it costs someone. Neither a lecture nor a family story with Star Trek wallpaper.

An entry guides the chapter and doesn't bind it. If a better idea changes one, change the entry and say why it fits the direction. Deep subjects should rotate, so that over the book every part of the core gets its turn.

## Chapters

### 1. Leaving (29 January to 6 February 2021, Dayton)

- **The idea:** what makes someone leave their country. Not one reason but a year of them, each reasonable, until staying is the decision that needs defending.
- **Question:** Who goes, and who stays behind?
- **Goes deep on:** health (a pandemic, a vaccine, an injection that can't wait), family, mortality, labor (work that moves to a screen, work that can't).
- **Touches:** timeline, geography, politics, faith, technology, society, how the past is remembered.
- **The world it shows:** COVID in an Ohio town, where the same shot is a miracle to one neighbour and a threat to the next. The Sanctuary Districts are already part of the world, glimpsed once: among the reasons Anna doesn't put on her spreadsheet, Nora waving that autumn at a line with their bags at the gate of Columbus's new District, an hour up the road, and Anna not carrying her out of the room for it.
- **Its shape:** one week, lived through, with the year before it arriving only in what people remember and say. Anna, Ben and Nora have lived in Walt and Ruth's house since March 2020, so that the old people never have to go into a store. Anna books her parents' vaccine the Friday the appointments open; on Monday Dolores gives Ruth her shot in the front hall, and Joy, her daughter, a COVID nurse, is leaving for Toronto; on Wednesday Walt and Ruth are vaccinated downtown, while Gene across the street, who remembers the Cutter batches of 1955, won't be; that night Ben says his dead parents would have been in that day's line, and asks for another year; on Thursday Anna fills in the Finnish forms anyway, to the sound of Ruth's lesson over an iPad; on Saturday, on the porch, Walt and Ruth say no.
- **Moves from → to:** the family is whole in Ohio, with no plan to go anywhere → Anna, Ben and Nora are leaving for Helsinki, and Walt and Ruth, asked to come, have said no, for good reasons. Nobody knows yet what that no will cost.

### 2. Godspeed (8 to 15 April 2024, Dayton)

- **The idea:** hope, for people who stay, is sending someone and waiting for them to come back. Dayton invented flight and has watched nearly all of it on a screen since; a family that stayed has known the one that left through a screen for three years; and for one week everyone is in the same room, and what they hope for is a homecoming.
- **Question:** What does a family hope for, when it has everything it needs this once?
- **Goes deep on:** faith (a congregation praying for the crew, and Ruth, who doesn't pray for outcomes, praying for a frightened stranger's nerve), technology (spaceflight, and the screen that has been the family for three years), family, art (Ruth's music, and a drawing).
- **Touches:** timeline (the eclipse, Glenn, Armstrong), geography (the town where flight was invented, never sure the world noticed), culture (two languages, one birthday tune), mental health (Ruth sees Renée's fear because she knows it from the inside, and says only what a teacher would), how the past is remembered (the Wrights' first circle, first printed in a beekeeping magazine).
- **The world it shows:** the last good time, without a wink. Ruth is well and gets her shot from Dolores at the open clinic; the eclipse of Monday 8 April darkens the street; a week later, on Nora's fifth birthday, the Shango launches with Renée Picard aboard. Nora's English is her second language; Theo, two, meets his grandfather off a screen; Walt gets up from a chair in two goes. Gene and Barb Kessler have sold up and are moving to their daughter's. At the bottom of the front page under the eclipse and the crew, the county commissioners are still arguing about whether Dayton needs a Sanctuary District, like the big cities: half-noticed, and paid off in chapter 3.
- **Canon as news:** the Europa Mission is the one thing in the chapter the reader didn't live through, so it arrives as it does to the people in it: as the biggest news of their lives. Nobody has gone anywhere but the moon, nobody even that far in fifty years, and Walt keeps looking at the newspaper photograph to see if it will turn out to be something else. The anchors keep saying it as if they were still checking. The reader's "that didn't happen" meets the characters' "I can't believe this is happening".
- **Its shape:** one week, lived through. The eclipse on the street (Theo checks that his grandfather goes on round the back; the crew on the front page under the eclipse, a promise Walt has followed for years, half believing, suddenly real; Gene's welding glass and porch light; Jupiter in the dark, "something you could hit with a stone"; the launch on Nora's birthday; Gene's news; Anna's one beat of what coming home costs her). Ruth and Nora at the piano, playing together on one bench. The clinic on Friday: Joy still in Toronto, "still with the children"; Renée on the waiting-room television, touching her ear, and Ruth reads her as she read forty years of pupils before recitals ("The frightened ones practise"); the book never confirms it. Church on Sunday, and Ruth praying for the French girl's nerve. Ben alone at his parents' grave in Toledo, holding his phone up to the stone. Monday: one birthday song in two languages at once; Walt's story of the Wrights and the beekeeper, and Dayton watching flight on a screen "with the children sitting closest"; the launch; Renée's "We are on our way. Don't wait up."; Godspeed ("It means go well"); the crew "due home the winter you're ten", which is all her fingers. The drawing of Europa begun in Anna's old orange crayon: "The paper is the ice. You only draw the cracks."
- **Moves from → to:** the family meet as people off a screen (a grandfather who is a face, a granddaughter who visits his language, parents older than the calls showed) → for a week they are in one house, playing and singing at the same moment, and their hope is fastened to a ship coming home on a date a child counts on all ten fingers. Nora keeps the crayon, and calls Helsinki home.

### 4. Father's Day (8 November 2026, Helsinki)

- **The idea:** a country is a decision people keep making. Finland's maternity box ("All you have to do is let the nurse see you early"), its shelter law, its drying cupboard and its letter in two languages, against a vote in Washington that cut Medicaid, a clinic that closed rather than wait, a sign on a door and "the dims have to go somewhere"; Ohio deciding again five days before, and electing the doctor of its first pandemic spring, which reaches nobody by Sunday; and Finland's own rules shutting Ruth out at the end. - **Question:** Is the danger out there on the news, or already in the family?
- **Goes deep on:** mental health, politics and government, values, ethics.
- **Touches:** timeline, geography, society, culture, family, economy, labor, power, education, transit, construction, conflict.
- **Leaves for later:** faith, other species, environment, how the past is remembered.
- **The world it shows:** Helsinki five years in, with a third child due in January. ### 6. The Long Way Round (26 to 28 May 2028, Belfast)

- **The idea:** a count is the one way of deciding that the side that loses can sign. Violence can drag a question to the table, and everybody it touched wants it to have bought something, the man who did it to be justified and the man whose brother it killed so that the brother died for something; but only the paper gets the losers' consent, and nobody can ever count which of the two bought the answer.
- **Question:** Did violence buy this, or did the vote? (Declan's, from chapter 3.)
- **Goes deep on:** politics and government (the Good Friday Agreement's poll, both parts of the island, a recount nobody argues with, a concession), conflict and how it's resolved, how the past is remembered (1973's boycott, 1998's Friday, a minute of film from Derry in 1968, a chant sung by people who never heard a bomb), faith (a Catholic having a Mass said every November for a Protestant soldier the IRA shot, and a Presbyterian who doesn't hold with praying for the dead and has never asked him to stop).

### 7. Gate 12A (14 to 21 January 2030, Dayton)

- **The idea:** everybody fired second. A war nobody declared is made of people each answering someone else's act: the Governor answers the count, the general his orders, the bank two governments, forty million viewers the caption they already agree with, and so nobody started it. The one way out of the regress is a person who says "I did this", and he is the one person neither side wants to hear.
- **Question:** Who started it? Nobody can say; Joel can say what he did.
- **Goes deep on:** conflict (a gate, a shot nobody can place, each side's story beginning with the other's shot), leisure (screens and social media: nine seconds under two captions, a name readable in one thread colour and not the other), law (two lawful orders, a paper in a plastic sleeve, a bank that obeys neither), family (Carol and Joel).
- **Touches:** labor and economy (a county payroll that comes in at zero), gender and sexuality (Carol's two names, printed side by side by Ohio law in 2013, on forty million phones and her garage door), ethics, society (a STAY sign across the street, flowers on the step), food (Danny's crackers, Sunday eggs, the District's last paid-up truck), how the past is remembered (the Governor's pandemic briefings).

### 9. Known As (August to October 2030, Dayton)

- **The idea:** a name against a category. What people are called by those who love them can't be sorted; the letters a ledger keeps can.
- **Question:** How do you keep caring for people when the thing you work for falls apart?
- **Goes deep on:** conflict and how it's resolved, politics and government, art (book censorship), leisure (screen time and social media).
- **Touches:** labor, law, economy, resources, society, ethics, family, gender and sexuality, food and health, faith.
- **The world it shows:** the Second Civil War as most people live it: each side pulling books off shelves, and neighbours turned against each other through their phones.
- **The sides:** today's American politics carried to its extremes, heavily implied and never named. Both sides are made of people, and their ideas aren't equal, and neither gets off. Each side's acts follow from what it is drawn from:
  - **The Stays** (Washington; "a President stays until a count can be trusted"): power that won't leave a count, order, loyalty, the "real" name on a license. Their acts: the general holding Wright-Patterson with rifles (chapter 7); Washington's regulator freezing the money of every government that answers to Philadelphia, so the county can't pay and the District's food stops (chapter 7, and the last truck on 1 February); Carol's old name sent to her phone and painted on her door (chapter 7); the first list of books, from the Stay-leaning legislature in Columbus in 2028, passed over the Governor's veto; the pharmacist in Troy (March 2030): in Stay country twenty miles north, men pull a woman out of her pharmacy for the M on her license and put her in the river with wire on her wrists, and the sheriff charges nobody; and in August the Stays pressing on Dayton from the north and from the base.
  - **The Counts** (Philadelphia; "the count was the count"; Ohio's statehouse and the Guard it commands): procedure, safety, harm, and the belief that being lawful makes you right. Their acts: the Governor sending soldiers with a paper to take a base, and "fired upon" (chapter 7); the Safety Board, set up under her emergency order in February when the county can't pay, made of Carol's own people (commissioners, the health commissioner, a union man, two pastors, half of them from her choir), which keeps the water running and opens the warehouses, then asks a signature for a ration, then, after a substation is bombed in April by nobody knows whom, a pledge to the count, then holds those who won't sign in the District, pending review, under a letter Carol adds to the form herself: H; the Board's second list of books (harm), and hymn 534 for "the Lord is on thy side", which both armies sing; and in August the colonel with the buses.
  - **Ohio's Guard:** the Governor's, a Count force. In April Washington orders it into federal service; some units go over, and Joel's stays with the Governor. Joel is kept off the line after the gate (the Counts' hero, who never said "they fired first") and serves in Dayton under the Board, guarding warehouses and the water plant, and signing for things.
  - **The night of 13 August:** the Stays are pressing on Dayton from the north and from the base. That afternoon the Safety Board leaves for Columbus with its files. The Governor's Guard column, under a colonel from Columbus, stops at the edge of town to come in at first light and hold the city on the river; nobody governs the night between. The colonel wants three things on his table by eight: the water plant's keys, the courthouse's, and the District's rolls, because the District lies between the river and the tracks, on the line he means to hold, and he will clear it by its letters: the H's (the Stays the Board held) east on buses as prisoners, to trade; the R's to the jail; the D's east to a state hospital, for their safety. Joel brought the list and signed for the buses. Carol cuts the ledger to its first column; Joel writes *None available* on the receipt. Gary Pruitt, whose phone has told him whose fault it is (the Stays', and anyone soft on them), reports the ledger, the clerk and the sergeant's visit. An eleven-minute hearing; the colonel, on his first morning holding a city about to be fought over, needs it seen that a famous name buys nothing; at four on Wednesday 14 August the Ohio Guard shoots Joel on the levee behind the county building, under the Governor's flag, for making a false record: shot by his own side, the side whose hero he was. Gary enters Carol into the District under H, the letter she made. The colonel's men fill the buses by looking at people, and fewer than a hundred go. The Counts take hostages, as Bell's men did, and Ben was on the martyr side of Bell.
  - **After:** in October the column is needed elsewhere, nobody ever closes the District, and its residents take the fence down for scrap. The wheat truce is made in Naomi's meetinghouse between the Stay sheriff of the county to the north and a man from what was left of the Board.
- **Its shape:** one night, 13 to 14 August 2030, in the hours when nobody governs Dayton. Carol reads her paper ledger aloud to her son, a name at a time. Then the two of them cut it down to its first column. Chapters 7 and 8 have told the war's first weeks, so the night carries only the Safety Board's slide that leads to it: water and warehouses, then a signature for a ration, then a pledge, then the letter H for those who wouldn't. Naomi's first grain truck (February, after the District's last paid truck on 1 February) is told from the ledger, the one person in Ohio who never saw Joel's face.
- **Moves from → to:** Carol's work is a record of what people are, kept for a county that no longer exists, and any side can sort people with it → all that's left of it is what people are called by those who love them, useless to a man with a bus. It cost her son, and the people with no one to look for them. The Sanctuary District is gone, not abolished, and everyone is hungry. Anna finds her mother anyway.

## Threads

What runs between chapters: something planted in one and paid off or echoed in another. This is the map that keeps the book connected as chapters are added. A thread stays only where it means more each time it returns, and a new chapter is checked against every thread here.

- **Ruth's four bars,** *Finlandia*'s hymn ("Be Still, My Soul"), played at the Sunday call: a mystery in chapter 4, explained in chapter 5 as her message to Anna, sung in the District as hymn number 534 in chapter 9.
- **"Thank you. That's kind.":** Ruth to the county worker she thinks Anna is, in chapter 4; Carol saying Anna's words to Ruth in chapter 5.
- **The county's form:** its "known as" field and its "none available" dropdown in chapter 5, which become Carol's ledger and the document behind Anna's permit in chapter 9.
- **The Henderson boy and the trash bins:** keeping up the house's appearance in chapters 4 and 5 (and the walker Walt says is for them), carrying water in chapter 9.
- **Lucille's shoelace,** tied round Ruth's neck in chapter 5, still missing from her shoe in chapter 9.
- **The Helsinki shelter:** Ben tells Nora the room under the building is for skis (chapter 4). Chapter 11 pays it off.
- **The no in chapter 1:** Walt and Ruth decline to come to Helsinki, and in chapter 4 Finland's rule, that a parent's permit is meant for one who moved with her child, shuts Ruth out.
- **Anna's Finnish:** the children live in Finnish and visit English; their parents study Finnish out of a textbook (chapter 4's dinner). A parent's permit is only for the relatives of Finnish citizens, and Anna can't become one without a certificate of satisfactory Finnish, spoken and written: Nora "could have passed the speaking half of it at four", and Anna's "Ei se mitään" is perfect (chapter 4). In chapter 5 she has booked the exam and filled in an application she can't yet send. In chapter 6 Ben tells Declan she is a citizen (April 2028; she passed the exam the autumn before) and sent her mother's form the same week. Ruth's permit comes in November 2029.
- **Ben's headstone in Toledo:** his parents' deaths in chapter 1, a funeral watched on a laptop and a grave he hasn't yet visited when he hands over his passport; he first stands at it alone in chapter 2, the Sunday before the launch, at one grey stone with both names, finds nothing to say, and holds up a picture of Theo to it; "I've got a headstone in Toledo. It doesn't call on Sundays" in chapter 4.
- **Gene Kessler across the street:** won't have the vaccine in chapter 1, but salts the Dorseys' steps, and leaves his porch light on for anybody coming home late. In chapter 2 he watches the eclipse through his welding glass, the porch light comes on by itself in the dark, and he tells Walt that he and Barb have sold up and are moving to their daughter's in South Carolina, because Barb wants to watch the grandchildren grow up somewhere besides a phone: the first time in her life she has gone first. So he is gone, kindly, before Walt lies seventeen hours on the floor in chapter 5, and chapter 4's "Everybody I knew there has moved away" can lean on it.
- **Two vaccines:** Walt caught polio three years before its vaccine; in chapter 1 he lines up for COVID's and is "on time", while Gene, who remembers the Cutter batches, won't.
- **The Sunday call:** Walt sets it on the porch in chapter 1, "Eleven o'clock, after church. Your mother'll play you something": the call of chapter 4, Ruth's four bars on it explained in chapter 5, and the hour Anna rings Carol in chapter 9.
- **A screen tipping to a ceiling:** a nurse's iPad tips back while Ben says goodbye to his father in chapter 1; Walt's tablet tips to the ceiling fan in chapter 4; Carol's phone tips to a ceiling with no fan as Walt dies in chapter 9.
- **Who holds the screen:** Joy holds up iPads for goodbyes all December (chapter 1); Ben holds his phone up to his parents' gravestone, with a picture of Theo on Walt's lap under the eclipse, "for as long as his arm would let him" (chapter 2); a boy in the District holds the phone up for every resident in the line, and changes arms in the second hour (chapter 3); Carol holds up her phone for Walt's goodbye (chapter 9).
- **Windows:** Walt's mother at the window of his polio ward in 1952, and Nora at the front window waving to him at the end of chapter 1; the family he will know through a screen from then on.
- **Ruth's arm:** "That one's Dolores's" (chapter 1), "she won't let a stranger near that arm" (chapter 4), "Give me your arm" inside the District (chapter 5).
- **Nora's Europa:** at the eclipse Walt tells her the crew go on her birthday ("For me?" "Sure. Who else?"). In chapter 2 she begins the drawing at her grandparents' table in Anna's old orange crayon, the only long one in the coffee can, because Ruth tells her the paper is the ice and only the cracks need drawing (Europa's cracks really are rusty orange-brown). Walt asks her to draw him what Renée finds; she says she'll finish it "at home", meaning Helsinki, and keeps the crayon. It has nine cracks in chapter 3; in chapter 4 it is on the fridge, finished the summer the crayon got too short to hold, with more cracks than anyone has counted and the ice still the paper. The crew come home in chapter 8, when she is ten, which she counted as all her fingers.
- **Renée's fear:** Ruth reads it on a waiting-room television in chapter 2 (a hand at her ear, canon's own tell), as a piano teacher reads a pupil before a recital, and prays for her nerve, not her success. The book never confirms the reading. Renée's "Don't wait up" from orbit, and her organism, come home in chapter 8.
- **Playing together:** over the iPad in chapter 1 nobody can play together; in chapter 2 Ruth and Nora play on one bench, and the family sings one birthday tune in two languages at once.
- **"It means go well":** Walt's gloss on Godspeed in chapter 2, beside Nora's *hyvää matkaa*: there for a later goodbye if one wants it.
- **Joy, Dolores's daughter:** leaves for Toronto in chapter 1, for a job nursing children, while her mother stays to "do the arms"; cared for Khan as a child, and meets him grown in chapter 10. Dolores walks out of the District's gate in June 2030 and isn't seen again, which stays open.
- **Declan Toner, Ben's colleague from Belfast:** beside him at the Bell Riots in chapter 3, where he grew up beside a peace wall "older than me", remembers, as a wee lad, an actor reading the IRA's party's leader's words on the news, and has a da who voted for John Hume's party all his life; he says "maybe it just works". In chapter 6 the question is his, and he answers it only for one dead man: "He didn't pay for anything."
- **"Rule's the rule" and "The count's the count":** Declan's words to Ben about the held-back hostage (chapter 3); Norman Kerr's, the night he loses his country by eighteen thousand votes (chapter 6), which Declan passes to Ben with America's November ahead; and the name one side takes in America's war, the Counts, whose President "said the count was the count" (chapters 7 and 9). In chapter 7 the Governor's press officer says it to Joel, never knowing where it has been, to mean "so we didn't start this": Norman said it to accept a loss, and she says it to be sure she's right.
- **America's count, against Ireland's:** Ben on the phone in May 2028, of November: "Like nobody's going to believe it, whoever wins." The two Presidents of January 2029 (chapters 7 and 9) are the answer.
- **The gate:** at the bottom of Brendan's street, opened at six and shut at half ten by the police from somewhere else (chapter 6); the gate at Wright-Patterson where two lines in the same camouflage stand ten feet apart, with a steel plate risen out of the road between them (chapter 7).
- **The District's food:** the contractor in Indiana that feeds the state's prisons, on a tray (chapter 5); its truck still coming the Monday after the payroll fails, "paid up to the end of the month" (chapter 7); its last truck on 1 February (chapter 9).
- **The wall, and who it hides:** San Francisco's District seen from a twenty-eighth floor, and the tents Ben never asked about (chapter 3); Belfast's peace walls (chapter 3), and in chapter 6 the one at the bottom of Brendan's street, still standing and still shut at night the day Ireland votes to be one country; Columbus's District on the news in chapter 1, Dayton's commissioners arguing on chapter 2's front page; Dayton votes to build its own the week of the riots, for "folks who can't manage" (chapter 3), which takes Ruth in chapter 5 and is torn down by its residents in chapter 9.
- **"Nobody we know":** Walt's answer about who will go into Dayton's District (chapter 3), while Ruth plays behind him; under chapter 4's "Do we know anybody in Pittsburgh?", asked of trouble at Pittsburgh's District, and under Walt's "places downtown, too. For folks who can't manage. I told him we're managing", never quoted back.
- **The box and the clinic:** Finland's maternity box, sent to bring every mother to the clinic, arrives the week Anna learns that Ruth's clinic closed rather than wait for Washington's cuts (chapter 4). Walt: "When you were born, we got a bill."
- **The held-back clip:** Ben holds back Lee, a hostage, saying they're all right (chapter 3). By the rule, and Declan agrees; the governor had already decided. Ben never knows that. It can come back to him whenever a rule is followed faithfully.
- **Four minutes late:** the residents' stories go out four minutes behind, and Ben, watching from a hotel bed, sees the storming after it has happened (chapter 3). Every screen in the book carries the past; this one shows it outright.
- **Joel Flory:** met at Gate 12A in chapter 7, shot on the levee in chapter 9, by his own side, the Governor's Guard, whose hero he was. He calls his mother Carol in chapter 5 ("we tried the other words and none of them fit") and throughout chapter 7; "Mom" is kept for the first column in chapter 9.
- **Black thread and brown:** the Army sews its names in black and the Air Force in a brown that disappears into camouflage (real). At Gate 12A the airman reads FLORY at once and Joel reads KASPRZAK only when the light falls on it; in the video forty million can read FLORY and nobody can read the airman's name (chapter 7). Chapter 9: "His name was sewn on in black thread."
- **Who fired first:** a shot under a canopy that comes from everywhere, a video that starts a second too late, a press officer's "We didn't start this", a garage door and a bunch of flowers where it is impossible to tell which came first (chapter 7); under it, chapter 6's "nobody can count which bought it". Joel's answer is "I fired"; Carol's is "Yes. You did." Chapter 9 can give him "I know who fired second" if it wants it, said to her.
- **"Eat something":** Joel to Danny Ruhl over a phone turned face down, then Carol to Joel at her counter ("Sitting down is part of it"), so the reader learns where he got it (chapter 7); Carol's parting words to him in chapter 9.
- **A hand on a phone turned face down:** Anna's on Ben's at the Father's Day dinner (chapter 4); Joel's on Danny's in the drill hall, buzzing all night with strangers (chapter 7).
- **Carol's two names:** her chosen name taken out of the county directory (chapter 5); the old one printed beside it by Ohio law in the newspaper in 2013, sent to her phone, put beside Joel's face, and painted on her garage door, which she and Joel paint grey in a frost so that it still shows at a low light (chapter 7); the "known as" column in chapter 9. Joel was nine when it went in the paper.
- **The card in the back of the ledger:** THANK YOU FOR YOUR SON, left on Carol's step with supermarket flowers; on its back, in her county hand, "Kasprzak, Eli", and his age, twenty. She keeps it in the back of the green ledger "with the things that had no column" (chapter 7). Chapter 9's ledger can turn it up, or Carol can cut round it.
- **The duck tie:** green with yellow ducks, chosen by Joel at seven for Father's Day; Carol wore it every Thursday for a year and for her clerk-of-the-month picture in the county newsletter, which the Stays put up to shame her (chapter 7). Under chapter 4's Father's Day card.
- **Late for work:** thousands at Gate 12A, and Dale Hutto across the street, a Stay, an engineer on the base, two cars back from the gate (chapter 7).
- **Gary Pruitt's side:** "I'm on your side, Carol." / "Which one's that, Gary?" / "Yours." (chapter 7). By August his phone has told him which, and he reports her and her son (chapter 9).
- **Alma,** born in January 2027, would be 26 in 2053 and 36 at First Contact. The book could quietly return to her decades later.
- **Subjects that return as the world changes:** food in a world with replicators, a future child baffled that a book was ever banned, how the Bell Riots are taught, and gender, screens and censorship once they're no longer fought over.
