# Outline

What each chapter is for, so the writing can be free while the book holds its direction. The core itself is in `CLAUDE.md`. This says which parts of it each chapter is responsible for.

## Using it

Before a chapter is written, fill in its entry, starting with its idea: the one concept the chapter thinks through, which its people and plot exist to serve. Before it goes to the producer, test it against that entry:

1. **Does it answer its question?** Something open at the start has moved by the end.
2. **Does it go deep on its deep subjects?** Each one must matter to what happens, not only be mentioned.
3. **Is there more it could say from the core?** Look at what it barely touches, and add only what fits.
4. **Does it sit inside the book's truth, and test its values?** However dark it gets, humanity finds a way, and the chapter has something to say about at least one of the nine values.
5. **Is the idea the subject, and the people and plot its case?** Every scene works the idea through or shows what it costs someone. Neither a lecture nor a family story with Star Trek wallpaper.

An entry guides the chapter and doesn't bind it. If a better idea changes one, change the entry and say why it fits the direction. Deep subjects should rotate, so that over the book every part of the core gets its turn.

## Chapters

### 1. Leaving (29 January to 6 February 2021, Dayton)

- **The idea:** what makes someone leave their country. Not one reason but a year of them, each reasonable, until staying is the decision that needs defending.
- **Question:** Who goes, and who stays behind?
- **Goes deep on:** health (a pandemic, a vaccine, an injection that can't wait), family, mortality, labor (work that moves to a screen, work that can't).
- **Touches:** timeline, geography, politics, faith, technology, society, how the past is remembered.
- **The world it shows:** COVID in an Ohio town, where the same shot is a miracle to one neighbour and a threat to the next.
- **Its shape:** one week, lived through, with the year before it arriving only in what people remember and say. Anna, Ben and Nora have lived in Walt and Ruth's house since March 2020, so that the old people never have to go into a store. Anna books her parents' vaccine the Friday the appointments open; on Monday Dolores gives Ruth her shot in the front hall, and Joy, her daughter, a COVID nurse, is leaving for Toronto; on Wednesday Walt and Ruth are vaccinated downtown, while Gene across the street, who remembers the Cutter batches of 1955, won't be; that night Ben says his dead parents would have been in that day's line, and asks for another year; on Thursday Anna fills in the Finnish forms anyway, to the sound of Ruth's lesson over an iPad; on Saturday, on the porch, Walt and Ruth say no.
- **Moves from → to:** the family is whole in Ohio, with no plan to go anywhere → Anna, Ben and Nora are leaving for Helsinki, and Walt and Ruth, asked to come, have said no, for good reasons. Nobody knows yet what that no will cost.

### 2. The launch (April 2024, Dayton)

- **The idea:** to be chosen with the chapter. Its subject is hope: what a launch toward Jupiter means to people who will never leave Ohio.
- **Question:** What does a family hope for, when it has everything it needs this once?
- **Goes deep on:** faith, technology (spaceflight), family, art (Ruth's music).
- **Touches:** timeline, geography (the town where flight was invented), culture, mental health, how the past is remembered.
- **The world it shows:** the last good time. The total eclipse of Monday 8 April crosses Ohio, and a week later, on Nora's fifth birthday, Renée Picard's ship launches. The family are home from Helsinki for the first time since they left: Nora's English is her second language now, and Walt and Ruth meet Theo, who is two, off a screen. Ruth is well, the clinic is open, and everyone is in the same house.
- **To check before writing:** whether *Picard* gives Renée the depression it seems to. If it does, Ruth sees something in her that everyone else misses.
- **Moves from → to:** to be written with the chapter. It ends with Nora's drawing of Europa begun, and the crew due home when she is about ten.

### 3. The feed (the first week of September 2024, San Francisco)

- **The idea:** who decides what the world sees. In canon, the Bell Riots turned opinion because the residents' own stories got out; here the companies carrying them choose whether they stay up.
- **Question:** to be chosen with the chapter.
- **Goes deep on:** technology (screens and the companies behind them), society, law and justice, conflict.
- **Touches:** economy, labor, politics, values, culture.
- **The world it shows:** a Sanctuary District a few miles from the offices of the companies that made the city rich. Ben has flown in to his company's offices, and the man beside him while the riots run on every screen is a colleague from Belfast, who grew up with what riots and grievance did and says that maybe it works: Data's claim in "The High Ground", years before it is answered.
- **To check before writing:** how the residents' stories got out in DS9 "Past Tense".
- **Moves from → to:** to be written with the chapter. Ben goes home to Helsinki with the half of America that calls Bell a martyr, while his wife's parents live among the half that builds higher fences.

### 4. Father's Day (November 2026, Helsinki)

- **The idea:** a country is a decision people keep making. Finland's baby box, shelter law, drying cupboard and letter in three languages against Ohio's levy vote, sign on a door and "the dims have to go somewhere", with Finland's own rule shutting Ruth out at the end. Same species; they decided differently.
- **Question:** Is the danger out there on the news, or already in the family?
- **Goes deep on:** mental health, politics and government, values, ethics.
- **Touches:** timeline, geography, society, culture, family, economy, labor, power, education, transit, construction, conflict.
- **Leaves for later:** faith, other species, environment, how the past is remembered.
- **Moves from → to:** America is something they watch → America has reached Ruth's door.
- **Its place:** the reader already knows everyone at the table, and the Finnish rule at its end is the price of the no in chapter 1.

### 5. Responsible Party (March 2027, Dayton)

- **The idea:** a form decides what a person is. Ruth's placement form and Carol's license are two cases of it, and Ruth's own declaration, made in a good year, is a person deciding first.
- **Question:** Where does Ruth end up, and who gets to decide?
- **Goes deep on:** law and justice, health, gender and sexuality, food (who can get healthy food, and at what price).
- **Touches:** economy, labor, family, ethics, politics, transit, faith.
- **The world it shows:** inside a Sanctuary District, through its paperwork.
- **The clerk:** Carol Flory, a trans woman, a person first, who fills in other people's identity on forms all day while her own legal identity is under threat.
- **Moves from → to:** the county is about to decide Ruth's life, on a signature she can't understand → Ruth decided it herself, in a good year, by naming Anna, who signs her mother into the District as the only way anyone can ever get her out. Ruth is inside, on the shot, with a Finnish application started, and Carol has put her own file in a supervisor's queue.

### 7. Gate 12A (January and February 2030, Dayton)

- **The idea:** to be chosen with the chapter. Its ground: a war nobody declared, that began with a shot nobody can place.
- **Question:** to be chosen with the chapter.
- **Goes deep on:** conflict, leisure (screens and social media), law, family.
- **Touches:** labor, economy, society, ethics, gender and sexuality, food.
- **The world it shows:** the first weeks of the Second Civil War, from Joel Flory's side. Two Presidents have been sworn in since January 2029, one in Washington and one in Philadelphia, and in Ohio people call each other Stays and Counts. On 14 January the Ohio Guard and the airmen of Wright-Patterson stand ten feet apart at Gate 12A for forty minutes, then fire on each other. That Friday the county's bank refuses to pay. Nine seconds of video make Joel the hero of one side and the butcher of the other, and his mother's picture goes up beside his. In February Naomi's first grain truck comes to the District's door.
- **Moves from → to:** to be written with the chapter. The reader comes out of it knowing Joel, so that chapter 9 costs them something.

### 8. The same week (February 2030, Helsinki)

### 9. Known As (August to October 2030, Dayton)

- **The idea:** a name against a category. What people are called by those who love them can't be sorted; the letters a ledger keeps can.
- **Question:** How do you keep caring for people when the thing you work for falls apart?
- **Goes deep on:** conflict and how it's resolved, politics and government, art (book censorship), leisure (screen time and social media).
- **Touches:** labor, law, economy, resources, society, ethics, family, gender and sexuality, food and health, faith.
- **The world it shows:** the Second Civil War as most people live it: each side pulling books off shelves, and neighbours turned against each other through their phones.
- **The sides:** today's American politics carried to its extremes, heavily implied and never named. Both sides are made of people, and their ideas aren't equal, and neither gets off.
- **Its shape:** one night, 13 to 14 August 2030, in the hours when nobody governs Dayton. Carol reads her paper ledger aloud to her son, a name at a time. Then the two of them cut it down to its first column. Chapters 7 and 8 have told the war's first weeks, so the night carries only the Safety Board's slide that leads to it: water and warehouses, then a signature for a ration, then a pledge, then the letter H for those who wouldn't.
- **Moves from → to:** Carol's work is a record of what people are, kept for a county that no longer exists, and any side can sort people with it → all that's left of it is what people are called by those who love them, useless to a man with a bus. It cost her son, and the people with no one to look for them. The Sanctuary District is gone, not abolished, and everyone is hungry. Anna finds her mother anyway.

## Threads

What runs between chapters: something planted in one and paid off or echoed in another. This is the map that keeps the book connected as chapters are added. A thread stays only where it means more each time it returns, and a new chapter is checked against every thread here.

- **Ruth's four bars,** *Finlandia*'s hymn ("Be Still, My Soul"), played at the Sunday call: a mystery in chapter 4, explained in chapter 5 as her message to Anna, sung in the District as hymn number 534 in chapter 9.
- **"Thank you. That's kind.":** Ruth to the county worker she thinks Anna is, in chapter 4; Carol saying Anna's words to Ruth in chapter 5.
- **The county's form:** its "known as" field and its "none available" dropdown in chapter 5, which become Carol's ledger and the document behind Anna's permit in chapter 9.
- **The Henderson boy and the trash bins:** keeping up the house's appearance in chapters 4 and 5, carrying water in chapter 9.
- **Lucille's shoelace,** tied round Ruth's neck in chapter 5, still missing from her shoe in chapter 9.
- **The Helsinki shelter:** Ben tells Nora the room under the building is for skis (chapter 4). Chapter 11 pays it off.
- **The no in chapter 1:** Walt and Ruth decline to come to Helsinki, and in chapter 4 Finland's rule, that a parent's permit is meant for one who moved with her child, shuts Ruth out.
- **Ben's two headstones in Toledo:** his parents' deaths in chapter 1, a funeral watched on a laptop and a grave he hasn't yet visited when he hands over his passport; said in a line in chapter 4.
- **Gene Kessler across the street:** won't have the vaccine in chapter 1, but salts the Dorseys' steps. He must be gone (dead, moved or in care) before Walt lies seventeen hours on the floor in chapter 5; chapter 2 or 4 settles which, without making him a lesson.
- **Two vaccines:** Walt caught polio three years before its vaccine; in chapter 1 he lines up for COVID's and is "on time", while Gene, who remembers the Cutter batches, won't.
- **The Sunday call:** Walt sets it on the porch in chapter 1, "Eleven o'clock, after church. Your mother'll play you something": the call of chapter 4, Ruth's four bars on it explained in chapter 5, and the hour Anna rings Carol in chapter 9.
- **A screen tipping to a ceiling:** a nurse's iPad tips back while Ben says goodbye to his father in chapter 1; Walt's tablet tips to the ceiling fan in chapter 4; Carol's phone tips to a ceiling with no fan as Walt dies in chapter 9.
- **Who holds the screen:** Joy holds up iPads for goodbyes all December (chapter 1); Carol holds up her phone for Walt's (chapter 9).
- **Windows:** Walt's mother at the window of his polio ward in 1952, and Nora at the front window waving to him at the end of chapter 1; the family he will know through a screen from then on.
- **Ruth's arm:** "That one's Dolores's" (chapter 1), the arm it took Dolores years to earn (chapter 4), "Give me your arm" inside the District (chapter 5).
- **Nora's Europa:** the launch on her fifth birthday in chapter 2, the orange crayon drawing on the fridge in chapter 4, the homecoming in chapter 8.
- **Joy, Dolores's daughter:** leaves for Toronto in chapter 1, for a job nursing children, while her mother stays to "do the arms"; cared for Khan as a child, and meets him grown in chapter 10. Dolores walks out of the District's gate in June 2030 and isn't seen again, which stays open.
- **Ben's colleague from Belfast:** beside him at the Bell Riots in chapter 3, voting in chapter 6.
- **Joel Flory:** met at Gate 12A in chapter 7, shot on the levee in chapter 9.
- **Alma,** born in January 2027, would be 26 in 2053 and 36 at First Contact. The book could quietly return to her decades later.
- **Subjects that return as the world changes:** food in a world with replicators, a future child baffled that a book was ever banned, how the Bell Riots are taught, and gender, screens and censorship once they're no longer fought over.
