# Chapter 1 purpose audit

A scene-by-scene check of "Dark by Four" against "no filler": what each scene is for, which beats carry that, and which don't. **Agreed with the owner, and applied in `chapters/01/novel.txt`,** the chapter rewritten as a narrated novel. Line ids are the old `chapters/01/script.yaml`.

The chapter's question, from the outline: *is the danger out there on the news, or already in the family?* Its idea, named after the audit: *a country is a decision people keep making.* The dinner, the call, the kitchen and the table all serve it. Bedtime serves it least: it's about a family deciding not to say, which is the same idea at the scale of one house.

## Breaking decided rules (fixed whatever else is agreed)

- **Spoken Finnish:** "makaronilaatikko" (l018), and "Ei se mitään" twice (l023, l080). The narrator says it, in English.
- **Fumbling with technology:** the call opens on Walt pointing the tablet at the ceiling (l029–l030). Cut. The fan at the end of the call (l077) stays, because chapter 3 echoes it ("a ceiling in Kettering. It had no fan"), but it becomes his hand giving out, which is the polio, not the device.

## The world (l001–l004)

- **What it says:** America is sliding (the riots, the Guard, "Sanctuary"), and the rest of the world is still reaching outward (Ireland united, a mission to Europa).
- **Values:** 9, people can change, as the hope set against the slide.
- **Carried by:** all four lines. The orange crayon plants Nora and the refrigerator the chapter ends beside.
- **Filler:** none. **Keep as it is.**

## Dinner (l005–l028b)

- **What it says:** a country that decided to look after people (the baby box, the shelter with room for everyone), seen by Americans who chose it and still follow home on a phone. Work is leaving people (Ben's team, eleven to two).
- **Values:** 7, a society is measured by how it treats those who can't manage; 8, work for everyone's good; 2, difference, through a child who has become a little Finnish.
- **Defines:** Ben, who jokes; Anna, who closes the door on the news; Nora, who has a language her parents don't.
- **Moves the story:** asks the chapter's question out loud ("Do we know anybody in Pittsburgh?").

**Why the old beats fail, from the owner's listen.** They're false, and the family doesn't come across as likeable:

- **Neither mistake would happen.** "Tall" and "funny" aren't alike in Finnish (*pitkä* and *hauska*), and a modern phone translates a child's card correctly. The joke rests on a mistake no phone would make.
- **Nobody five years in misses "it's nothing".** *Ei se mitään* is one of the first phrases anyone living in Finland learns. Ben not knowing it makes him look like he never tried, and "ladies and gentlemen" turns that into smugness.
- **The banter sounds like a sitcom,** and AI voices play sarcasm as snark. "On the record", "ladies and gentlemen", "Not at the table" and "entered into the record" are bits the family performs for each other, not warmth. So when the call breaks their hearts, the listener isn't yet on their side.

**The real gap is fluency, not vocabulary.** The parents know the words. What they'll never have is the ease: the speed, the jokes, the language the children use with each other. That's true of immigrant families, it's audible, and it needs nobody to be foolish.

**The rework (agreed).** Ben can read a little, so he asks Nora to read him his own card, and she translates it aloud, in English, as she goes. She's seven, so she improves it along the way. Then she turns to Theo and says something in Finnish, fast, and both children laugh. Ben asks what was funny. Nora says, in English, "Nothing." It's the first time she has kept something from them in a language they can't keep up with, and the narrator carries it: *Ben has had five years of Tuesday night classes. He can read a card, slowly. He can't follow his children when they talk to each other at the speed of children. She lives in a language her parents can only visit.*

This drops the phone, the cup and the macaroni. Ben's job (l007b) stays a fact about work in the opening narration rather than a joke. Theo gets the laugh. Ben and Anna are moved, not arch: the warmth is that he wants his daughter to read him his card.

The rule that came out of this, that humour works on the words alone, is now in the story's notes.

## The table (l119–l123)

- **What it says:** the system that works still has a door that won't open: a permit is not usually granted to someone who can get treatment in her home country, and that sentence is exactly where Ruth falls.
- **Values:** 6 and 7, Finland's own principles tested on an outsider; the book's view that no country is simply good.
- **Carried by:** "She reads that sentence again." The model of narrator restraint.
- **Filler:** none. **Keep as it is.**

## What the audit changes, in sum

- Cut: district heating, macaroni, the spilled cup, the ceiling-fan opening.
- Rework: Nora reads her father his card, then shares a joke with Theo in Finnish that her parents can't follow. The family's sitcom bits go.
- Add four carrying lines (Pittsburgh, the Finnish, the county visit, bedtime) and the "downtown" fact.
- After the Adams take-and-leave list was agreed, three more: a digression on the baby box (universal since 1949, its pattern changing every year: Kela's own history and Wikipedia's "Maternity package"), the narrator confiding that Ruth plays the four bars only while the call is on (chapter 2 reveals they're *Finlandia*, for Anna), and the levy line, the chapter's one piece of absurdity.
- The novel-format rewrite keeps "Do we know anybody in Pittsburgh?" spoken, and drops "she knows what it means when he says fine three times".
