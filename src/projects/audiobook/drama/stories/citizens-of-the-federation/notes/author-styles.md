# Author styles: what this book can learn from Adams, Tolkien and Rowling

What this book takes from these writers, and the narrator's craft that came out of it. It started from the owner's push for the book to be philosophically and morally dense, with no filler, and asks what kind of narrator and prose carries that density by ear.

## The four styles

| | Douglas Adams | J. R. R. Tolkien | J. K. Rowling | This book |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Narrator | A wry personality who digresses and confides | Remote and epic (warmer in *The Hobbit*) | Close third person, inside Harry's head | An observer who sees everything, cares, and holds back |
| Dense with | Ideas | World | Plot | Morality, in intent |
| Tone | Comic | Mythic | Warm, brisk | Tender, curious; Star Trek's hope, grave only when the times are |
| How ideas arrive | Jokes that are arguments | Myth, history, languages | The choices characters make | A plain fact set beside a small domestic one |
| Built for the ear | Yes: *Hitchhiker's* began as BBC radio | No | No | Yes |

- **Adams** sets the cosmically vast against the trivial (the Earth demolished for a bypass, the hero worried about tea) and follows an idea with deadpan logic until it turns absurd. Nearly every paragraph is a joke that is also a small piece of philosophy. Plot is loose; voice and ideas hold the reader.
- **Tolkien** builds the world from its past: invented languages, genealogies and legends told inside the story, with characters forever remembering older ages. His moral themes (power corrupts, mercy, the small person's courage) are carried by myth more than argument.
- **Rowling** teaches the world through one child's eyes, in plain, brisk, dialogue-heavy prose. Her density is plot: clues planted early and paid off books later. Exposition arrives through character, and morality is personal: choice over ability, love as protection, prejudice exposed.

## Where Adams uses narration, and where dialogue

- **Narration carries the idea:** how the universe works, its scale, its history, and the idea's logic followed to its end. The Guide's entries are the purest form, and he often cuts into one just as the plot is tense.
- **Dialogue carries people failing to keep up with it:** misunderstanding, talking past each other, small human worries set against huge events, bureaucrats being reasonable about something monstrous, and now and then two sides of an argument.
- **The meaning is in the gap** between the size of what the narration explains and the size of how people respond. This book has the same split: the narrator gives the shelter law or the injection, and the people say "That's for the cans" and "I've got four more plates". Adams' people misunderstand; ours deflect.

## What the book takes from Adams, and what it leaves

Agreed with the owner. The owner keeps coming back to Adams for his ideas, not for his dry British humour, which might be funny but doesn't fit this book.

**Take:**

1. **Ideas first.** Each chapter thinks through one idea (named in its outline entry), and its people and plot serve it.
2. **Narration carries the idea; dialogue carries how people respond to it,** as above. The meaning is in the gap.
3. **The explaining passage,** this book's Guide entry: the narrator lays out how something in the world works (the shelter law, the injection, the drying cupboard) as plain fact, beside the people it touches.
4. **An idea's logic followed to its end,** usually to what it costs a person: the immigration rule, followed to its end, is Ruth.
5. **Absurdity, as the book's one kind of humour,** under three conditions: it's the absurdity of systems and rules, never at a person's expense and never the universe as a joke; it's told dead straight, with no wink; and it lands as both funny and true, or it's cut. It's closer to *Catch-22* than to Adams, and it suits one voice reading flat, because the joke is in the logic, not the timing. The book already does it: the county attorney's opinion that the District is "in the community" (chapter 2), Lucille declaring Ruth Jewish to get her sealed food (chapter 2), the permit refused to someone who "can get treatment in her home country" (chapter 1). An example of the kind: *The levy that paid for the clinic needed a majority of the people who voted. Many of the people the clinic looked after did not vote, for the reasons the clinic was looking after them.* It will likely have more room in the later centuries, where the Federation's own rules are the system being tested.
6. **Digressions,** which may wander from the plot but never from the chapter's idea. The baby box going to every Finnish mother for generations, with grown-ups who know which year's box they slept in, is a digression about "a country is a decision people keep making".
7. **A confiding manner,** telling the listener what the characters don't notice ("You should know about the four bars. Ruth has played them every Sunday for a month, always while the call is on. Walt thinks she's practising."). It confides the present and the past, never what's coming, and it says "you", never "I": an "I" would make the narrator a character.
8. **Writing for the ear.** *Hitchhiker's* began on radio, and its sentences are built to be heard once.

**Leave:**

1. **Dry British humour:** deadpan understatement, irony, bathos, the joke as a passage's payoff. Answering the listener's question is where a confiding line slides into it ("a room that is only waiting is a waste of a room"), so that's where to watch.
2. **The narrator as a wit,** amused at the world. Ours is warm and curious.
3. **The universe as a joke,** and shrugging at meaning: the "42". This book holds Star Trek's hope.
4. **Satire's mockery.** His bureaucrats are figures of fun; ours are people, judged by what they do.
5. **Characters as types who misunderstand.** Ours understand perfectly, and deflect.
6. **A loose plot held up by voice.** Something open at the start still has to move by the end.

**Not Adams' at all, though he shares it:** the vast fact set beside the small domestic one is this book's own tool. Adams uses the small to shrink the vast, for comedy; this book uses it so the vast can be felt (the Europa mission and the orange crayon, the war-ready shelter and "He said, skis"). Lines like "because orange was the crayon she had" stay: they're tender, not ironic.

## What else the book takes from the others

- **Rowling's planted payoffs** are what "He said, skis" already does. A plant must be felt when it's planted, so it has to be audible to land by ear.
- **Tolkien's remembered history** fits a part of the core that hasn't had its turn: how each era tells the story of the ones before it. The later centuries are where it belongs.
- **Not to copy.** Rowling's close third person would end the book's many-lives, observer structure.

## The narrator's craft

Agreed with the owner, and applied in chapter 1's rewrite (`chapters/01/novel.txt`).

**More voice, but tender, never wry.** The narrator may confide in the listener and digress, within the limits above. It never reaches for a wry joke: warmth that makes you smile is fine ("because orange was the crayon she had"), irony isn't. The one humour is the absurdity of systems, told straight.

**What the tender edge is on the page.** It isn't adjectives. It's where the narrator looks, and what it's willing to say out loud:

- **It notices the smaller person in the room.** Nora holding the shelter door with both hands. Theo laughing without knowing why.
- **It names what nobody says,** as a fact: "Neither of them says so."
- **It gives history as a record, with full names and numbers side by side.** "Ruth Dorsey taught piano in the front room of that house for forty years. She has lived with her illness for fifty-two." The weight is in the two numbers, not in a word about them.
- **It knows what people know and remember, and rarely names what they feel.** Characters may say "I was so relieved"; the narrator says what Anna did, and when.
- **It never uses a word that tells the listener what to feel:** no sadly, cruelly, bravely, heartbreaking, poor.

**How much is too much.** World facts and stage business are welcome as long as each earns its place. The line that carries a moment's meaning (the carrying line) is different: **few, never two in a row,** each placed straight after the moment it carries, followed by a pause. Chapter 1 has five in twenty minutes. It's too much when it:

- **explains something the listener already heard land.** The novel test's "she knows what it means when he says fine three times" explains a joke that worked. Cut it.
- **generalizes from this person to all people.** An aphorism is Adams' move, or a preacher's. Keep it to Anna, not to "parents".
- **reaches into what's coming.** Never state what the listener knows is coming.

A dialogue tag earns its place when the voice would get the line's surface wrong without it: "That's for the cans," as if he's never seen the thing before, makes the lie audible. A tag that only decorates ("she says, grinning at him") is cut when the scene is tight.

**What a carrying line looks like.** The moment, then a plain fact about this person's life that the moment touches, then silence. From chapter 1:

- **Pittsburgh.** After "Do we know anybody in Pittsburgh?" and "No.": *Pittsburgh is a morning's drive from Dayton. Neither of them says so.*
- **The Finnish at dinner.** After Nora and Theo laugh at something in Finnish, and Nora tells her father it was nothing: *Ben has had five years of Tuesday night classes. He can read a card, slowly. He can't follow his children when they talk to each other at the speed of children. She lives in a language her parents can only visit.*
- **Ruth's county visit.** After "Well. Thank you. That's kind.": *For a year, when she was eleven, Anna sat on the stairs on Thursdays, waiting for someone to say those words. Today she is the one who says them.*
- **Bedtime.** After Anna answers Nora's "Why did she call you miss?" with the Finnish for it's nothing: *She says it the way her father says fine.* Three generations of hiding it, in one plain sentence.
- **When not to add one.** Ruth's "Does your mother know?" and Anna's "Yes. I told her." The surface is the meaning, and any narrator line after it would explain it.
