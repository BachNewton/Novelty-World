# Chapter 1: Dark by Four

It is Sunday, the eighth of November, two thousand and twenty-six. Anna Dorsey and Ben Novak have lived in Helsinki for five years.

It has been two years and two months since the riots in San Francisco, the ones that took their name from a man called Gabriel Bell, who died in them. In America the hearings go on. The National Guard is in two more cities than it was in the summer. The word Sanctuary, which used to be a kind word, is in the papers most days.

Elsewhere, there are other kinds of news. Ireland has been one country for two years. A French astronaut named Renée Picard has gone out to Europa, and the pictures from that mission are pinned up in classrooms all over the world, including one in Helsinki, where a seven-year-old has copied the moon in orange crayon, because orange was the crayon she had.

And in Helsinki, at sixty degrees north, under this much cloud, it is dark by four.

---

The flags were out this morning, on the schools and the churches, because in Finland the second Sunday of November is Father's Day. Ben, who is from Toledo, Ohio, was woken at seven by his daughter, with coffee he hadn't asked for and a card she wouldn't let him open until dinner.

It is a third-floor apartment in the north of the city. Five years ago they carried a two-year-old up these stairs. Now there are two children at the table and a third on the way, due in January, and in the hall there is a cardboard box from the Finnish state, full of baby clothes, with a mattress in the bottom, so that the box can be a bed.

The box has gone to every mother in Finland since nineteen forty-nine. The pattern on what's inside changes every year, so there are Finnish grown-ups who can tell you the year a baby was born from the pattern on its sleeping bag. A country decided, once, that every child here should start out in the same bed. It has gone on deciding it, every year since.

Nobody made them leave. Nothing had happened to them, in Ohio, in two thousand and twenty-one. They read the news, and looked at their daughter, and a job came up. On the forms they are third-country nationals. Neither of them can yet follow the evening news in Finnish, so they follow it in English, from home, on a phone.

Five days ago there was an election in America. They voted in it from this table, on ballots that went by post to a county in Ohio. In Finland, a foreigner who has lived here for two years may vote for the city council, though not for parliament. So they have a vote on the trams here, and a vote on the country there.

Ben was hired to write software. These days he mostly checks what the software writes. The team he left behind in Columbus had eleven people on it. It has two. The other nine are looking for work, in a country that has less of it each year.

---

At the table, Ben's phone lights up beside his plate, and he reads it before he can stop himself. Anna, cutting up Theo's food, waits for him to say it.

“They've sent the Guard into Pittsburgh,” Ben says.

“Do we know anybody in Pittsburgh?” Anna asks.

“No,” he says.

Pittsburgh is a morning's drive from Dayton. Neither of them says so.

Anna reaches over and turns his phone face down, and leaves her hand on it a moment. “Nora has been waiting all day,” she says. “Open your card.”

Nora has been waiting since seven, and she has decided that she will read it to him. Ben could read it himself. He has had five years of Tuesday night classes, and he can read a card, slowly. He hands it to her, and she holds it up with both hands, the way she has seen people read things out at school.

“Dear Dad,” Nora reads. “You are the best dad in Finland and in America. You are funny. You make good pancakes. You are not good at singing. Love, Nora.”

Ben can read enough of it to know that the part about singing isn't there. “Thank you, Nora,” he says. “That is a very good card.”

“I know,” says Nora.

Then she leans across to her brother and says something to him in Finnish, quickly, too quickly, the way two children talk, and Theo laughs so hard he has to put his fork down. Nora laughs too. Ben looks at Anna, and Anna shakes her head. She didn't catch it either.

“What was that?” Ben asks. “What's so funny?”

Nora thinks about it, and answers him in English. “Nothing.”

Ben can read a card, slowly. He can't follow his children when they talk to each other at the speed of children. Nobody had to teach Nora. She lives in a language her parents can only visit.

---

Under the building there is a room with concrete walls and a steel door, because Finnish law has long said that a building this size must have one. There is space in it for everyone who lives upstairs. In peacetime the residents keep their skis there, and their winter tires. The law says it must be possible to empty it and make it ready within seventy-two hours.

Ben brought the crib up from there this afternoon. Nora held the door, which took both of her hands. She asked him what the room was for. He said, skis.

---

At six, the tablet goes up against the fruit bowl. Dayton, Ohio is seven hours behind, so it is eleven in the morning there. The Sunday call has been made every week for five years, and on the other end, Walt Dorsey is already waiting, his chair pulled in close, his face filling the screen.

“There's my girl,” Walt says. “How's that baby doing? You look good. You look big.”

“Thank you, Dad,” says Anna. “Happy Father's Day.”

Walt tells her that Father's Day is in June, and Anna tells him that here it's today, and they put the flags out for it. Walt shakes his head at Finland, as he does most weeks. “Dark at four, and Father's Day in November, and you people pay taxes for this. Well, tell Ben I said happy Father's Day. Where's my Nora?”

Nora leans into the picture, already on her way out. “Hi, Grandpa. I have to take a bath now.”

“Well, you go on, then,” Walt tells her. “That's the important thing.”

When she's gone, he leans in closer. “Have you got a name yet, for that baby?”

“We've got a list,” Anna says. “It has to work in two languages.”

“Walter works,” says Walt.

“Not in either one, Dad.”

Walter Dorsey caught polio in the summer of nineteen fifty-two, when he was five. It was the worst year for polio in American history. He went on to work thirty-four years at Delco, on a steel brace, on one good leg. Decades on, the disease can come back for the muscles it spared. It has come back for the good leg.

“So what's new?” Anna asks him. “How's the house? How's Mom?”

“Oh, everything's fine,” Walt says. “We had rain Tuesday, so I didn't get to the gutters, but they'll keep. The Henderson boy brought the cans up. Your mother's fine. She's been in with her piano. Everything's fine here.”

Anna looks past him, into the hall. “Dad, what's that by the door behind you? Is that a walker?”

Walt glances back at it, as if he has never seen it before. “That's for the cans.”

“The Henderson boy brings up the cans,” Anna says.

“Well, there you go,” says Walt. “It's not getting much use.”

From somewhere behind him there is a piano. Four bars of something, then it stops, and the same four bars again. Then it stops, and there are footsteps. Ruth Dorsey taught piano in the front room of that house for forty years. She has lived with her illness for fifty-two.

You should know about the four bars. On Sundays, Ruth plays them while the call is on. Walt thinks she's practising.

“Walter, who is that?” Ruth asks, from the doorway. “Who are you talking to?”

“It's Annie, Ruthie,” Walt says. “It's Sunday. Come and say hello to Annie.”

“Hi, Mom,” says Anna. “You look nice. I like that sweater.”

Ruth bends to the screen and looks at her daughter for a long moment, politely, the way you look at someone you are meeting. “Oh. I'm sorry, Walter, you might have told me. Good morning, miss. You've come from the county.”

Walt starts to tell her who it is, and Ruth waves him quiet. “No, it's quite all right. You're welcome to look at anything you like. I keep a clean kitchen. Walter, this young woman has come all this way and nobody has offered her a thing.”

It was the autumn of nineteen ninety-six. Ruth was in the hospital for eleven weeks, and when she came home, a woman from the county came to the house on Thursdays, for a year, with a clipboard, to see whether the child should stay. Anna was eleven. She used to watch from the stairs.

“I take my medicine,” Ruth says. “You can write that down. My daughter's at her lesson just now, or you could ask her yourself. She's fed and she's clean, and she goes to school every day with a lunch I make for her. She is a very good girl. She doesn't need to go anywhere.”

“I know she is,” Anna says.

“You'll tell them that.”

“I'll tell them,” says Anna. “I'm writing it down right now, Mrs. Dorsey. You're doing a very good job. She's staying right where she is.”

“Well,” says Ruth. “Thank you. That's kind.”

For a year, when she was eleven, Anna sat on the stairs on Thursdays, waiting for someone to say those words. Today she is the one who says them.

Ruth looks at her more kindly now. “You look tired, dear. How far along are you?”

“Seven months,” Anna says.

“And you're on your feet, going around to people's houses. Does your mother know?”

“Yes,” says Anna. “I told her.”

“Good,” says Ruth. “You want your mother for that.”

She goes back to the front room. After a moment, there are the four bars again.

“Don't mind that,” Walt says. “That's a bad morning, is all. She'd know you most days. She's been a little off since the clinic closed, and it's the time change, it gets her turned around.”

“Since what closed?” Anna asks.

Walt looks at her as if he hasn't heard, and she asks him again. “Dad. Dolores's clinic, on Wayne Avenue? That clinic?”

“Well, the levy went down in the spring,” Walt says, “so the county lost its money or some such. I voted for the darn thing. There was a sign on the door in September. Dolores called me herself. Nineteen years she's been giving your mother that shot, and she cried on the phone. It's nothing to do with us. They sent a letter.”

The levy that paid for the clinic needed a majority of the people who voted. Many of the people the clinic looked after did not vote, for the reasons the clinic was looking after them.

“So who's giving her the shot?” Anna asks.

“There's a fella at the county,” says Walt. “He says there's a place in Columbus, if she gets on the list. And he says there are other places, for people who can't manage. Downtown. I told him we're managing.”

Downtown, between the river and the rail line, the county has put up a fence. What is inside it is called the Sanctuary District.

“Columbus is an hour and a half away, Dad,” Anna says. “You don't drive on the highway anymore.”

“I've got a call in,” says Walt. “Annie, I've got a call in. You look after that baby. You don't need this. It's Father's Day, isn't it? Everything's fine here.”

His hand gives out on the tablet, and it tips back, and for a few seconds the two of them look together at the ceiling fan in Dayton. Then the call is gone.

The medicine Ruth Dorsey takes comes as an injection, once a month. It leaves the body slowly, over weeks. A person can miss one and seem all right. A person can miss a second and seem a little off. In the bathroom in Helsinki, Ben is running the water, and asking a four-year-old to please sit down.

---

At bedtime, Nora is still thinking about it. “Mom? I heard Grandma. Why did she call you miss?”

Anna tucks the blanket in along her side, and tells her, in Finnish, that it's nothing. She gets every sound of it right.

“That was good, Mom,” says Nora.

She says it the way her father says fine.

---

Over nearly every kitchen sink in Finland there is a cupboard with no floor. You wash a plate and put it in the rack inside, and it drips into the sink behind a closed door. A woman worked it out in the nineteen-forties, to give back some of the hours a housewife's life went to dishes.

Anna has lived with one of these cupboards for five years. She takes each plate out and dries it with a towel. Her mother did.

Ben comes in from the children's room. “They're both down,” he says. “You don't have to dry those, you know.”

“September,” says Anna.

“What?”

“It closed in September,” she says. “That's nine Sundays. Nine Sundays he sat there and told me about the gutters.”

“How was she?” Ben asks.

“She thought I was from the county. The Thursday lady. She told me what a good girl I was, and could I please not take me away. So I said I wouldn't. I told her I'd put it in the report.”

“Oh, Anna,” Ben says.

“Don't,” she says. “I've got four more plates.”

“Okay,” says Ben. “So we find someone. There has to be a home nurse, or an agency, somebody who comes to the house. We can pay for that. That's a thing we can actually do.”

“Who?” says Anna. “Tell me who. I've been standing here trying to think of one name. Everybody I knew there is either sixty-five or in Arizona. It took Dolores four years to get her to take that shot, Ben. She won't let a stranger near her.”

“I know,” Ben says.

“You know what happens here when a clinic closes?” Anna says. “A letter comes. From the city, in three languages, with the new address and the tram that goes there. In Dayton a sign goes up on a door. It's the same species, Ben. They just decided differently.”

“We pay for that letter,” Ben says. “Have you seen my paycheck?”

“I'd pay double. Every child in this country has had a free hot lunch at school since my dad was a baby, and his county can't keep one nurse in one room.”

She puts a plate away, and takes out another. “I could go,” she says. “For a month, just to set something up. They let you fly until thirty-six weeks.”

“You're at thirty-one,” says Ben. “So that's a month, and then you're in Ohio at thirty-five weeks, with your dad and his cans, and nobody to help you.”

“I know what thirty-five weeks is,” Anna says.

“I just mean, what if you get there and something happens, and I can't get you back?”

“Flights don't stop, Ben.”

“No,” he says. “I know they don't.”

He picks up a towel, and dries a glass, and puts it back in the cupboard with no floor. “I don't know what to tell you,” he says. “I never had this. My folks were gone before we left. I left two headstones in Toledo. They don't call on Sundays.”

“There's a word for her now,” says Anna. “Did you know that? I heard a man use it on the news, on the American feed, right out loud, a man in a tie. Dim. Like a bulb.”

She dries the same plate again. “He said, well, the dims have to go somewhere. And I thought, huh. And I went and got the kids their lunch. I made their lunch.”

“That's why we left, Anna,” Ben says. “That is exactly why we left.”

“I know why we left. I filled out the forms. You wanted to give it another year, and I sat at my mother's kitchen table with a laptop and filled out every form, with her playing in the next room, and I said, I am not raising Nora here. That was me.”

“And you were right,” Ben says.

“Don't.”

“No, you were. Our daughter just told her brother a joke in a language I can't follow, and then she went to bed, in a place where nobody has ever once had to teach her how to hide under a desk. You did that. I'm not going to say it wasn't right.”

“I know it was right,” Anna says. “I'd do it again. If you put me back at that table tomorrow, I'd fill it out again. That's the part I stand here thinking about.”

“We asked them to come,” says Ben.

“We asked them once. On the porch. He said who would do the tomatoes. And she had Dolores. So I said, okay, Dad. I was so relieved. I said okay, and I went inside, and I was so relieved.”

“Say we asked again,” Ben says. “I'm not saying no. I'm asking, what does she get here? It's dark at four. Nobody at the pharmacy can talk to her. On a bad Sunday she doesn't know who you are. At least there she knows the house.”

“She has a piano there,” says Anna.

“We could get a piano.”

“Up those stairs?”

“I'd carry it,” says Ben.

Neither of them says anything for a while. Then Ben hangs his towel on the oven door. “Come to bed. We've got the clinic on Tuesday. We have a baby in January, Anna.”

“I know when we have a baby,” Anna says. “In a minute. Go on. I'm just going to finish these.”

---

She finishes them. She hangs up the towel. Then she sits down at the table, between Nora's orange moon on the refrigerator and the box in the hall, and she opens the laptop, and goes to the website of the Finnish Immigration Service, which offers its pages in English.

It is ten o'clock. In Dayton it is three in the afternoon, and still light.

The page she finds is for family members other than a spouse or a child. It says an elderly parent may be considered dependent on a relative in Finland. It says that financial dependence alone is not enough. It says a permit is not usually granted to someone who can get treatment in her home country.

She reads that sentence again.

Further down, it explains how to apply. The form can be filled out online. But the applicant must then go, in person, to prove who she is.
