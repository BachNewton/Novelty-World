# Chapter 12: Known As

Six months after the crew came home, on the night of Tuesday the thirteenth of August, nobody governed Dayton.

Sergeant Joel Flory watched it happen from the gate of the Sanctuary District. At four that afternoon the Safety Board's cars went past on the river road toward Columbus, with boxes of files on their back seats. The Board, commissioners and pastors, had run the city for the Governor since the spring, and a fight was coming that a board would be no use in. The Stays, Washington's side, were coming down from the counties to the north and out from the air base on the east side of town. At dusk the Governor's own Guard came in the other way, trucks and then school buses, to hold the city instead. The power had been off for a week. Up and down every street, people sat out on their porches in the dark to see whose trucks would come first.

At eight a runner came to the gate for Joel.

The colonel had set up a folding table in the lobby of the county building on Third Street, behind the glass doors Joel had once stood in front of all night on election night, with his back to the road. He had a head cold, a box of tissues on the table, and a way of apologising each time he used one. Before anything else he asked Joel whether his men had eaten.

Then he gave him a typed list. By eight in the morning he wanted three things on this table: the keys to the water plant, the keys to the courthouse, and the rolls of the Sanctuary District.

The District lay on the line he meant to hold, and he wanted it empty before the Stays got there. He would empty it by its letters. The county had given every resident one: E for those who could work if anybody would hire them, D for those it had judged couldn't look after themselves. Since the spring there had been a third. The D's would go to the state hospital in Columbus. The E's would go out of the gate, wherever they liked. The H's would go to Columbus too, under guard, to be held there until the Stays would trade for the Governor's people they had in the jail at Troy.

Joel had signed for the buses that afternoon, eleven of them, before he asked what they were for.

"I'm told the county kept no rolls after its computers went dark," said the colonel, and apologised, and blew his nose. "I'm told there's a book, and you have it."

Joel said he did.

"Eight o'clock," said the colonel.

Everybody in the column knew one other thing about him. At the armory in Springfield the week before, two of his drivers had sold a tank of the column's diesel to a farmer, and he'd had them shot in the yard before supper. A column that steals from itself, he had said, is finished.

---

Gary Pruitt was on the steps outside, with his folder under his arm. The county hadn't paid him since January. He came in every day anyway, because nobody had told him to stop, and because there was nobody else left who knew where anybody was.

"I told him," he said. "He asked who had the rolls. I don't know how to not tell a man a thing he asks me."

"I know, Gary."

"I know what's in that book." Pruitt looked out at the dark street. "Thirty years, every person I ever got out of anywhere, I got out with a piece of paper that was true. Mrs. Dorsey got her shot on a piece of paper. The day the papers stop being true, nobody can find anybody." He moved the folder to his other arm. "If it isn't on his table at eight, it goes in my notebook. I write everything down, son. I'd rather you heard it from me first."

---

Joel walked back to the river in the dark.

He could leave. He thought about it honestly, the whole way. The roads north were open until first light, and nobody would look for one sergeant in a city with no lights. But the book would stay behind in his pack, or go with him, and either way the colonel would fill his buses in the morning, by the letters or by looking, and it would be Cody and the rest of the squad who did the filling, with nobody standing in front of them.

After Gate 12A the Guard had kept Joel off the line. A hero who wouldn't say the other side fired first was no use to anybody's cameras. In March, when the Board took the city and needed men on the District's fence, it got Joel's squad, and Joel got the gate. In his pack was his mother's ledger, kept dry, as she had asked.

He kept it the way she had. For every new face at the gate he wrote down the county's name for them and their letter, because the gate needed those. Then he asked his mother's question: *What do people call you, when they like you?* Most people looked at him as if nobody had asked them anything in years. Then they told him.

In April the pastor from Salem Avenue had come to the gate, the one his mother had sung tenor under, with a typed page and two men from the Board behind him. Somebody had bombed the substation, he said. People had been taking twice at the warehouses, so the Board had asked for a signature with each ration card, and then, after the substation, a pledge to the count. Some people wouldn't sign it. They would have to wait somewhere while their cases were reviewed, and the District already had a fence and a form.

"Can your form take one more letter, Joel?" said the pastor. He looked as if he hadn't slept since the substation. "H. For held."

Joel had written the H's himself, in his own hand, in his mother's book.

Miss Bea Yancey came to the gate in May with her purse on her arm. She had run the children's room at the main library for thirty-one years, and when the Board sent it a list of books to take off the shelves, she carried them to the church basement instead. The Board asked her where. She wouldn't say, and she wouldn't sign.

At the gate she looked at the ledger open on his arm.

"Which column do you want me in, Joel?"

He had put her in the first with an H, and in the second as *Miss Bea*.

He had stayed on the Governor's side all the same. In March, in Troy, men on the Stays' side had pulled a woman out of her own pharmacy because her license said M, and put her in the river with wire round her wrists, and the sheriff there charged nobody. His mother's license said M now.

---

Lucille Odom was awake. She mostly was, since the District's insulin ran out in June and the sore on her foot stopped healing.

When the trucks from Indiana stopped crossing the state line with the meal trays, the Board had let her at a stove at last, and she ran the kitchen of Building C on whatever came out of the county's warehouses and whatever the neighbourhood could carry over. She had one rule in her line. "Nobody is anything in my line, baby. You're hungry or you're not. Move up." At three every afternoon, whatever was happening, she sat down outside the kitchen door with her book of crosswords and did one in pen, and the line waited.

She had her bad foot up on a sack of wheat when Joel came in with the lantern.

The wheat had come in April, on a grain truck, driven by a woman in the white head covering of the Old German Baptist Brethren. Joel had last seen his aunt Naomi when he was nine, the summer his mother's two names went into the newspaper. Her church kept no television and no internet, so she may have been the one person in Ohio who had never seen his face on a screen. She knew who he was all the same.

"The elder says to bring it to the door and ask nobody what they are," she said. There had been twenty-eight sacks when she set out. At the first checkpoint they asked which side the church was on, and Brother Landis said it hadn't got one, so they took four. Then the other side took four. "That's the fare," said Naomi. "We don't resist it."

She didn't ask after his mother. She couldn't bring herself to say the name his mother had chosen, and his mother had asked her never to say the other one, so she said neither. She climbed back up into the cab.

"Mother asks if you're eating," she said.

Ever since his mother left the church, that sentence had come to Carol through Naomi, the only thing that ever came from the farm. Now it had come to Joel. That was when he learned where his mother had got *eat something*.

---

He put the colonel's list on Lucille's table, and the ledger beside it, and told her.

Lucille read the list twice, with her finger. "That hospital in Columbus. Who's paying it?"

"Nobody, since January."

"Mm." She knew who was a D in Building C; she cut up every one of their meals herself. "Give it to him, baby."

He looked at her.

"You're one boy. They'll sort us with that book or without it." She took her foot off the sack and set it on the floor, carefully. "They shot two of their own over diesel. I heard."

"I could open the gate."

"And they go where? Half of C can't walk to the corner. The other side's coming up Third Street by the weekend." She shook her head. "And they shoot you anyhow."

"I could burn it."

"Then burn it."

Joel shook his head. "Gary knows I had it, so it's a lie anyway, and then nothing's left anywhere that says who was in here. There are people in this book who died in here, Miss Lucille. Nothing else says they were." He put his hand on it. "Mrs. Dorsey's got a letter from Finland that'll take her in because she's in here. Burn this, and there's nothing to say she ever was."

Lucille waited.

"And I wrote the H's," he said. "In my writing. He'd be filling those buses out of my handwriting, and I'd stand at the gate tomorrow and watch Cody put Miss Bea on one, and tell myself the colonel did it." He kept his hand where it was. "After the shooting at the base they wanted me to say I returned fire. I said I fired. If they go on those buses, I did that too."

He opened the ledger at the first page and ran his finger down the second column.

"What people call you, when they like you," he said. "That's all anybody who comes looking for you needs to know. It's no use at all to a man with a bus."

---

At the back of the ledger were more than a hundred blank pages his mother had ruled and never needed. Joel fetched the long steel rule from the old intake office. Lucille pressed it flat across the blank pages with both hands, a hand's width from the spine, and leaned her weight on it, and Joel ran her box cutter down its edge, a few pages at each pass, then the green cloth cover, until what was left at the back was a narrow book, with room on each line for one thing and no other.

Then he began at the beginning and copied the second column into it, line by line, and read each name aloud as he wrote it.

"Big Mike. Sister. Miss Odessa. Doc. Baby Ray. Grandma Tran. The Reverend. Sugar."

Some lines in the second column were empty, where his mother had never been told a name, or he hadn't. He left an empty line for each of them. It was all that would be left of that person, and he wouldn't have them left out.

"Ti Jak." He had come in from Springfield in the winter, after the government ended the papers that let Springfield's Haitians work, and the factory let him go, and then his landlord did. At the gate he had said *Jak*, so that Joel would manage it. Joel asked what they called him at home, and he laughed and said *Ti Jak*, which meant Little Jak. He was six foot three. He had kept goal for a Haitian team in Springfield's Sunday league, and in the District he kept goal for the children against the wall of Building A, between two chalk marks, and let in exactly as many as each of them needed.

"The Henderson boy." Caleb was nineteen, the boy who had found Mrs. Dorsey's husband on his kitchen floor three years before. He wouldn't sign a pledge that called his father, who worked on the base, a traitor, so he was an H, and since the pumps stopped he had carried water up the stairs of Building C all day for anybody who asked. At the gate he had said, "I don't know, sir. The Henderson boy, I guess."

"What'd you put for me?" said Lucille.

"Miss Lucille."

"Hm," said Lucille, pleased.

Around three in the morning Mrs. Dorsey came into the kitchen.

She was in her nightgown and her good coat, with her husband's cap from the car-parts plant on over her white hair, and a cloth in her hand. She had not had her shot since May. In June Dolores, who had given it to her for more than twenty years, went out of the gate to look for more of it at a pharmacy she knew of across the lines. Joel had opened the gate for her himself. He had eaten at her table every Wednesday of his life, and fought his mother there over the last spring roll. *Back Thursday*, she'd said, and patted his cheek as if he were still twelve. Every Monday since, Mrs. Dorsey had come to the visitors' window in her coat and asked whether it was Dolores's day, and every Monday Joel had told her, "Not today, Mrs. Dorsey," because he couldn't think what else was true.

Since then she had stopped sleeping, and then she had begun cleaning. She wiped the clean counter, end to end.

"You'll want to see the cupboards," she said to Lucille, very politely. "Everybody from the county does. My daughter's at school. She takes a lunch I make her."

"Yes, ma'am," said Lucille. "I'll put that down."

"She's clean. She's fed."

"Have you eaten, Mrs. Dorsey?"

Mrs. Dorsey looked at the pot on the stove and the stack of trays, and her face closed. She ate only what she had heard being opened. Lucille got up, went to the back of the pantry, and came back with a can of peaches. She opened it at the counter, where Mrs. Dorsey could hear the opener go in, and gave it to her with a fork.

Mrs. Dorsey ate the peaches standing up, every one, in her husband's cap.

"They're very good," she said. "You can put that in your report."

When she had gone, Lucille sat down and looked at the empty can.

"Those were my peaches," she said. She had been saving them all year. When this was over, she had told Joel once, she was going to eat a whole can of peaches by herself, standing up, and not tell a soul.

Joel found the line. *Dorsey, Ruth (D). Mrs. Dorsey. Daughter, Anna, in Finland. Lessons, Tues & Thurs. Her arm is Dolores's.* Under it, in his own pencil, *Husband died, 10 Feb.*, and *Dolores out, June.* There was no line for Dolores coming back.

He wrote *Mrs. Dorsey* in the narrow book, and went on.

At the very back of the ledger, where his mother kept the things that had no column, was a card. THANK YOU FOR YOUR SON, it said on the front, and on the back, in her writing, *Eli Kasprzak, 20.* It was too wide for the book now. He folded it in half and put it back, and said the name once, aloud, the way the boy's mother had said it on the news.

When the sky over the river began to go grey he came to the last line. He wrote one more under it, and closed the book before the ink was dry. Inside the back cover he wrote an address in Toronto.

"If anybody's ever going north," he said, "it goes to my mother."

Then he cut out every page his mother had written, close to the spine, and burned them a handful at a time in the barrel behind Building C. At the edge of the lot Gary Pruitt sat in his car, where a phone could still find a signal, and watched the smoke go up, and wrote down the time.

Lucille put the narrow book in her apron.

"In twenty-six years," she said, "nobody ever searched a kitchen."

While Joel washed his face at her sink, she read the colonel's list again. Beside the rolls of the District, in Joel's hand, there were two words now.

"That's not a lie, then," she said. "You burned them. There aren't any."

"It's a lie, Miss Lucille," said Joel. "I'm the reason there aren't."

---

At eight on Wednesday morning, at the folding table in the lobby, Sergeant Flory, shaved, with his boots done, handed the colonel his list. Beside the keys to the water plant he had signed his name, and beside the keys to the courthouse. Beside the rolls of the Sanctuary District he had written *None available.*

At noon Gary Pruitt came to the same table with a spiral notebook. It had the book in it, and who had kept it, and the lantern in the kitchen of Building C all night, and the time of the smoke.

They asked the sergeant where the book was. Each time, he said what he had written. The hearing took eleven minutes. The colonel had a city to hold and a fight coming to it, and on his first morning in it a famous sergeant had lied to him in writing. He needed Dayton to see that a famous name bought nothing. He signed the order, and apologised for his cold.

Pruitt's hand shook as he put the notebook away.

"I said I'd tell them," he said.

"You did, Gary," said Joel.

They gave him the afternoon in a room off the lobby with a soldier at the door. His hands wouldn't keep still, and he sat on them. He found he wanted his mother, plainly, as he hadn't wanted anybody since he was small, and he was glad she was in Toronto, where nobody could make her watch. He thought about writing to her. He had already written the one thing.

At four they walked him up the levee behind the county building. The wind was off the river, and on it he could smell Miss Lucille's kitchen, the wheat boiling for supper. She had always said that if they ever let her at a stove, you'd smell it from the river. He was thinking that she'd been right.

Soldiers of the Ohio Guard shot Joel Flory there, for making a false record. They were his own side. On the telephone poles along Third Street there were still posters of his face from the winter before last, with HERO under it. He was twenty-six.

---

At three o'clock Lucille had sat down outside the kitchen door with her crossword and her pen, and the line had waited. She didn't fill in a square. She was still sitting there at four, when a sound came across the water, a lot of rifles all at once, and then nothing.

She put the cap on her pen. Then she got up on the bad foot and went in and stirred the pot, because there were four hundred people to feed, and the line moved up.

It was a Wednesday. At seven Miss Bea came over from Building F with the people who sang on Wednesdays, and they gathered in the day room by the piano, where Mrs. Dorsey was sitting. She began the slow hymn she used to play at the end of every Sunday call to Finland. Partway through she stopped, in the middle, and took her hands off the keys and laid them in her lap.

Miss Bea hummed the note. Fourteen people sang hymn number five thirty-four, "Be Still, My Soul," in four parts, with no piano and no one on the tenor line. Mrs. Dorsey sat on the bench with her hands in her lap and listened to every verse.

---

The colonel couldn't sort them.

Months of moving to wherever the water still ran had mixed everybody up, and every one of them, when a soldier asked, gave a name. So on Thursday the soldiers filled the buses by looking at people, the way people do. They took men who looked to them like Stays, and people who looked to them as if they belonged in a hospital. They took Ti Jak, because he looked to somebody as if he came from somewhere else. He was carrying a sack of wheat to the kitchen when they stopped him, and he put it down carefully against the wall before he went.

On the stairs of Building C a soldier of nineteen stopped Caleb with a can of water in each hand and asked his name. Caleb said, "The Henderson boy, I guess," and the soldier let him go on up.

Mrs. Dorsey was not on the buses. Lucille had put her in an apron that morning and sat her at the sinks with the women peeling potatoes, and nobody looks twice at a woman peeling potatoes. Fewer than a hundred people went to Columbus. By the weekend the fighting had reached the north side of town, and the buses never came back for the rest.

---

In October the column was needed somewhere else. No one had ever ordered the Montgomery County Sanctuary District closed, and no one ever did. It was getting cold, and steel would buy flour. Over nine days the residents took the fence down themselves, a panel at a time, and carried it across the river to the scrap yard. Miss Bea walked down Salem Avenue to the church basement and her books. Lucille stayed in her kitchen and cooked from a stool, and people walked in off the street to her line from every side, because there was no fence to stop them.

That month, in a meetinghouse of Naomi's church, the sheriff who had charged nobody in Troy met a man from what was left of the Board, and they agreed that a truck with wheat in it could pass. Neither of them called it peace, and it held.

Naomi came with the first truck. Lucille met her at the kitchen door and told her there were old people inside who had nobody.

Naomi looked at the ground between them, and took her time.

"Then they'll have us," she said.

She took nine of them north, Mrs. Dorsey among them. Lucille gave her the narrow book from her apron and showed her the address in the back. "It's his," she said. "It goes to his mother."

On the truck Mrs. Dorsey told Naomi that she knew perfectly well who had sent her. At the farm she would eat nothing she hadn't heard opened, and Naomi's kitchen ran on jars she had put up herself, every one with a lid that popped when it came off. Mrs. Dorsey listened for the pop, and ate. On the first evening she sat at the kitchen table, in a house that had never held an instrument, and spread her hands on the oilcloth, and Naomi's granddaughter watched her from the doorway.

A week later Naomi sat at the same table with the narrow book. Beside each of her nine she squeezed in, in pencil, her own name, as the one responsible for them, and where they were. Beside *Mrs. Dorsey* she added one word more. Then she wrapped the book in brown paper, copied the address from the back cover, and posted it to Toronto from the post office in town, without a letter.

---

In Helsinki, Anna rang Toronto every Sunday at six in the evening, eleven in the morning in Dayton, because it was the hour she had always rung, and there was nobody left in Dayton to ring.

The last message from Dolores had come in June: *Going to find her more. Back Thursday.* After that there was nothing, and Anna had stopped telling herself that the lines were down. She knew from it that Ruth had gone without her shot since May. She had written to every office in Columbus that would take a letter, asking where the District's residents had gone. The two that answered said the same two words: *none available*.

She kept their letters in the hall drawer, under her passport and the letter from Finland that nobody had used. Nora had stopped asking when Grandma was coming. She asked instead, on Sundays, whether Carol had found anything, and Anna said, "Not yet," because it was the truest thing she had.

---

The parcel reached Carol on a Wednesday at the end of October, at Joy's kitchen table in Toronto, an hour before choir.

Carol had work now, three days a week at a settlement office on Bloor Street, helping people newer to Canada than she was through its forms, and she asked each of them first what they liked to be called. On Wednesdays she sang tenor in the church down the road. In August, the week the news came, she had gone to choir anyway, and stood in the tenor line, and not sung a note, and nobody had asked her to.

She knew her sister's hand on the brown paper before she saw the postmark. There was no letter, only the book.

She knew the green cloth spine too, cut down to the width of her hand. In August her phone had told her that her son was shot for a false record. She hadn't known until now what the record was.

Every line was in Joel's writing. *Big Mike. Sister. Miss Odessa.* Halfway down the first page it said *Mrs. Dorsey*, and beside it, in her sister's pencil, *With N. Garber, the farm,* and a word after that, which she would come back to.

Folded in half inside the back cover was the card from her front step, with the boy's name on the back in her own writing. Joel had kept him too.

She turned to the last page. Under the last name, in ballpoint, in the book's one column, Joel had written another.

*Mom.*

He had called her Carol since he was nine, because the other words hadn't fit. She sat at Joy's table with her hand flat on the page, and Joy came in from the hall and saw her face, and sat down across from her without a word, and stayed. Carol had spent her life filling in boxes for other people. Nobody had ever filled in that one for her.

After a while she looked at the clock. It was half past eleven at night in Helsinki. She rang anyway, and Anna answered on the first ring.

"I've got a book," said Carol. "It only answers one question. What do people call her, Anna, when they like her?"

There was a silence from Helsinki, and then Anna's voice, not steady.

"Her pupils call her Mrs. Dorsey. My father called her Ruthie. She said he wasn't to be encouraged."

"Yes," said Carol. "I've got her right here. Page one."

She ran her finger along the line to her sister's pencil.

"She went north with my sister, to the farm," she said. "It says here she's eating."
