# Chapter 10: Gate 12A

The polls in Ohio closed at half past seven, and by eight Sergeant Joel Flory had been standing in front of the county's glass doors long enough to stop feeling his feet.

The Governor had sent the Guard to the boards of elections after the threats began in the summer, and Dayton's board was in a grey office building on Third Street, a few blocks from the river. Joel's platoon stood in a line across the front of it, in helmets, rifles held low, with the county's seal on the glass behind them. Inside, by morning, every ballot in the county would be counted, with a person from each party at every table. That was the whole of what they were guarding: rooms full of paper, and the people counting it.

Across Third Street, in a parking lot, thirty or forty men had rifles of their own.

They had come in ones and twos since dark, with flags on poles, and a pickup had pulled up on the curb with its horn held down, so that the sound went on until it became a kind of weather. Somebody over there had a radio, and all evening it had been saying that trucks were coming in the back of the building with boxes.

Cody Ruhl stood at Joel's left elbow. He was nineteen, and had joined for the college money, as Joel had at seventeen, and he had never in his life stood across a road from men with guns. Every few minutes he moved his grip on his rifle and then moved it back.

"Easy," said Joel, without looking at him.

"I'm easy, Sarge."

At the end of the line, Lieutenant Mercer said, loud enough to carry, "Let one of them try it." He was twenty-six, and his wife was due in February, and between saying things like that he took his phone out of his sleeve pocket and looked at it, in case she had written, and put it back.

At twenty to nine a car turned in off Third Street toward the side of the building.

Somebody across the road shouted, "There," and the whole crowd came to the curb at once. Three men stepped down into the road with their rifles up at their shoulders, and Joel heard, all along his own line, the small sounds of young men doing the same: a strap, a heel on concrete, a breath.

He stepped out one pace in front of the line and turned his back on the road.

"Fingers out," he said. "Out of the guard. Look at me. Don't look at them. Look at me."

"Sergeant," said Mercer. "Get back in line."

Joel didn't. He looked at Cody, whose finger was where it should not have been, and kept looking until it came out.

The car stopped. Two women got out of it, both somewhere past seventy, one in a red coat and one in a blue one, with lanyards round their necks. Between them they lifted a grey metal box out of the back seat, and then they stood there holding it, with forty rifles pointed in their general direction, and didn't move.

Then the one in the red coat said something to the one in the blue, and they came up the walk together, carrying the box between them, at the pace of two women with bad knees, straight through the middle of the soldiers and in at the glass doors.

Nobody fired. The men in the road looked at each other and climbed back onto the curb. One of them laughed, too loudly. The horn stopped for a minute, and started again.

"I had it on," said Cody. His voice had gone high. "Sarge. I had my finger on it."

"I know. Now it's off. Leave it off."

An hour later a man came across the road on his own, with his hands open. Joel knew him by sight by then: forty or so, in a hunting jacket, who had held his rifle all evening like a thing he'd been told he would need. He had leaned it against the pickup. He stopped in the middle of the road.

"Back across," said Mercer.

Joel walked down to the curb.

"Was that the trucks?" said the man. "The boxes?"

"That was one polling place," said Joel. "They bring them two to a car. One from each party. The law says they have to ride together, so neither one's ever alone with it."

The man looked at the doors.

"The one in the red coat and the one in the blue," said Joel.

"My girl's at home with a fever," said the man. He said it as if it were the answer to something. Then he went back across the road and stood by the pickup with his rifle again, among the others, and when Joel looked for him near one in the morning the place by the pickup was empty, and he never knew whether the man had gone home or only somewhere else.

At one every phone across the road lit up at once. In Washington, the candidate of the President's party had come out in front of his flags and said that the count in four states was corrupt, that he had won, and that no count would be accepted after tonight. The men in the parking lot cheered and leaned on their horns, and some drove away and some stayed until it was light. Nobody on Third Street fired a shot that night.

Joel stood in front of the doors until dawn. He had learned something on that curb, and he believed it: a thing like this held if somebody stood in front of it and said *look at me*.

---

He spent most of the winter on state duty, guarding buildings full of paper, and watched the country come apart on the television in the armory's day room.

In the four states, the count said one man had won and the legislatures said the other, and each sent its own answer to Congress. In January Congress took the legislatures' word, and half of it walked out and went to Philadelphia. By then people called Philadelphia's side the Counts, and Washington's the Stays, after the court orders its lawyers kept asking for to stay the count, which is the law's word for stop. Both sides put signs in their yards. On the twentieth, at noon, the company watched two men swear the same oath at once, on two screens a sergeant had set side by side: one on the Capitol steps in Washington, one outside the old hall in Philadelphia where the Declaration had been signed. Nearly every man in the room had voted for the one in Philadelphia, and they cheered him, and booed the other, and then went quiet, because neither picture went away.

Ohio split along its own seams. The Governor, the doctor who had stood on everyone's television in the first spring of the virus with the day's dead, said that Ohio stood with Philadelphia and the count. The legislature voted that it stood with Washington, and so did most of the counties, and the federal bases answered to Washington as they always had. One of those was Wright-Patterson, the great Air Force base on the edge of Dayton, where half of Joel's street had worked at one time or another.

Washington's side had said a great many things since November about people like Joel's mother. The man it swore in had promised, among other things, to find every one of them in the government's records and correct them.

---

On the Sunday morning, Washington called Ohio's Guard into federal service, as a President had done in California in the summer of 2025, over its governor's head. On Sunday night the Governor countermanded it. On Monday evening the captain stood on a chair in the drill hall of the armory in Springfield, half an hour from Dayton, and read both papers aloud.

"Washington says we report to the nearest federal base by noon tomorrow. That's Wright-Patterson. The Governor says we don't, because we're Ohio's. And Philadelphia's asked her to hold the base's gates until it answers to Philadelphia, so that's where she's sending us." He got down off the chair. "We go up to Gate 12A at six. Enough of us on the road, a paper in a sleeve, and a colonel on the other side decides he's got better things to do with his career. Nobody here is going to shoot anybody. Anyone who won't go, see me tonight. You'll be held here for a court-martial. I won't think less of you, and it won't matter what I think."

A dozen men from the battalion had already driven through the base's gates that afternoon, in their own cars, to answer Washington.

Joel lay on his camp bed in the dark with his boots on. He had sworn the oath at seventeen in a recruiting office in Dayton, after his mother signed the consent form at the kitchen table. *I will obey the orders of the President of the United States and the Governor of Ohio.* He said it over in his head until the words stopped meaning anything, and then started again. There was one President in it. Whoever wrote it had never pictured two.

He thought about the dozen. He could be one of them: drive up the road at five, show his card, walk through, and turn round. Then at six he would be standing behind the airmen when his own squad came up the road in their trucks, and he would be keeping his word to a man whose side meant to find his mother in a file and correct her. He couldn't get past the picture of Cody's face coming out of the truck and seeing him on the far side.

Then he thought about the captain's door, twenty yards away, and found he had already been thinking about it for an hour. He could knock, and in the morning he would sit in this hall under guard with his hands clean, and Cody and the rest would go up the road under Mercer, who said things like *let one of them try it* to prove he wasn't frightened. Joel had asked his squad after supper, one at a time, whether any of them would stay back. They were nineteen and twenty, and every one of them had voted for the count. Cody had looked at him as if he didn't follow.

"It's just standing, Sarge," he'd said. "Like the board."

That left the road, and the airmen. Joel didn't know any airmen. He supposed they had sworn something very like what he had.

At eleven he went out to the parking lot, where there was a signal, and rang his mother. She answered on the first ring.

He told her where he was going, though he wasn't supposed to.

She didn't say anything for a while. She had been raised among people who would not lift a hand against anybody, not even to save their own lives. She had left them, and she had signed his form anyway, because it was his life. She had not told him what to do since he was about fourteen, and he didn't think she would start now.

"Why are you going?" she said.

"Because if I'm there, they won't."

"Who won't?"

"Any of them. Mine." He looked across the lot at the dark drill hall. "I stood in front of them at the board, Carol. It held."

She was quiet again. He could hear her kitchen clock.

"Have you eaten anything?"

"Some."

"Eat something," said Carol. "Then go to sleep."

---

At twenty to seven on Tuesday morning, Joel stood ten feet from an airman of the United States Air Force, and both of them were trying to keep their feet warm.

Gate 12A was the main gate on the north side of the base, where on a weekday morning the cars came in one after another to work. A canopy roofed its lanes, lit white underneath, so that the airmen on the gate and the Guardsmen on the road stood in a box of light with the dark all round it. Behind the airmen, a slab of steel had tipped up out of the roadway when the trucks appeared. In front of them, the Guard had laid a row of concrete blocks across the lanes. Behind Joel the headlights ran back toward Dayton as far as he could see, every car with its engine running, full of people who were going to be late. Farther along the same road, out in the dark, was the cow pasture where the Wright brothers had learned to fly.

The captain had the Governor's order in a plastic sleeve. On the other side of the blocks, an Air Force colonel had Washington's.

The airman across from Joel was about twenty, one of the base's own police, who check your card at the gate every morning; he had been on this gate when the trucks came. He had the wrong gloves for it, thin ones, and every few minutes he took the right one off and put the bare hand in his armpit, and then remembered where he was and put the glove back on. His name was sewn over his pocket, but the Air Force sewed its names in a brown thread that disappeared into the camouflage, and under the canopy lights Joel couldn't read it.

The airman could read Joel's. The Army's thread was black.

Once, stamping his feet, he said to nobody in particular, "They told me Ohio'd be warmer."

"Who told you that?" said Joel.

"Everybody in Green Bay."

Then the colonel looked round, and the airman faced front again, and neither of them said anything more.

The colonel and the captain talked across the blocks with their breath going up between them, and each went away to make phone calls and came back. Mercer stood at the captain's shoulder with his phone in his hand. The sky over the hangars went from black to grey.

At about twenty past seven the airman turned to look at his colonel, the light fell across his chest, and Joel could make out the brown thread. KASPRZAK. He tried to say it to himself and couldn't.

Then a rifle fired.

Under the canopy the sound came off the roof and the concrete and the gatehouse windows all at once, and it came from everywhere. Joel thought it was behind him. Then he thought it was to his left. The airman's head turned. His rifle came up. Afterward Joel could never say whether it was coming up at him or only coming up, because anybody's hands go to what they're holding when a gun goes off near them.

Joel fired twice.

Then everybody was firing. It went on for nine seconds.

When it stopped, the first thing Joel heard was a car alarm, out on the road. The second was the captain, shouting cease fire, though it had ceased. Joel was still aiming over the blocks at nothing. The airman lay on his back on the far side, with his rifle beside him and one glove off.

Joel started over the blocks. Mercer took him by the back of his vest and pulled him down, hard, and held him there, saying his name over and over, as if Joel were the one who was hurt. On the other side another airman was kneeling, working at the boy's chest with both hands. Joel watched the hands until somebody stepped between.

Seven people died at Gate 12A, Guardsmen and airmen. The Guard pulled back down the road before eight. The base stayed exactly where it was.

---

The video came out on Wednesday morning.

A woman in the stopped line had her phone in a holder on the dashboard, and when the first shot came she reached for it. So it started a second late. It showed the white box of light under the canopy, a little crooked, and men moving. Then, for most of its nine seconds, it showed one young man side-on behind a rifle, firing, close enough that his name could be read off his chest. Then the phone dropped into the footwell, and the woman said *Jesus, oh Jesus*, and it ended.

By Wednesday night forty million people had watched it, and it would have been hard to find two who had watched the same thing.

On Washington's channels it was a militiaman firing on airmen at their own gate, and it was called BUTCHER. On Philadelphia's it was a soldier returning fire on men who had opened up on him first, and it was called HERO. Within an hour somebody had paused it on its clearest frame and drawn a red circle round the name on the young man's chest. FLORY, in black thread. Nobody drew a circle round the airman's. In no frame of it could the brown be read.

That morning a woman from the Governor's office came to see Joel in an empty classroom off the drill hall. She was about his age, in a good wool coat, and she was kind to him, and she had a camera operator waiting in the hall.

"Just a minute of you," she said. "Ohio needs to hear it from you. All you'd have to say is that you returned fire."

"I don't know who fired first, ma'am."

"Sergeant." She leaned toward him. "We're the lawful side. The count's the count. We didn't start this."

"I don't know who fired first."

"Then you returned fire."

"I fired," said Joel.

She wrote something down, and thanked him for his service. The camera operator went home.

That evening the company watched the Governor on a laptop on a folding table. In the first spring of the virus, when Joel was sixteen, she had been on Dolores's television every afternoon with the day's dead, and his mother had believed her like scripture. Now she stood in front of two flags and read that Ohio's soldiers had been fired upon, and had returned fire. She hadn't slept; it was in her face. She believed what she was reading. Joel could see that she did.

Later the news put up the names of the dead with their pictures. The airman from Green Bay was Eli Kasprzak, and he was twenty. His mother sat at her kitchen table in Wisconsin with a school photograph of him in a hockey jersey, holding the frame in both hands, and kept her voice steady to the end.

"He hated the cold," she said. "Can you believe that? A boy from Green Bay."

She said it *Kasper-zack*. Joel had been saying it wrong in his head for two days. He said it right, once, under his breath, where nobody heard.

Cody Ruhl sat on the next camp bed watching the nine seconds. He had fired too, at the gate, and didn't know at what. The phone finished the video and started it over, and he watched it again. After the fourth time Joel reached across, took the phone out of his hand, and put it face down on the blanket, and kept his own hand on it.

"Eat something," he said.

"I'm not hungry, Sarge."

"Didn't ask if you were hungry."

Cody ate half a packet of crackers, because Joel sat there until he did. Then he lay down with his boots on. Joel stayed on the next bed with his hand on Cody's phone, while his own buzzed in his pocket every few seconds, all night, with strangers.

---

On Thursday Carol's pictures went up beside his.

Since the county let her go, Carol had gone into the Sanctuary District every Thursday with Dolores, through the visitors' window, and sat at a table in the day room with anybody who needed a form filled in, which was nearly everybody. That Thursday she was doing a housing appeal for a woman who couldn't hold a pen any more, and her phone lit up on the table with a message from a number she didn't know. It was her old name, and nothing else.

She turned the phone over. She went on with the form, and when she looked down she had written the woman's date of birth in the box for her income, which was a mistake she had not made in twenty years. She crossed it out, neatly, and initialled the crossing, and found her hand wouldn't stop.

By the afternoon there were three pictures of her going round beside the one of him. The first was her county ID, taken on a bad day. The second was from a county newsletter of the year Joel was seven, which nobody had ever taken down: a young clerk with short hair, in a tie, with the old name under it. The third was a page of the *Dayton Daily News* from 2013. When Carol changed her name, Ohio law had made her print both names in the paper side by side, a month before the judge would let her keep the new one, in case anybody objected. Nobody had. The paper had kept the page.

Washington's channels put the old name under the nine seconds. Philadelphia's put her ID picture beside his, with a flag in pale blue and pink, and called her the hero's mother, and a woman from Columbus wrote to ask whether she would speak on the statehouse steps. Carol wrote back, *No, thank you,* because she had been brought up to answer letters.

On Friday morning somebody had sprayed the old name across her garage door in red, in letters as tall as she was. On the front step, somebody else had left a bunch of supermarket flowers in their cellophane, with a card that said THANK YOU FOR YOUR SON. It was impossible to tell which had come first.

She put the flowers in water, because they were flowers, and the card in her coat pocket. That night a pickup sat at the end of her drive with its engine running for an hour, and nobody got out, and then it drove away.

---

Joel got six hours on Saturday. Carol had bought paint, a grey as near the door's old colour as the hardware store had, and two rollers and a brush for the edges. There was frost on the drive, and the can said not to use it in a frost. They used it anyway.

He took the brush and went round each red letter first, carefully, inside the edges, before he picked up a roller. He had been nine when her two names went into the newspaper. Before that the old one had been the name on the mail, and he had called her by another word, and then for a while by no word at all, until they settled on Carol. In the cold the paint went on thick, and the red showed through the first coat as pink.

"They put up the one from the newsletter," he said.

"They did."

"That's the tie I gave you."

Carol had known he would see it. She hadn't known he would remember. It was green, with yellow ducks on it, and he had chosen it himself at seven, for Father's Day, from a rack at the mall.

"You had very strong views about ducks," she said. "I liked that tie."

"They put it up to make fun of you."

"I know why they put it up. I still liked the tie."

Across the street, Dale Hutto came out in his slippers with a coffee. Dale had been an engineer on the base for thirty years, and since the autumn he'd had a sign in his yard that said STAY, in big letters, and nothing else. The children on the street had been telling it "Good boy" on their way to school ever since. He called across that he was sorry about her door, and that he wouldn't have that on his street. Then he looked at Joel's back a while, and went in.

"He was in the line on Tuesday," said Carol. "Two cars back from the gate. He was late for work."

Joel rolled the same square three times.

"The woman from the Governor wanted me to say they fired first."

"Did you?"

"I don't know who fired first. I said I fired."

Carol didn't tell him it wasn't his fault, or that he'd had no choice, or that anybody would have done the same. His captain had told him those things all week, and so had strangers on his phone who called him a hero.

"Yes," she said. "You did."

He put the roller down in the tray.

"He said something to me," he said. "Before. When it was still just standing. That they'd told him Ohio would be warmer."

"What was his name?"

"Eli Kasprzak."

"Spell it."

He spelled it. She took the card out of her pocket, the one that said THANK YOU FOR YOUR SON, and wrote the name on the back in the small, neat hand of her county forms, and asked him how old. He told her, and she wrote that down too.

They did the top half of the door together, Joel reaching the part she couldn't. The pink went under the second coat.

"Go to Joy's," said Joel.

She kept painting.

"Dale says there was a truck at the end of the drive last night," he said. "I can't be here, Carol. I'll be lying in that drill hall thinking about this door instead of about the boys next to me." He wiped his hands on his trousers. "Go to Toronto."

Carol finished her corner. Then she put the brush across the top of the can.

She had a life in this town. She had the tenor line in Miss Bea Yancey's choir on Wednesdays, at the Methodist church on Salem Avenue, and the tax office down the street from it in the season, and Thursdays in the day room, where she was the only person who asked anybody what they liked to be called. She had Dolores, who had not turned her away at thirty-five and had been her mother since, and who would not leave Ruth Dorsey's arm to anybody for any war. And she had him.

But she had been raised by people who would not lift a hand against anybody, and she knew what her son would do if that truck came back while he was standing in this drive. She had watched him do it on every phone in America.

"If I stay," she said, "you'll come. And the next time it won't be paint, and you'll be here with your rifle."

He didn't argue, because it was true.

She went into the house and came back with a green ledger, the kind with a cloth spine that the county used to buy by the box, and gave it to him.

"I can't go in on Thursdays," he said.

"Then keep it till somebody can. Keep it dry."

He opened it. She had ruled every page herself, in columns. In the first went the name the county had for each resident, and the letter it had sorted them under. In the second went what they liked to be called. In the third, who they had. In the fourth, what they needed. There were nearly two years of Thursdays in it. Halfway down a page he found *Dorsey, Ruth (D)*, and beside it *Mrs. Dorsey*, and *Daughter, Anna, in Finland*, and *Lessons, Tues & Thurs. Her arm is Dolores's.*

Carol took the card out of her pocket and put it in the back of the ledger, where she kept the things that had no column.

---

She left on Sunday, before it was light.

Dolores came at five, in her coat over her nightgown, and stood in the cold drive while Carol put one bag in the car, the size of bag a visitor would carry. Dolores had not asked her to stay. She had not asked her to go, either. She had only come.

"Tell Joy I've got arms to do," she said.

Carol held on to her until the cold came through both their coats. Then she got in and drove north up the interstate, three hours and more, to the bridge at Detroit.

On the far side of the river, the Canadian officer in the booth took her passport and looked at it, and at her.

"Purpose of your visit?"

"Family," said Carol.

"How long will you be in Canada?"

All her life, Carol Flory had answered every box about herself with the truth, and it had cost her a good deal. She looked past the officer at the road going on into Canada, grey and wet, and the lights of Windsor.

"Two weeks," she said.

The officer handed it back and waved her through.
