# Chapter 6: The Long Way Round

On the last Friday of May 2028, Declan Toner's da had been ready to vote since six in the morning, when the gate opened.

When Declan let himself in at half past eight, his father was standing at the front window in his good coat, buttoned over his cardigan, with his cap already on and his bus pass in his hand.

"You're late," said Brendan Toner.

"It's half eight, Da. They're open till ten tonight."

"They were open at seven."

He was eighty. He had driven the city's buses all his working life, and he had never once been late for anything a timetable could tell him about.

The street Declan grew up on was short: two rows of red-brick houses facing each other, and at the bottom of it, where the next street should have been, a wall. It was concrete as high as the upstairs windows, with steel mesh on top of that. Beyond it the Protestant streets began, and the wall ran on both ways past the end of the street, further than you could see from the step. Two streets along there was a gate in it, for cars and people. The police opened it at six every morning and closed it at half past ten every night, from an office somewhere else, by remote control.

Across the street, Mickey Quinn was leaning on a car with a poster in its back window, a green one, with YES on it in letters a foot high.

"You'll want a lift, Brendan."

"I've a son," said Brendan.

"I hope your son's voting the right way."

"My son votes how he likes. Which is more than you ever let anybody do."

Mickey laughed. He had spent eleven years in prison for the IRA, and come out, and gone into politics, and he and Brendan had been having the same argument on this street since they were young men. Neither of them had won it yet.

Declan's daughter, Niamh, was in the back of the car with her passport on her knee. She was eighteen since March, and it was her first vote. Anybody born here could have a British passport or an Irish one, or both, and Niamh had both, and she had brought the Irish one. Declan had decided on the drive over not to ask whether that meant anything.

His phone buzzed while his da was getting into the front seat, which took a while. It was Ben, in Helsinki, whom he still saw on calls most days of the week.

*Good luck today? Is that what you say?*

*No idea,* Declan wrote back. *Ask me tomorrow.*

---

The polling station was the primary school at the top of the road, where Declan had learned to read. It was May, the month of First Communions, and the corridor was hung with the children's paintings of themselves in white dresses and little suits, every one of them smiling, with the priest drawn much smaller than the children.

The booths were plywood boxes along the wall of the assembly hall, each with a shelf, and on each shelf, tied to a string so that nobody could walk off with it, a pencil. The man ahead of them in the queue had brought his own pen, clipped into his top pocket, because he had read somewhere that pencil could be rubbed out afterwards, and the woman at the desk told him that was perfectly all right. All over the island that day, in schools and church halls on both sides of the border, the question was being answered one cross at a time, with pencils on strings.

Brendan held out his bus pass for proof of who he was. The woman at the desk ran her finger down a long printed list, found him, and drew a line through his name with a ruler.

"Thank you, Mr. Toner."

"The last time they asked this question," Brendan told Niamh, loud enough for the queue, "I stayed at home. We all did."

"Why?"

"Because we'd have lost, and they'd have counted us losing. So we let them have it on their own. Ninety-nine out of a hundred, they got." He put his bus pass away. "Then in '98 I voted for the Agreement. That was a Friday too. It's the Agreement says they've to ask us again, whenever it looks like we'd win."

He went into a booth. Niamh went into the one beside it, and Declan into the end one.

The paper was plain, with one question on it. *Should Northern Ireland cease to be part of the United Kingdom and become part of a united Ireland?* There was a box for yes and a box for no.

Declan stood with the pencil and its string.

If this went yes, nobody would ever be able to say which had done it, the thirty years of bombs or the paper in his hand, and he would have to live in whichever answer people chose.

He put his cross where his family had always wanted it, and found that his hand wasn't steady, and folded the paper so nobody would see the cross had a wobble in it.

Outside, his da was waiting on the steps with his cap in his hand.

"Well?" he said to Niamh.

"It's a secret ballot, Granda."

Brendan looked at her for a moment and then put his cap on.

"Good girl," he said.

---

The count began at nine on Saturday morning, and Declan's da had the television on for it at eight.

In the North all the boxes were counted together, at long tables in one great hall down by the docks, and in the South in counting centres all over the country. It went slowly, and there was nothing to see but paper. But at every counting table people from both campaigns stood behind the barrier, watching each paper come out of its box and keeping their own tallies on clipboards, and long before anybody official said a word, the tallies talked. By eleven, the television said, the tallies in the South were running yes. The tallies in the North were running close, and nobody would say more than that.

Niamh's mother was on nights at the hospital all that week, so Niamh had come with Declan, and was lying on her granda's sofa with her phone.

At two o'clock, as he did every Saturday, Norman Kerr came through the gate.

Norman had driven the city's buses out of the same depot as Brendan, for as long as Brendan had. Their wives had died within a year of each other, and since then Norman had come over on Saturdays for the racing and his tea, and on Wednesdays Brendan went over to him for the football. In all those years they had never once agreed about anything that mattered. Norman had voted no on Friday in a church hall on his own side of the wall, and had told everybody so, because he had never seen the point of a secret.

He hung his coat on the end of the stairs and looked at the television.

"Is it over?"

"The South's going yes," said Brendan.

"The South was always going yes. It's not the South I'm asking about."

He sat down in the chair that had been his for ten years of Saturdays and watched the paper being counted. After a while, without taking his eyes off it, he said, "If it goes, you know what bought it."

"Paper," said Brendan.

"Bombs." Norman said it to the screen. "Thirty years of them, till the English would sooner be shot of the lot of us than pay for another one. Your crowd'll stand up tonight and say it was the paper, and it was the bombs, and every one of you knows it."

"The bombs bought three and a half thousand funerals," said Brendan. "That's all they ever bought."

"Tell that to Mickey Quinn."

"I've been telling Mickey Quinn since 1972."

Declan, by the window, kept out of it. He had said Norman's thing himself once, to Ben, at the wall of a Sanctuary District in San Francisco, and he didn't want to say it in this room.

---

In the middle of the afternoon, Niamh held her phone out to her grandfather.

"Granda. Is this you?"

It was a minute of old black-and-white film. A street of terraced houses, a crowd of young men in coats and ties, and a line of police with long sticks. The police waded into the crowd, and the sticks went up and came down. A man in a good suit, with blood running down his face, was being held up by two others.

Derry, the caption said. The fifth of October, 1968.

Brendan put on his glasses and watched it twice.

He had been twenty that autumn. He had gone up from Belfast on a bus with some lads from work, to march for one man, one vote, in a city where most people were Catholic and the council never was. The government had banned the march, and they had marched anyway.

"No," he said. "That's not me. I was further back." He handed the phone back. "Everybody from Belfast says they're in that film."

"But you were there."

"I was there. A man from the Dublin television got about a minute of it, and the whole world saw that minute. London, America. For about a year after that, the whole world was on our side."

"And then?" said Norman.

Brendan took his glasses off.

"And then the bombs," he said.

"Aye," said Norman.

It was the only thing they agreed on all afternoon, and neither of them noticed.

---

At ten past six, in the yard of Dublin Castle, a woman in a dark suit stood at a microphone and read out the South's result. The crowd in the yard had been waiting all afternoon. It was yes. It was closer than anyone in Dublin had expected, and when she got to the end of the figures the yard went up, flags and all, and the camera shook.

Brendan watched it with his hands on his knees.

"So now it's us," he said.

The North was closer than close. In two of the areas the counting stopped, and the papers went back into their bundles to be counted again, because the no campaign had asked, and they were entitled to, and nobody argued. The presenters filled the time. Brendan made tea, and then more tea, and then sandwiches nobody ate. Niamh fell asleep on the sofa and woke up and asked whether it was over, and went back to sleep.

At ten o'clock Norman looked at the clock on the mantelpiece. Then he looked at the television, where nothing had changed, and stayed where he was.

At twenty past, Brendan got up and put the kettle on again.

At half past ten, two streets away, the gate closed.

---

The result came at twenty to midnight.

The returning officer for Northern Ireland was a tired man in glasses, standing in the great hall with the campaigns on either side of him, and he read the figures in the flat voice of someone who knew that whichever way he read them, half the people listening would never forgive him. He read the number for no first. Then he read the number for yes, and it was the larger.

The difference was a little over eighteen thousand, out of more than a million.

For one second Declan was simply glad, all the way through, with nothing in it but his da's whole life coming right. Then he looked at Norman before he could stop himself.

"You'd not fill the football ground with that," said Norman.

On the screen, the hall had gone up at one end and gone still at the other. Then the picture cut to a newsroom in London, where a presenter, reading it off a sheet in her hand, said that Northern Ireland had voted to leave the United Kingdom, and that the South had voted to have it, and that nothing would change for some years yet, while the two governments worked out how. In the corner of the screen, under a map of the island all in one colour, it said, in capitals, A UNITED IRELAND.

Brendan Toner had voted for this, one way and another, every election of his life. He reached for the remote and turned the sound down, which was the kindest thing he could think of to do.

On the silent screen, the leader of the biggest unionist party walked out to a bank of microphones. The words came up at the bottom. *The people have decided, and we will abide by it.*

Norman watched her to the end.

Outside, a car went past with its horn going, and then another, and then the street was full of them. Doors opened. There were flags, and somebody had a speaker in a car boot. Niamh was at the window.

From the street, a crowd of young men took up a chant. Declan knew it before the words came. Everybody did. *Ooh, ah, up the Ra.* He had heard it at weddings, and at Niamh's school dance, sung by children, and never once by anybody old enough to remember what it was about.

"It's only a song, Granda," said Niamh. She said it to the window, not to him.

"It was never a song," said Brendan.

Then Mickey Quinn's voice came over all of them, sharp enough to carry down the whole street. "Lads. Lads. Not tonight. There's cameras."

The chant stopped. It had been sung, though, and a room's width away from Norman Kerr.

Norman stood up and went to the stairs for his coat.

"I'll run you round," said Declan.

---

With the gate closed, the way to Norman's house was the long way round: down the hill to the four-lane road that runs past the city centre, along it for a mile, off again, and up the other side. It took ten minutes to get to a house Declan could have seen from his old bedroom window, if the wall hadn't been in the way.

The streets on the other side were quiet. On a corner a chip shop was still open, its window steamed up, and inside, above the counter, a television was showing the hall at the docks with the sound off, and nobody in the queue was looking at it.

Norman sat with his coat on his knee.

"Our own prime minister," he said. "Off the table, he said. Two years ago, in this city. I watched him say it."

"I know."

"London couldn't wait. You'll not have noticed that. Your crowd were too busy." A mural went by on the end wall of a house: a man in a balaclava with a rifle, painted by Norman's own side, in honour of its own gunmen. Norman didn't look at it. He never did. "Your da'll tell you it was the paper. He tells me every Saturday."

"I know he does."

"My brother Sammy drove a milk lorry," said Norman. "Weekends he was a soldier, the part-time kind. Two of them came out of an alley on his round one morning in 1976, and shot him in his cab. He was twenty-five."

Declan knew. Everybody on his da's street knew, and nobody had ever once said it in front of Norman.

"So you tell me," said Norman. "If it was the paper, Declan, what was Sammy for?"

The lights of the big road went over them one at a time.

Declan thought about the wall in San Francisco, and the candles, and how easy it had been to say it there, about people he had never met.

"I don't know what bought it," he said. "Nobody does. People will be fighting about it when the both of us are dead. But I'm not going to tell you Sammy paid for it. He didn't pay for anything. Somebody shot him on his milk round."

They came up off the big road and onto Norman's road. A boy on a bike went past with a Union Jack round his shoulders and his face set hard, going nowhere in particular, fast.

"Your da has a Mass said for him," said Norman. "Every November. Did you know that?"

Declan hadn't known.

"We don't pray for the dead. The dead are where God has them, and there's nothing you can say to Him that'll shift them. I told your da that, the first year. He said that was grand, he'd have it said anyway." Norman turned his cap over in his hands. "Every November since. I've never once asked him to stop."

Declan pulled up outside the house. It was the same as Brendan's: red brick, two windows up and one down and a front door, built by the same people in the same year for the same wages, on the other side of a wall.

Norman didn't get out at once.

"For fifty years," he said, "I've told every one of yous that would listen: you want out, get a majority. A majority's a majority. I said it in the depot. I said it to your da across his own table. I'll not stop saying it the night it goes against me." He opened the door. "The count's the count."

He got out, and then he bent back down to the window.

"Tell your da thanks for the tea."

Declan watched him up the path. Norman had his key ready before he got to the door, and the hall light came on, and the door shut.

---

Declan drove back.

On his da's street, the cars had gone home and the flags had gone in, and somebody had left a speaker sitting on a wall. Mickey Quinn was out on his step alone, with a cigarette, looking down toward the bottom of the street. Declan nodded to him, and Mickey nodded back. Neither of them said anything about the song.

His da was still up, in his chair, with the television on and the sound still off. Niamh was asleep on the sofa under his coat.

"Did he get home?"

"He did."

"Did he say anything?"

"He said thanks for the tea."

Brendan nodded. It seemed to be what he had been waiting up for. Declan didn't ask him about the Masses. They were his da's to tell, and he had kept them to himself this long.

He went out to the front step. It was a soft night, and nearly one in the morning, and nearly three in Helsinki, and he rang Ben anyway, because Ben had sent three messages since the result, the last one only a question mark.

Ben answered on the first ring. He had been up for it.

"So," Ben said. "Is it congratulations?"

"Ask me in thirty years."

"I watched Dublin. The castle. It looked like congratulations."

"That's Dublin," said Declan. "I've just driven a man home the long way round because his country ended at twenty to twelve and the gate shut at half ten."

"Is he okay?" said Ben.

"No. He'll be over on Saturday for the racing, but no."

There was a child's voice somewhere behind Ben, and Ben's voice going away from the phone and coming back. Alma was up, he said. She didn't know what time it was in any country.

"We had news too," Ben said. "Anna's a citizen. Last month. She's a Finn. She sent the form for her mom the same week."

"How is her mother?"

"Still inside. Dolores goes in every month with the shot, and it's working. Anna says she's teaching some of the kids in there piano, on a keyboard she drew on the backs of cereal boxes. No sound. She corrects them anyway."

"Tell Anna congratulations," said Declan. "That one I'm sure of."

"I'll tell her." Ben paused. "We've got ours in November. The election."

"How's it looking?"

"Like nobody's going to believe it, whoever wins."

Declan looked down the street to the bottom of it, where the wall was, and past it to the dark roofs on the other side.

"There's a man over there lost tonight by eighteen thousand votes," he said. "His brother was shot by the side that won. He sat in my car and told me the count's the count."

"Can we borrow him?" said Ben.

"You cannot," said Declan. "We need him."

At six the next morning the gate opened, the same as every morning. Nobody was waiting at it. It was Sunday, and it wasn't anybody's day to go over.
