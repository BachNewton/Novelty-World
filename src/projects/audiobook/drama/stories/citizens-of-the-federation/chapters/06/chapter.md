# Chapter 6: Labor Day

Ben Novak's body was still in Helsinki. On the last Saturday of August it woke him at four in the morning in a hotel in San Francisco, sure that it was two in the afternoon and that he had slept through lunch.

Brynner had called its engineers in from every office it had, for a week of meetings, the first since the pandemic. The meetings began on Tuesday, after the long weekend. Ben had come early to get used to the clock, and so had Declan Toner, whose idea it had been. "A weekend first," Declan had said on the screen one morning, "to be eejits in."

Declan came through the hotel's revolving door at eight that evening with his bag over his shoulder and a jacket far too warm for California. He looked at his watch, and then at Ben.

"Eight o'clock," he said.

It was. For four years, at eight o'clock San Francisco time, the two of them had appeared to each other in little squares on a screen and started the day. They had been in one room only once since, for a weekend in Ohio in April, to watch the moon cover the sun. This was the city they had lived in side by side, the year Declan was sent over from Belfast, and they had never been back in it together. Ben found he had nothing clever to say.

"You look terrible," said Declan, pleased. "Come on. You're buying."

---

On Sunday they had lunch at the Vietnamese place round the corner from the office, where they had eaten twice a week the year Declan lived here. Declan ordered for both of them without asking, and got it right.

Afterward they walked back the old way without deciding to, round the corner and under the freeway.

The tents were gone. The sidewalk had been washed paler than the street, and a row of planters stood along it, close together, with nothing much growing in them. On a pillar of the freeway the city had screwed up a notice: no camping, and a phone number for anyone who wanted a free bus ticket home. The corner lot where Declan had once put his finger through a fence had a building on it now, half finished, wrapped in plastic.

Declan stopped where the woman with the broom had kept her tent. There was nothing to show it had ever been there.

"Forty mornings, maybe," he said. "I said good morning to her forty mornings, and I never asked her name."

Ben hadn't said good morning to her once.

Declan read the notice on the pillar twice. "Your Supreme Court said they could, in June," he said. "I read it on the plane. Fine you or jail you for sleeping outside, whether there's a bed for you anywhere or not." He looked at the planters. "A ticket home, if you've a home. And if you've not?"

Ben didn't know. They went up to the office, because Declan wanted to see where he'd be sitting. It was on the twenty-eighth floor, all glass, and from the window you could see across the city to the hills. Declan stood at it a while. Then he pointed.

"Is that it?"

A few miles off, between the roofs, a long grey line ran straight for a block, turned a corner, and ran straight again.

"That's it," said Ben. "District A."

The city had built it the year after the Novaks and the Toners left, on the far side of town, where neither of them had ever had a reason to go. Ben knew it the way he knew the sewage works was out there somewhere. The guards had words for the people inside: *gimmes*, for the ones who were only poor, and *dims*, for the ones sick in their heads.

Four years ago, at the fenced lot, the woman with the broom had told them where its people had gone: *wherever they could carry it to*. Ben had taken it to mean they had scattered. From the twenty-eighth floor it looked as if someone had gathered them up.

Declan didn't say anything. He put his hand flat on the glass, and then took it away.

---

That evening, in a restaurant near the hotel, every phone in the room lit up at once.

The pictures were from a helicopter: smoke over the District, and fire outside the long low building by its gate where people were signed in, the Processing Centre. A fight over a ration card had turned into a riot. The District's guards had run, and some hadn't got out in time. By ten o'clock a crowd of residents held the Processing Centre, and the guards' shotguns, and six of the people who worked there.

Ben lay on his hotel bed and watched until the phone was hot in his hand. A few minutes before midnight a new video started, live, from inside.

A man in a clean shirt sat in front of a phone that somebody was holding up for him. He had a tired, ordinary face, the face of a man who would coach a children's baseball team. He said his name was Michael Webb, that he used to be a plant manager at a chemical company, and that he was speaking for the residents of Sanctuary District A.

"We're holding six people," he said. "We don't want to hurt anybody. All we want is—"

The picture stopped. When Ben tapped it, the video was gone. He looked for a copy everywhere he could think of, and every copy was gone too.

At two in the morning a message came from Declan, two floors down. *You see that?*

*Yes.*

*What was the end of the sentence, do you think.*

Ben lay in the dark with the sirens going and didn't know.

---

Monday was Labor Day, the holiday America gives itself at the end of summer in honour of working people, and the office was nearly empty. Ben and Declan went in anyway, because neither of them could sleep, and watching it alone was worse.

Dana Reyes was there. She ran the small team that decided what could be shown on Brynner's video service, and she had been up all night. She sat on the edge of a desk and told them.

Just before midnight the police had asked for the District to be cut off, so that the people inside could talk to their negotiator and nobody else. Every company that carried anything into the District had done as it was asked, and one of them was Brynner, which ran the District's network for the city. Meanwhile every big video site had taken down every clip from inside, given each one a kind of fingerprint, and shared the fingerprints, so that a copy put up anywhere vanished in seconds.

"We all learned to do that after a man in New Zealand put a massacre on the internet live," said Dana. "It's a good system. I helped build ours. It was built for people who film themselves killing people." She rubbed her eyes. "As far as anybody knows, nobody in there has killed anyone."

So that was where the end of the sentence had gone. Ben's own company had cut it off.

All morning there were only the helicopter pictures: a police negotiator at the gate, on a phone, and the National Guard on its way. In Helsinki, where it was evening, Anna had seen them over the children's supper and gone straight to a map. *Is that near you?*

*A few miles. I'm fine. We're watching it on TV like everybody else.*

That was true until three o'clock.

---

At three Chris Brynner came in.

He had founded the company and still owned most of it. He went into the glass room at the end of the floor with Dana and a lawyer on the speakerphone, and they were in there for forty minutes. Whatever changed his mind, he kept to himself, then and afterward.

At five o'clock, said the message that went to everyone in the company, Brynner would switch the District's network back on, whatever the police wanted. Anyone inside could tell their story on the front page of Brynner's video service, to anyone in the world who cared to listen. Everything would go out four minutes late, and somebody in this building would watch every story before it went. The rules were short. No guns on camera. No threats. No hostages.

A sentence went round the floor within the hour, from people who had been near the glass room: *We'll lose the city contract, and we'll have the biggest night we've ever had.* People argued about whether he'd been joking.

Dana's team was four people, and two of them were asleep. At half past four she asked anyone in the building who understood English to put on a pair of headphones, and then she told them what she had told Brynner.

"The police have said it already," she said. "The day a hostage turns up on our front page, we're carrying the hostage-takers' message for them. Their lawyers will be in front of a judge within the hour, and they'll win, and we go dark. For everybody in that line, not just the one." She looked along the row of chairs. "And a hostage says what the people holding her want her to say. You can never know which words are hers. So, no hostages. Not one."

Ben's team built the part of the service that played the video. Every story that went out tonight would play through code he had written. He sat down. Declan sat down beside him, the way he had at the next desk the year he lived here, and pulled his headphones on.

---

This is how the world saw Sanctuary District A that night.

In the Processing Centre a person sat down on a bench in front of a phone and talked. What they said waited four minutes on Brynner's machines, and in those four minutes it came up on a screen in front of somebody in a glass tower who had put on headphones an hour before and had never decided anything like this in their life. Under the screen were two buttons. Green, and within a minute people in Lagos and Manila and Ohio were watching a stranger say who they were. Red, and it went into a folder that nobody outside the building would ever see. If the four minutes ran out with nothing pressed, it went into the folder too.

By nine o'clock more people were watching Brynner than had ever watched it for anything. Hardly any of them knew that every story they saw had passed under somebody's finger first.

---

Michael Webb went first, in the same clean shirt, and he finished his sentence. All they wanted, he said, was what they had been promised when they came in: work, and the door.

Then the line began.

Ben never knew how long it was. It went out of the room and down the steps into the street. A boy of about fourteen held the phone up for each speaker; Webb called him Danny. Somewhere in the second hour the picture began to shake, and then steadied again when the boy changed arms.

A man called Henry Garcia said he had been living in the District for two years. He had never been in trouble with the law, he said, not once. He didn't want to hurt anybody. He wanted to work. Ben pressed green, and all over the world people watched Henry Garcia say it.

An old man said the police had woken him in a bank doorway in July and offered him a bus ticket home. "I said, home to who?" he said. "So they brought me here." Green.

A woman with a split lip said that in her first week inside she had taken a ration card off a man in the food line, because she was hungry and he was slow, and that she'd do it again tomorrow. "Don't feel sorry for me," she said. "Feel sorry for him. He's still in here." She held no gun and threatened nobody, so the rules let her through. Ben pressed green, and found he believed her more than anyone yet.

A big man with a careful voice put his hands flat on his knees before he began.

"They call me a dim in here," he said. He held the word out a moment, the way you'd hold up something somebody had thrown at you, and then put it down. "I hear things. I know I do." He looked down at his hands, and then made himself look up again. "I've been on a list for a doctor two years. If my sister in Sacramento's watching, hi, Lainie. I'm okay."

Then Declan sat up so suddenly that his chair rolled.

On his screen a woman was settling herself on the bench, small and upright, with her grey hair pinned back, and she had a milk carton in her lap with a plant growing in it: a geranium, with one red flower.

"That's her," said Declan. "Ben. That's her."

Ben looked. He had walked past her for months, and he would not have known her face.

She said her name was Rosa Delgado, and that she had kept a tent off Market Street for three years, under the freeway, and kept her bit of sidewalk swept. In July the city came with a truck. They wouldn't let her bring a plant through the gate, she said, so she had broken off a piece of it and carried it in her pocket. She held up the milk carton to show the phone. It had taken.

Declan pressed green so hard the desk shook. Then he waved at the screen, at a woman four minutes away who couldn't see him, and went red to the ears.

"Shut up," he said, though Ben hadn't said anything.

A man sat down with a shotgun across his knees and said that the people out there should be frightened, because the people in here had been frightened for years, and if the soldiers came in they'd see. Declan pressed red before he had finished. "Threat," he said, and the next one sat down. Ben watched him do it, and saw how easy the rule was when you didn't like the man.

Then a woman said that the men who had taken the building would have hurt the six by now, if a resident called Bell hadn't stopped them. Bell had found people he trusted and put them on the door of the room where the six were kept. "Bell says nobody touches them," she said. "So nobody does."

At half past nine Dana went into the glass room with the city's lawyers and the police on the speakerphone. At the door she turned to the row of reviewers. "Nobody comes in here. You've got the rules. The rules are the answer."

---

At ten past ten Michael Webb sat down on the bench again, and this time he wasn't alone. Beside him was a woman with a District lanyard round her neck and her hair coming loose.

"They're saying out there that we've hurt them," said Webb. "We haven't. This is Lee."

"I work at the clinic here," said the woman. "Public health." She looked straight into the phone. "We're all right. All six of us. They've fed us. Nobody's hurt anybody. Bell won't let them."

Ben's hand stopped over the buttons.

No hostages. It was the shortest rule on the list. He could see the reason for it: a woman in a room with men holding shotguns would say she was all right whether she was or not. He could see the other reason, too. If this went out, the city's lawyers would go to a judge, and the line he had been watching for five hours, the old man and the woman with the split lip and whoever was still waiting on the steps, would go dark.

And he could see her face. Out there, people were saying the six were dead. The negotiator had heard Webb say they weren't, on the phone, every hour since midnight, and everybody knew what a man holding hostages says on the phone. Nobody had heard it from her.

He had three minutes and forty seconds.

"Declan."

Declan pulled one side of his headphones off and leaned over. He watched the clip through, and was quiet for a moment.

"There's a fella with a shotgun at that door, Ben," he said. "You can't see him. But she knows he's there."

"She's telling people she's alive."

"Aye. And she'd say it if she wasn't fine at all." Declan said it gently, the way you'd tell somebody their flight was cancelled. "Rule's the rule."

Ben pushed his chair back and half stood, and looked across at the glass room. Dana was on her feet in there with her back to the floor and one hand pressed to her forehead, and a man's voice was coming out of the speakerphone, loud enough to hear through the glass. *The rules are the answer,* she had said, and shut the door. Whatever he did with this, he would have to do it himself. He sat down again. The clock on his screen said two minutes and fifty seconds.

He thought, and was ashamed of it at once, that his family lived in Finland on a permit that went with this job. He never knew afterward whether that thought had moved his hand, or had only been there while it moved.

He pressed red. The clip went into the folder, and the next person sat down on the bench.

They stayed until midnight, and walked back to the hotel through the empty streets. Halfway there Declan said, "You did right," and Ben said, "I know."

---

At four on Tuesday morning his body woke him, because in Helsinki it was two in the afternoon, and he lay in bed with the phone and watched the line.

Just before five an old woman was telling the phone about a hotel where she had cleaned rooms for twenty-two years. Somebody else was holding the phone now; the boy had gone. She was halfway through a story about a guest who had left a hundred-dollar bill on a pillow when she turned her head toward a noise. Whoever held the phone turned too. Then the picture stopped, and in its place came a plain card in Brynner's colours saying they would be back.

A reviewer on the floor had seen what came next, and decided the world wouldn't. And whatever Ben had just watched had happened four minutes ago. The soldiers had been inside for four minutes before he saw the old woman turn her head.

He messaged Anna, and she rang him at once. It was three in the afternoon there, and she was walking Theo to fetch Nora from daycare. She didn't say much. She stayed on the line while he watched the doors of the Processing Centre smoking on the news, and he could hear a tram going by, and Theo telling her something about a dog.

---

By nine they knew more. All six had come out alive. One of them, a guard, with a blanket round his shoulders, told the cameras that the soldiers had fired at everyone, and in the smoke a guard looked like anybody else. A resident called Gabriel Bell had stepped in front of him and taken the bullet.

By noon Bell's name was on the list of the dead. Michael Webb's had been on it since the morning. The rest of the list took days, and it went into the hundreds.

In the afternoon Lee spoke to a reporter from a hospital bed. She said what she had said on the bench. They had been fed. Nobody had hurt them. Bell wouldn't let them.

Ben watched it on his phone in a corridor outside a meeting room. He was so glad she was alive that he said "Oh" out loud, to nobody. Then he thought of the folder.

Declan had come out to find him.

"It wouldn't have changed anything," said Declan.

"You don't know that."

"No," said Declan. "I don't."

All that week Ben went looking for when the governor had decided to send the soldiers in. One paper said Monday afternoon, before the line had even begun. A man who had been in the room said it was after midnight. The governor's office said it would not discuss the timing of an operational decision. Nobody anywhere said what the governor had been watching on Monday night, or whether he had been watching anything at all.

---

The meetings went on, because nobody knew how to call them off. On Thursday the city cancelled its contract with Brynner. That evening Declan asked to see the wall up close before he flew home, and Ben took him in a car.

Soldiers stood on the gate. Along the sidewalk opposite, people had left candles in jars, flowers, and pieces of cardboard with names on them: BELL, and WEBB, and HENRY GARCIA, and names neither of them knew. A woman was relighting the candles that had gone out. Further along, a smaller crowd held signs that said THANK YOU NATIONAL GUARD, and the two crowds kept to their own ends of the block and didn't look at each other.

Declan walked the length of the cardboard, reading every name. Rosa Delgado's wasn't there.

"Doesn't mean anything," he said. "The list's not done."

Then he went across to the wall and walked along it with his hand held flat, an inch from the concrete. Ben knew what he was thinking of. Declan had told him on a corner four years ago: a wall at the bottom of his da's street, between the Catholic houses and the Protestant ones, that a general had promised would be very, very temporary.

"When I was a wean," said Declan, "the government took one party off the news. Sinn Féin, the party that spoke for the IRA, while the IRA was planting bombs. Not what they said. Just their voices." He took his hand away from the wall. "Their leader would come on, and his lips would move, and an actor would read out his words. Funniest thing on television. We used to do the voice."

A soldier on the gate looked at them, and looked away.

"My da voted for John Hume every election of his life," said Declan. "Hume wanted one Ireland as much as the IRA did, and he wouldn't have a gun near it. He talked. He got the Nobel Prize for it." He nodded at the candles. "And this year, for the first time ever, the woman running the government in Belfast is from the party that had the actor doing its voice."

"That's different," said Ben. "That was a vote."

"It was. And for thirty years before the vote there were bombs." Declan looked across at the cardboard. "A week ago, how many people in this whole country could have told you one name from behind that wall? They asked nicely for years. Then they took six people, and the whole world listened to them for a night."

"Most of them never hurt anybody."

"I know. I held back the one who wanted to." Declan was quiet for a long time. "I'd love it to be the talking that does it. I'd love it to be Hume. My da would love it to be Hume. But maybe it works. The other thing. Maybe it just works, and we'd all rather not know."

They stood on the sidewalk until the woman had lit every candle. Then they went back to the car.

On Friday Brynner's numbers came in, the biggest it had ever had. Declan flew home to Belfast, and Ben to Helsinki.

---

He was home on Saturday afternoon. Nora met him at the door with a drawing of the moon of Jupiter the astronauts were flying to, white and cracked all over like an egg, because she had heard there was ice. Anna held on to him in the hall for longer than she usually did, and asked nothing, and he was grateful.

At six on Sunday evening, eleven in the morning in Dayton, she propped the tablet against the fruit bowl, and Walt's face came up much too close to the camera.

"Well, there he is," said Walt. "We saw your old city on the news all week."

"I was there," said Ben.

"I know it. Hell of a thing. That fellow Bell." Walt shook his head. "Pastor prayed for the hostages this morning. And their families."

"Bell kept the hostages alive."

"That's what the one fellow said." Walt didn't argue. He didn't agree, either. "They took hostages, Ben."

Behind him, in the front room, Ruth was at the piano, going over something for next Sunday, stopping and going back.

Then Walt told them his news, in the voice he used for the weather. The county had voted on Thursday. Dayton was going to build a District of its own, down by the river where the old warehouses were. They'd been arguing about it since the spring, Walt said, and after California it had gone through easy.

"Who's going in it?" said Anna.

"Folks who can't manage, I guess." Walt didn't look round at the piano. He looked straight at the camera. "Nobody we know, Annie."

Anna said nothing at all.

She was on a bench by a hangar at the edge of a mown field, with a grey plane going up beyond the trees and her father not looking at her. *Don't you let them put your mother in one of those places.* He had asked it in April, when the county was still only arguing. She had given him her word. Now there would be one of those places twenty minutes from his house, and he was sitting in his kitchen telling her it was for nobody they knew, so that she wouldn't have to think about what she had promised. She could see him doing it. She thought he could probably see her seeing it.

Ben watched her face and thought he knew what was in it. He was thinking of Ruth too, and the injection that kept her well, and of the big man on the bench who had been on a list for a doctor for two years. He opened his mouth to tell Walt about him.

Under the table, Anna's hand found his and held on. He took it to mean *not now*, and let it go.

On the screen, Walt went on to the tomatoes. In the front room Ruth stopped what she was practising and began the slow hymn she played at the end of every call, though the call wasn't over. Anna listened to it all the way through.

---

That night Ben's body was still in San Francisco. At two in the morning he lay wide awake, because it was four in the afternoon there, an hour before the line began.

Somewhere on Brynner's machines, in a place nobody outside the building would ever look, a woman with her hair coming loose was still saying that all six of them were all right. Nobody would ever know if it would have mattered. He had been right, and Declan had said so, and he had said *I know*.

Beside him Anna was awake too. She wanted him to turn over and ask her what was wrong, in so many words, so that she could tell him about a bench by a hangar and what she had promised there, and hear him say that whatever it took, they would do it together. Ben thought he knew what was wrong, so he didn't ask. They lay side by side in the dark until morning, each carrying something the other didn't know was there.
