# Chapter 19: First Contact

*Your wrong about the feild,* the letter began. *Not wrong like stupid. Wrong like everybody.*

It reached Nora Novak at noon, at her desk at the university in Helsinki, as all his letters did, because he wrote them at three in the morning in Montana. For nearly six years a man there had been writing to her about the shape of empty space. He could not spell *field*, or *separate*, or, on one occasion, *Montana*. Under the spelling, every time, was mathematics that Nora had to get up and walk round the room to follow.

He had read a paper of hers, the summer her brother was deciding about the thing from Io, and written to her and a dozen other physicists in four countries. She was the only one who wrote back. Her colleagues, when she showed them, laughed at the spelling and stopped reading. Nora had kept reading. She half believed him. She had got that from her grandfather, who had half believed most things promised on television, and read every word anyway.

Nothing can go faster than light, and light takes years to reach even the nearest star. The man in Montana didn't want to go faster. He wanted to move space itself: squeeze it up in front of a ship and let it out behind, so that the ship sat still in a pocket between and was carried, like a man on a moving walkway who gets to the far end of an airport without running. He was building the engine in a hole in the ground, in an old missile field outside a town called Bozeman, out of a missile.

In the winter he wrote: *April. Come and see it go. Or come and see it not go, which will be funnier.* He signed it as he signed them all, *Z. Cochrane*, and spelled *funnier* with one *n*.

---

She told them at Sunday lunch.

Her father asked whether the man had ever got anything to work. Nora said nobody had, ever, so it would be hard to hold it against him. Theo, in from the coast for the weekend, wanted to know whether there was any sea in Montana, and was disappointed. He had been telling them about the cod. Last summer, for the first time in his working life, the cod had spawned in the deep place off Bornholm where the floor of the sea had been dead, and the eggs had lived.

"I'm coming," said Alma.

Alma was thirty-six. She taught the piano at their grandmother's bench, and ran at five every morning on the shore path, by the feel of it under her feet, because she had seen almost nothing since the winter after the shelter, when the lenses of her eyes clouded over. She had been on a list for the operation for nine years. The list had moved for a great many people in nine years. It had not moved much for her, and nobody had ever said why. Her name and her address had been on the internet for ten years, on the register of Augments, for anybody who cared to look.

"I'd like to be somewhere nobody's looked me up," she said. "And if it goes, I want to hear it."

Their mother didn't say anything at the table. But in the next three weeks every form the journey needed came off the printer in the hall, filled in, two copies of each, and on the morning they left Anna stood at the door with all of it in a plastic folder, Alma's papers at the front.

"Bring her home," she said, to Nora.

"I will."

Anna held on to the folder a moment after Nora had taken hold of the other end. She had promised her own father something once, on a bench by a field in Ohio, and she had broken it, and everybody at that door knew. Then she let go.

---

It took them eleven days: a cargo ship to Halifax, trains where the lines still ran, and the back of a farm truck for the last two hundred miles. The war had been over ten years. Twice the train went past green road signs with the names of cities on them, still pointing the way, to places that weren't there any more.

The camp was a scatter of huts and trailers among the low concrete hatches of the old missile field, with a windmill, a water tank, a bar with a jukebox, and fifty or so people who had lived through the war by keeping their rifles close. They had kept them since, too, against hungry people, and against raiders from the Eastern Coalition, the armies from the east that had come over the mountains twice since the war ended, and against whatever came next. A man on the gate looked at the sisters, and at their papers, and at the sisters again, before he lifted the bar.

Zefram Cochrane was in the bar, and the jukebox was playing something very loud and very old. He was not the man in the letters. Or he was exactly the man in the letters, and Nora had not known what the letters looked like at three in the morning.

"You're the one who wrote back," he said, and bought her a drink. Before she had finished it he told her that he had built the thing to get rich, and that once he was rich he was going to live on a beach somewhere and never look at another equation as long as he lived. Then he spent an hour arguing with her about an equation.

Lily Sloane was his partner, who had built the ship with him, and who kept him sober enough to finish it. She had spent six months scavenging titanium out of the ruins for its cockpit, and she looked at the sisters the way the man on the gate had. But on the second day she took Alma down into the silo, where it was cold and echoing and smelled of oil, and put Alma's hands flat on the side of the ship.

Alma stood with her palms on the metal, listening to the pumps.

"It's cold," she said. "It's bigger than the stairwell at home."

"It was a missile," said Lily. "Built to end the world, before the last war. We had a better idea for it."

That afternoon a man asked Alma why a blind woman could carry two sacks of cement up a ramp without stopping, and she told him, because she had stopped hiding it the spring she was eighteen. "I'm an Augment," she said. The camp went quiet.

The man was called Lyle. He had a son who had gone east to fight in the Eugenics Wars and never come home. That night he sat outside the trailer where the sisters slept, on an upturned crate, with his rifle across his knees, and he was there in the morning. Nora couldn't tell whether he was guarding them or watching them.

"Both," said Alma. "Probably both."

---

On the last night before the flight, something in the sky fired on the camp.

It came late: lights falling out of a clear sky onto the silo, and the ground jumping, and a noise Nora felt in her teeth. She was out of the trailer with her hand on Alma's arm before she knew she was awake. Everybody had a rifle, and everybody was pointing it up. The camp said it was the Eastern Coalition, because it always was, and for the rest of the night men lay along the edge of the field with their rifles aimed at a sky that did nothing more.

Some of the men on the night shift in the silo did not come up.

There were strangers in the silo before morning, working on the ship, people nobody in the camp had seen before, and by the next night they had gone. In all the weeks the sisters stayed, nobody Nora asked could tell her who they had been.

In the morning, at the water tank, she heard a woman say that it was a funny thing, wasn't it, the sky coming down the same week an Augment turned up. Nobody answered her. Nobody told her to stop, either.

---

The ship went up in the late morning.

The camp stood on the slope above the silo, at a distance Lily had paced out, and Nora told Alma what she saw. The great lid of the silo grinding back. Smoke, first, coming up out of the ground in a slow white column. Then the fire under it, and the sound, which Alma didn't need telling about, because it came up through the soles of her feet and into her chest, and she laughed out loud with her hands over her ears.

"It's going," said Nora. "It's going up. It's above the hills. It's a light. It's—"

It was a missile, going up with a man in it who wanted to live on a beach. Her brother had once poured something into a dead sea off the back of the ship he had laid mines from. People did that, Nora thought: they took what they had and made it into something else, because it was what they had.

"*Hyvää matkaa*," she said, under her breath.

In the middle of the afternoon a shout came up out of the bunker where somebody was listening to the radio, and then a great many shouts. For a little while, somewhere above the sky, a ship with a man in it had moved faster than light. The man was coming home. By evening he was in the bar, and so was everybody else.

---

It was full dark when the light came down out of the sky again.

It came slowly, from very high, and it got bigger. The last time a light had come out of this sky, the night before, it had killed men in the silo. So the bar emptied in a moment and the camp ran for its rifles, and when the thing came down over the field, out of a noise like wind, fifty people stood along the line of trucks at the edge of it with their rifles up.

It was a ship. It was the size of a building, and it settled on its legs in the middle of the field, lit underneath, and sat there making a sound like breathing.

"Tell me," said Alma.

Nora told her. She told it the way she had told her the cloud on the horizon in the shelter summer: what was there, and nothing that wasn't. A ship, not one of ours. Lights. A ramp coming down out of its belly. Figures at the top of the ramp, three, four, in long robes with hoods.

Near the bar, out in the light, Cochrane had walked forward on his own, with a few people behind him.

The first of the figures came down the ramp and put back its hood. From the trucks Nora could see a man, or something shaped like one, tall, with dark hair cut straight across. He raised his hand with the fingers parted in a way she had never seen a hand held. Cochrane tried to do the same thing back and couldn't manage it. Then he held out his hand instead, and after a moment the stranger took it.

"They're shaking hands," said Nora. Her voice did something. "Alma. They're shaking hands."

Somebody near the bar said afterward that the stranger had wished them a long life, and that his people had seen the little ship's flight from out among the stars, and come to see who had made it.

Cochrane took them into the bar. The door stood open, and the jukebox came out across the field, an old rock-and-roll song about nothing at all. Along the trucks nobody lowered a rifle. A man laughed, too loudly, and stopped.

After a while one of the visitors came out of the bar alone, and started back toward the ship. Halfway across the field he stopped, and turned, and looked out at the dark where the trucks were.

He could see them. Nora was sure of it. He stood in the light with his hands at his sides and looked at fifty rifles, and didn't move, and didn't go in.

"What's he doing?" said Alma.

"Standing there. Looking at us."

"What does he look like?"

Nora looked. "Tall. Grey robe. His eyebrows go up at the ends, like a question." She looked again, to be sure. "His ears are pointed."

"You're making it up."

"I'm not."

"You always say that."

"I want to see him," said Alma.

There was only one way she could. Alma saw a face from as close as a kiss, and from no further.

---

Nora knew what it would look like from the trucks.

It would look like the Augment going to them. The camp had been fired on from the sky the night before, and buried its dead that morning, and a woman at the water tank had already put the two things together. Lyle was three trucks along, with his rifle laid across the roof of his cab and his face like a shut door. If one man along this line was frightened enough, or angry enough, or only startled, a shot would come from somewhere, and afterward nobody would be able to say who had fired first. People had said that about every war she had ever read about.

And nobody knew what the visitor would do. Nobody on Earth knew what anybody like him would do.

She saw her mother at the door with the folder, holding on to the other end of it. *Bring her home.* If a shot came, Nora would be the one to stand in that kitchen and tell her. She would have walked her sister out under fifty rifles because Alma wanted to look at a face, and she would have to say so to the woman who had once said *you can* to Alma at a shelter door and had lived with it ever since.

She could stay. It was the sensible thing; it was what the line of trucks was for. The man from the sky had come to meet Cochrane, and he had met him. Nothing more was needed of anybody tonight. In a minute he would turn and go up his ramp, and he would take with him what he had seen of the rest of them, which was a row of rifles in the dark.

She thought of a knock on a steel door, three blows with a fist, not loud, on the first night in the shelter. She had stood with her hand flat on the inside of that door and not opened it, and she had never found out who it was. And she thought of what she had said to her brother at their parents' table, about the first one we ever met: that if anybody ever came, they would want to know what we did with it.

He was standing in the field, waiting. He had been waiting the whole time she had been thinking. Nobody was going.

Nora looked at her own hands. There was nothing in them.

"Come on, then," she said, and took her sister's arm.

---

They went out between two trucks into the field.

"Where do you think you're going?" said a man's voice behind them. Then another, sharper, "Get back here," and a third that said only the one word, *Augment*, the way people had been saying it all Alma's life. Somewhere along the line a rifle bolt went back and forward. Nora didn't turn round. She kept her free hand out from her side, open, where anybody could see it was empty.

"Rut," she said. "Big one. Now flat. Now a stone."

Alma counted the steps under her breath, as she had once counted stairs.

Every one of them was a step further from the trucks, and from the promise. Nora felt her mother's hand on the other end of the folder the whole way. Behind them the line was so quiet that she could hear the jukebox clearly, and the breathing sound of the ship, and her own heart. She waited for the shot. It didn't come. In front of them the visitor watched them come and didn't move, except that his eyebrows went a little further up.

They stopped in front of him. Close to, he was taller than she had thought, and older, and very still.

Nora had thought about what she would say for the whole width of the field, and none of it was any use.

"My sister can't see well," she said. "She'd like to look at you. She has to come very close."

The visitor looked at Alma for some time. Then he inclined his head, which Nora took to be yes.

Alma let go of Nora's arm and stepped forward, slowly, with her chin up, until she was near enough to see, and looked.

Nobody in the field said anything. The visitor didn't step back. He looked at her too, and Nora understood that he was doing the same thing Alma was doing: finding out.

"Nora," said Alma.

"Yes."

"You're not making it up."

Nora laughed, and found that she was also crying, and couldn't have said which had started first.

"Your people are armed," said the visitor. His English was careful and very correct.

"Last night something came out of the sky and killed some of them," said Nora. "They're frightened."

"And you are not?"

"I'm terrified," said Alma. She hadn't stepped back. "I wanted to see you."

The visitor considered that, the way Nora had seen her grandmother consider a pupil's excuse.

"That is also why we came," he said.

---

Behind them, somebody was walking out across the field.

It was a boy of about ten, from the trailer by the water tank, who had got away from his mother and was waving at the ship with his whole arm. Then it was his mother, running after him, and then, because she had gone, two men who had stood beside her at the trucks, and then a woman with a lantern, and then more of them, in ones and twos, the way people come out onto a street after a storm to see what's left. Some of them still carried their rifles, pointing at the ground. Most didn't.

Lyle didn't come. Nora saw him, when she looked back, still at his truck. But his rifle was leaning against the bumper now, and his hands were in his pockets, and he stayed like that, watching, until the field was full of people looking up at a ship and asking a stranger questions he answered one at a time.

Not everybody came. Some stood at the trucks with their rifles until it was light.

---

Nora didn't sleep. A little before dawn, when it was the middle of the afternoon in Helsinki, she sat on the step of the trailer, at the one place in the camp where her phone could find a signal, and wrote to her parents.

She wrote down what had happened, in order: the ship, the light, the handshake, the field, the visitor's eyebrows, the boy. Her whole life she had improved everything she put into words. She read this through twice, and there was nothing in it to improve, and nothing she wanted to leave out. She sent it as it was.

Her father answered first, within the hour. *Your mother has read it to me three times. I have it by heart.* A little later, from the coast, there was one from Theo, who had been forwarded it: *Did anybody tell them about the sea?*

Nora sat with the phone on her knee and watched the ship standing in the field in the first of the light. Her grandfather had half believed in the eight going to Jupiter, and read every word anyway. She would have liked to tell him that the other half was true as well.

In the morning a few people wouldn't stand near Alma at the water tank, and some of them never did, for as long as the sisters were there. A girl of about nine came up to her instead and asked what his face had looked like close to.

"Eyebrows," said Alma, and the girl nodded, as if she'd thought so.

There was still a jar in a drawing in a piano bench in Helsinki with nothing written under it, and there might always be. Nobody had been able to ask it. But last night, in that field, there had been no question which word. The stranger had stood in the light and waited, and been looked at, and looked back, and answered when he was spoken to.

*Joku*, Nora thought. Somebody.
