# Chapter 15: Alma

Alma Novak was the only person in the family who had ever caught her sister out.

Nora was fifteen, and she still improved everything she read aloud. For two years, since Alma's eyes had begun to go, she had also been describing the world to her: the snow on the balcony railing, the faces on the tram, the colour of whatever was for supper. One evening in February she told Alma that a man had gone past the kitchen window on a bicycle, wearing a pineapple.

"We're on the fourth floor," said Alma.

There was a silence at the table. It wasn't allowed.

"It was a very tall bicycle," said Nora, and Alma laughed so hard that she slid off her chair, and Ben caught her, because somebody always caught her now.

She was eight. She could still tell light from dark, and a window from a wall, and her mother from her father by their shapes against the light. She couldn't see faces any more, or the board at school, where she was learning to read with her fingers, in raised dots. In the autumn she had had her first fit, on the kitchen floor, and now there was a tablet with her breakfast for that. When she was tired her words came slowly, as if each one had to be fetched from another room. She went up the stairs of the building counting out loud, sixty-four, and if anybody talked to her on the way she started again from the bottom.

In the evenings her grandmother taught her the piano by feel.

The piano was the real one, upright and secondhand, that had come up four flights for Ruth. Ruth was seventy-nine, and well, and she sat at the bass end with Alma beside her and set the child's hands on the keys for her, finger by finger. Alma could play the twinkling one with both hands now, and the start of a minuet. When Ruth's left hand shook, as it always had, Alma could feel it through the bench.

"Your hand's doing it," said Alma.

"It's been doing it since before your mother was born," said Ruth. "It still knows where the notes are. So do yours. Again."

---

The envelope from Ohio came at the end of February, nearly two years after Ben had written for it.

He had written in the week Alma was diagnosed, to the state's office of vital statistics in Columbus, for a copy of his sister's death certificate. Nothing had come back, because nothing came back from Ohio then. The war had ended on paper, but the offices that kept America's records were still finding out which of them had kept anything. Then one Tuesday there it was on the mat, with an American stamp and a Finnish sticker over the address where it had been forwarded twice.

He opened it at the kitchen table. It was one page, a photocopy of a form filled in by hand in 1989.

*Katherine Ann Novak. Age: 9.* His father's name was on the line for the person who had given the details. Halfway down, in a doctor's capitals, was a box headed *Cause of death*, and in it:

*Aspiration pneumonia.*

And under that, on the line for what had caused it:

*Neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (Batten disease), late infantile.*

The gene had no name yet then. Nobody would find it for another twelve years. But it was the same illness. It was Alma's.

Ben sat with the page until the coffee beside him was cold. Then he remembered something he hadn't thought about since he was a boy. Every August until he was twelve, his grandmother had taken him on the bus to an eye doctor downtown in Toledo, though he had never needed glasses. She stood behind the doctor's chair while he read the letters off the wall, all the way to the bottom line. He had thought it was just her way of being thorough, like the socks. She had been watching for it. Every summer she had stood there waiting to see whether he would fail to read the bottom line, and every summer he hadn't, and she had taken him home on the bus and never said a word.

He took the page to Ruth, who was at the piano going over the minuet, and held it out. She put on her glasses and read it the way she read every form, to the bottom.

"Well," she said. "Now somebody's said."

"If anybody had told me," said Ben, "we'd have been tested. Anna and me. Before Nora. We'd have known."

Anna had come to the doorway. She didn't ask what they would have done with knowing. She didn't need to; they could all see it. At the end of that road there was no Alma, counting her way up the stairs.

"Then I'm glad nobody told you," said Anna.

Ruth took off her glasses.

"You'd have had the choice," she said. "That's all telling anybody ever gets you. Somebody should have, all the same."

---

On the first Sunday in March, at the hour of the old call, Carol rang from Toronto.

"It's ready," she said. "Bastien says the network for CLN6 is done. They'd take her in April." She didn't soften the next part, because Anna had asked her long ago never to. "What's gone stays gone, Anna. It mends what's failing. Every month there's a little more that's gone." And then, because she was Carol: "Bring her. Before anybody hands you a pen, I read you every page. That's still the deal."

Everybody in the family already knew what the network did. They had been carrying it for two years. It would save her. It would make her more than saved. It could never be taken out, and it would go down to any children she had. In Canada it was a crime to give it to her.

And the world had made up its mind what to call it. Three countries kept registers now. In Finland's parliament that spring, one party wanted a register too, and it was the same party that had wanted fewer foreigners. Children had found the word before their parents had. Theo had heard it in his school's yard, about a boy who could do too many pull-ups. Alma had heard it in hers, about a girl who won every race, and the girl had cried.

---

They talked about it that night, after Alma was in bed, round the kitchen table: Ben and Anna, Nora and Theo, and Ruth, who said she was too old to be sent to her room.

Nora had read everything. She had read about the American trial, before Alma was born, that had put a good copy of the gene into children's spines and held them steady for two years, and then hadn't. She had read about the twins in China in 2018, and the country on the far side of the world that had begun doing it on purpose. She was going to be a scientist, and she believed in it the way her grandmother believed in commas.

"She'd be better," said Nora. "Not just well. Better. That's what they're all so scared of." She looked round the table. "So we do it, and we tell everybody. We make them look at her. If we hide her, it's like saying they're right."

"If we tell everybody, they close the Institute," said Anna. "Every child it ever treated is on a list by Christmas. And Carol and Joy lose everything."

"So we lie."

"So we don't say."

"That's what Grandpa used to do," said Nora, and nobody at the table had an answer to that, because it was true.

Ben hadn't said anything. He worked for a company whose machines kept everything anybody had ever put on them, and he knew what happened to records. They were kept, and kept, and then one day somebody needed them for something.

"What if it's found?" he said. "Not now. In ten years. Twenty. When they've got their register."

Nobody could tell him. He found, sitting there, that some part of him wanted the other road: Alma at home, loved, counted by nobody, for as long as she had. It was the only road where nobody would ever come to the door for her. He was ashamed of how much he wanted it, because he understood that what he would really be choosing on that road was not to be afraid.

Ruth put her hands flat on the table.

"Most of my life I took a medicine that made me less than I was," she said. "Less of everything. My hands. Me. I took it because the other thing was worse." She looked at Ben. "Now somebody's offering that child one that makes her more, and you're sitting here wondering."

"They'll count her."

"They counted me. They put me behind a fence with a card round my neck and a letter beside my name." Ruth didn't raise her voice. "I taught the piano anyway."

Theo had been turning a fork over and over. He was thirteen, and the sea was his, and he said things when they occurred to him.

"If she doesn't have it, she dies," he said. "That's the only thing anybody's said that's actually about her."

Anna looked at her son, and then across the table at Ben. Her mother had once written down, in a good year, that Anna could do something terrible to her. Nobody had written anything down for Alma. She was eight.

"Toronto," said Ben. "We hear every page. And we ask her."

Nora pushed her chair back. At the door she stopped.

"I won't tell anybody," she said. "Ever. You know I won't." She held on to the door frame. "But I'm not going to say it isn't a lie."

---

They told Alma on the Tuesday, at bedtime, in her own room. Ba was in the bed with her: the knitted rabbit with one eye sewn back on, that had been Nora's since San Francisco and was Alma's now, because a girl who can't see needs a rabbit more than a girl of fifteen does, Nora said.

Anna sat on the bed. Ben took the chair.

"There's a hospital in Canada," said Anna. "Where Carol works. They've made a medicine for what you've got. It goes in once, with a needle, and it mends the parts of you that are getting sick. Your eyes might get better. Your legs. And you wouldn't die of this."

Alma lay very still, holding Ba by the ear.

"But it does something else as well," said Anna, "and it can't be taken out again. It makes the rest of you stronger than other people. Quicker. You'd hardly ever be ill. And if you grow up and have children one day, they'll be like that too." She made herself go on. "Some people are frightened of people like that. There's a word for it."

"Augment," said Alma.

"Yes."

"Elias says it. To Minea, because she wins." Alma thought. "Would I be faster than Theo?"

"Probably," said Ben.

Alma seemed to find that worth having. Then she found the next thing.

"Would people say it to me?"

"Not if they don't know," said Anna. "That's the hardest part. Nobody can know. Not your teacher, not anybody at school. Only us and Grandma and Carol. Not ever."

"Not even Venla?"

"Not even Venla."

"Like a surprise?"

"No," said Anna. "Surprises end."

Alma was quiet until Anna thought she had fallen asleep. When she spoke again her words had begun to come slowly, as they did at night.

"If I don't have it, I'll die?"

"Yes," said Anna. "Not this year. But yes."

"Like Katie," said Alma. She knew about Katie. She had held the photograph once and been told whose it was, and since then she had asked about her often, because Katie was hers.

Ben leaned forward in the chair.

"Katie had what you have," he said. "There wasn't a medicine then. Nobody had one."

"Did she count the stairs?"

"I don't know," said Ben. "I was too small. Nobody ever told me anything about her." He found that his voice was not going to manage much more. "So I'm telling you everything."

Alma turned her face toward his shape against the lamp.

"Will it hurt?"

"A needle in your back," said Anna. "Once. They make the place numb first."

Alma considered the needle. Then she considered something else, and they could both see her arrive at it.

"Then I could see if Nora's making it up," she said.

Ben laughed, and then had to put his hand over his face.

"Yes," said Anna.

"Then I want it." Alma moved Ba under her chin. "Can I tell Ba?"

"Ba can know."

When she was asleep, Anna sat on in the dark. It was a yes, and she would hold on to it. It was also an eight-year-old's yes, to catching her sister out, and Anna made herself know what she would have done if it had been no. She would have taken her to Toronto anyway. She had asked because Alma had a right to be asked and to know the truth, not because the answer could change anything, and she wasn't going to pretend to herself that it was more than that.

On Wednesday, walking to the tram with Antti Laine, Ben asked him what he would do. He didn't say what about. Antti didn't ask. He thought about it for a whole street, walking his bicycle.

"I would do it," he said. "Then I would not sleep."

---

At the Institute, on Monday the ninth of April, Joy took Alma down to the lounge, where there was a piano nobody played, and left her with somebody she trusted, and came back up. Carol shut her office door.

She read them every page, aloud, slowly, in the voice she had once used at a county counter. What the network was. That it would go in once, through a needle into the fluid round Alma's spine, and write itself into every cell she had. What it would mend, and what it couldn't bring back. What it would make of her besides, and of every child she ever had, and of theirs. That under a Canadian law of 2004, giving it to her was a crime that carried ten years, and that the paper they would sign said they understood that. It took an hour. She didn't skip a line.

Then she put the form down, and took the pen out of the neck of her cardigan, where Anna had never seen it missing, and laid it on the desk.

"There's one more thing," she said. "It isn't on any page."

She looked at Joy, by the window, and Joy nodded.

"If you ever tell anybody what I'm going to tell you," said Carol, "I lose Canada. Joy goes to prison. That's not why I'm saying it. I'm saying it so you know what you're holding." She was quiet a moment. "A family from Manila asked me if there was anything else they should know. I told them no. I'm not telling you that."

Anna had known Carol for nearly fifteen years, and had never heard her stop in the middle of saying something. She stopped now. Then she told them.

Down in the sub-basement were fourteen boxes. In 2012 couples at a fertility clinic who had made more embryos than they needed had given the spares away, for research only, and ticked a box saying they understood none would ever be used to make a child. One of the Institute's scientists, whose name was printed on none of the forms, had made fourteen. He had changed them before they were born, and raised them in this building, and signed for them every year, once as their guardian and once as the man experimenting on them, in the same pen. Every network upstairs had been learned on those fourteen. Alma's too.

"One of them's downstairs," said Carol. "With your daughter."

Nobody said anything.

"The couples," said Anna at last. "Do they know?"

"No."

Somewhere there were couples who had given away a few cells in a dish. One of them had a son they didn't know about, and he was downstairs. And Anna's daughter was going to see again by what had been learned on him.

Ben said it first. He said it plainly, because there was no other way to say it.

"Then we'd be using it. What he did to them."

"Yes," said Carol.

"Every family upstairs is."

"Every family upstairs doesn't know. You do."

Joy turned round from the window.

"The law's right," she said. "Whoever wrote it was picturing exactly what that man did. That's the worst of it." She looked at Ben, and then at Anna. "I've nursed here fourteen years anyway. I'm not going to tell you what to do with that."

Ben stood up. He said he wanted to see Alma before anybody signed anything, and Carol said that was the most sensible thing anybody had said in that office in two years.

---

In the lounge a young man was on the bench beside Alma, unshaven, in a university sweatshirt, playing the left hand of the twinkling one so badly that she had both hands over her mouth.

"That's wrong," she was saying. "That's so wrong."

"I've been practising," said the young man. "This is me practised."

Ben knew who he was before Alma said his name. Khan.

"She asked me if I was one," said Khan, when Ben sat down. He said it without lowering his voice, because Alma was right there and it was hers to hear. "I said yes. She asked me what it's like. I said I'd never been anything else, so I couldn't really tell her."

"It's a rubbish answer," said Alma.

"It's a rubbish answer," Khan agreed, and played the bass wrong again on purpose, and she shrieked.

When she had gone back to the minuet, Ben said, low, "Carol told us. About the boxes."

"Ah," said Khan. He watched Alma's hands. "And now you'd like me to tell you it's all right."

Ben didn't say no.

"I can't," said Khan. "I'm what it was done to. Every child who walks out of this building walks out on us, and I'm glad of every one of them, and if anybody could find the old man I'd stand up in a court and say what he was." He was quiet a moment. "She was asked. That's more than he ever did for us."

"She's eight."

"I know how old she is." Khan smiled, crooked, the smile of somebody used to it working. "She'll be one of us, Mr. Novak. There are going to be a great many of us. One day there'll be enough of us that nobody has to ask anybody's permission for anything."

Ben couldn't tell whether that was meant as a comfort. He thought Khan couldn't either.

He sat on the end of the bench and watched his daughter laugh with an Augment. He had been afraid of a word. Now he was afraid of something with a face, which was kind to his daughter, and he didn't know what to do with that.

---

Upstairs the form lay on Carol's desk. On the line for the patient there was nothing, because the patient was eight. Under it was a line for whoever spoke for her.

Anna thought of the girl at school who won every race and cried. She thought of Alma told every day of her life that the one thing about herself she must never say was the thing that had saved her. She thought of a child Alma might have one day, who would not have been asked either, and who might hold this form against her. She would rather be blamed by that child than have no such child to blame her.

And she thought of fourteen children nobody had asked at all, and of the old man's pen on both lines. Whatever she wrote now, she would be one of the people who had taken what he made, knowing how he made it. The form had no line for that. She had never yet signed anything she knew was wrong. She was going to, and she was going to know it afterward, every time she looked at her daughter's eyes.

She signed. Ben took the pen and signed under her. Carol initialled the line for the witness, small and neat. In the margin, where she always did, she wrote what the patient liked to be called. She had asked Alma that morning, and Alma had said "Alma," as if it were obvious.

---

On Tuesday a doctor put the needle into Alma's back, low down, between two bones, while Alma lay curled on her side with Ba under her chin, and Anna sat in front of her and held her right hand.

"Ow," said Alma, in a tone of great reproach.

Then it was done. A little clear liquid, and that was all. It would be months before anybody knew how much of her it would bring back.

---

At the end of May the light had come back over Helsinki so far that it was still light at ten at night, and the family took the ferry out to Suomenlinna, because Theo had decided that his sister should see the sea.

She couldn't see it the way he did. But she could see more than in March. She could see the brightness where the water was, and the darker line of the islands, and the gulls as quick grey marks. A great white ship bound across the sea slid out past them with all its windows lit, and Alma knew it was a ship before anybody told her.

"*Laiva*," she said.

Theo, who had learned that word on this ferry when he was a year old, said nothing at all for a moment.

Nora was on Alma's other side. "There's a dolphin," she said. "Right by the window. Doing a flip."

"There isn't," said Alma. "I'd see it."

Nora laughed until people looked.

On the bench across from them a woman from their building, who had listened to Alma counting her way up the stairs all winter, leaned over to Anna and said in Finnish that the little one was doing so well. It was a miracle. What had they done?

"A trial," said Anna, in Finnish. "In Canada. We were lucky."

It was the first lie she had ever told about her daughter, and she heard her father in it, as plainly as if he were on the bench beside her. *Everything's fine.* She had promised herself on the stairs at eleven that her children would have the truth, and they had it. They had all of it. It was everybody else who would have to make do with *fine*, for the rest of Alma's life.

Nora had heard. She opened her mouth, and Anna saw her decide, and shut it again. She turned to the window and watched the islands go by with her jaw set, the way she had held the door frame in March.

Alma had heard too. She sat between her brother and her sister with her face to the brightness and her hands in her lap, and said nothing, the way she had been taught. She was eight, and very good at it already.
