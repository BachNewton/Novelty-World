# Chapter 7: Father's Day

Across the street, the school had its flag up. Finland keeps Father's Day in November, on the second Sunday, in the dark, with the flags out, and four years in, Ben Novak still found that faintly wonderful.

Nora had brought him coffee before it was light, most of it still in the cup, and a card in an envelope that he wasn't allowed to open until dinner. She had made it at school. She was seven, and had started in August, and she walked there by herself in the mornings with a reflector pinned to her coat, the way seven-year-olds do in Finland. Ben had not got used to it yet. She stood by the bed and watched him not open the card, to be sure.

When she had gone, his phone lit up. It was Mikko.

Mikko had sent a photograph: a card in a child's capitals, propped against a coffee cup on a breakfast table. *HYVÄÄ ISÄNPÄIVÄÄ*, it said, Happy Father's Day, and under that, smaller, a line Ben could read now without his phone's help. *ISI ON NYT AINA KOTONA.* Daddy is always home now.

Under it Mikko had written, in English: *Best review I've had all year.*

For six years Mikko had worked beside Ben: on his screen every weekday morning while Ben was still in San Francisco, and then at the next desk in Helsinki. He had found the Novaks their daycare by the sea, and he had been better at the video player than Ben was, which everybody knew, Mikko included. Then, the spring before last, Brynner gave most of the team's work to a machine. The Americans were let go by email. The Finns were let go after weeks of the meetings Finnish law insists on first, which Mikko called the politest sacking in Europe. Two of the team were kept, to check what the machine decided: Ben, and Declan Toner in Belfast. Nobody said why it was them. Ben had a guess. Their names were in a log somewhere, from a night in San Francisco when the two of them had sat at a pair of buttons and decided what the world saw.

Ben typed an answer, and deleted it, and typed another. In the end he sent a heart, which was the kind of thing he had always thought less of other people for sending, and pushed the phone under his pillow.

Mikko had been looking for work for a year and a half. Ben knew the rule by heart now, as everybody did who had been here on a work permit as long as he had: if his own job went, the family had six months to find another, or leave Finland.

---

In the afternoon Ben went down to the basement for the crib.

The baby was due in the middle of January, a girl, and Anna wanted the crib up by Christmas. Theo's old one was under the building, in pieces, tied with string, behind a steel door as thick as his hand. Nora came down to hold the door. It was heavy enough that she needed both hands and her shoulder, and she held it like a job.

Inside were the concrete walls and the air vents, and the storage cages where the building kept its skis and summer tyres. On the inside of the door hung the laminated notice Ben had first read with his phone held up to it. He could read most of it now. Nora could read all of it, and did, aloud, while he untied the string, and then stopped.

"What's it really for?" she said. "This room."

Ben had an answer ready. He had had it ready for four years: *skis*. It was true, and it was the only part he had ever wanted her to have.

He looked at his daughter with her shoulder against the door. Once, on a kitchen floor in Ohio, Anna had got down so that her face was level with Nora's and told her the truth about a game they played at preschool. Ben had watched from the table and thought he could never have done it.

He put the string down.

"It's a shelter," he said. "A long time ago, before Grandpa was born, Finland's big neighbour attacked it. Russia. So now every big building here has a room like this underneath, with a strong door, where everybody who lives upstairs can fit."

"Aino's great-grandma had to leave her house," said Nora. "In a war. She took a sewing machine."

"That's the war. The same one."

"Are they going to attack us?"

"I don't think so," said Ben. "I hope nobody ever needs this room. But if anybody ever did, there'd be a place for everyone. That's the rule here."

"Even us?"

He understood what she was asking. She had heard, somewhere, at school or at the playground, that they were from somewhere else.

"Even us," he said. "Everybody who lives upstairs."

Nora looked round at the walls again, slowly, as if she were measuring them. On the way up, with the crib's sides under his arm, Ben heard her ask whether there was a room like this under her school. There was. She seemed to find that a comfort. Ben, to his surprise, found that he did too.

---

By dinner Nora had been waiting all day, and she had decided to read the card to him herself.

Since she had learned to read in the summer, Nora had read everything aloud, and improved most of it. The milk carton said that milk was good for girls and bad for brothers. A note home from school had once said there would be no more school on Fridays, ever. Nobody had made a rule about it, but the family kept one anyway: you never caught her out.

Ben could have read the card himself, given a minute and nobody watching. But Nora held it up in both hands, facing him, the way her teacher held things up at school, and he let her.

"Dear Dad," she read, putting it into English as she went. "You are the best dad in Finland and in America. You are very good at losing at Uno. You are not good at singing."

The card had two lines on it, in a teacher's printing, under a drawing of a man with a very large head. Ben counted them twice. Across the table, Anna's mouth gave a small, betraying twitch. She had counted too. She caught his eye and looked quickly at her plate.

He told Nora it was a very good card.

"I know," said Nora. She leaned over to her little brother and said something in Finnish, so fast that Ben understood none of it, and Theo laughed until he had to put down his fork. Ben laughed too, a beat behind, at a joke he would never hear, at his own table. Across from him Anna shook her head: she hadn't caught it either. It happened most days now, and it was always a small thing. Each time, Ben felt it the way you feel a stair in the dark that isn't there.

Then his phone lit up beside his plate. He had promised not to look at it at the table, and he looked anyway. Most people did, that autumn.

On the screen, army trucks were crossing one of Pittsburgh's yellow bridges.

"They've sent the Guard into Pittsburgh," he said. "Trouble at the District."

He had once sat up all night watching a District's gate on a screen, four minutes behind, and he knew what trucks like that were for.

"Do we know anybody in Pittsburgh?" Anna asked.

"No."

People asked that question a great deal, at kitchen tables a long way from the trouble. It was how they measured how close a thing had come. Ben was relieved by the answer, for as long as he let himself be. Anna wasn't. She was already making the drive in her head: west out of Pittsburgh on the interstate, four hours, and then her parents' kitchen in Dayton, Ohio. She knew exactly how short four hours was.

She reached over, turned his phone face down, and left her hand on it. Ben saw how long the hand stayed there, and said nothing.

"Read Theo the part about singing," she said.

She said it with a perfectly straight face, which on Anna was the least straight face there was. There was no part about singing. She knew it, and Nora knew she knew it, and that was the game. It was also the quickest way she could think of to get Pittsburgh off the table.

Nora read it again in Finnish, with something added that made Theo shriek. Ben, to prove her right, sang. For the rest of dinner Anna laughed at everything the children said, always a moment after they did. Ben heard the moment every time, and sang louder.

---

At six Anna propped the tablet against the fruit bowl, and there was her father. It was eleven in the morning in Dayton.

"There's my girl. How's that baby? You look big."

"Thanks, Dad. Happy Father's Day."

"That's June."

"Here it's today. The flags are out."

"Dark by four, and Father's Day in November," said Walt. "What'll they think of next." Then he wanted to know about the big box he could see in the hall.

It had come on Thursday, a cardboard box the size of a small suitcase, with the government's name printed on the side. Nora had already been through it one thing at a time and taken the ball for herself. Anna carried the tablet out and showed him the rest: a snowsuit, a wool cap, sheets, socks no longer than her thumb, a bath thermometer, a picture book, tiny nail scissors. In the bottom lay a mattress, because the box was also the bed.

"Every baby gets one," she said. "Every baby in the country, since just after the war. All you have to do is see the nurse early, so they know you're coming."

Walt looked at it all without a word.

"When you were born," he said, "we got a bill."

Then: "Got a name yet, for that baby?"

"We've got a list. It has to work in two languages."

"Walter works."

"It's a girl, Dad."

"Doesn't stop it working."

He laughed, and shifted in his chair, and she watched him do it.

"So what's new?" she asked. "How's Mom?"

"Well, we had the election. You see we got the doctor in? The one from the TV, that first spring, with the numbers." He leaned toward the camera, pleased. "They ran her off, and now she's governor. First woman we ever had. I figured I owed her the one vote. Your mother didn't go."

"Mom didn't vote?"

"Didn't feel like it. First one she's missed since she was eighteen."

"Is she all right?"

"She's fine. She won't eat much. Soup, if she opens the can herself." Walt moved on. "We had rain Tuesday, so I didn't get to the gutters, but they'll keep. The Henderson boy took the garbage cans down for me. Your mother's been at her piano."

Over his shoulder, in the front hall, something stood by the door. Anna leaned toward the screen.

"Dad, what's that behind you? Is that a walker?"

Walt glanced back at it. "That's for the garbage cans. For wheeling them down to the street."

"The Henderson boy takes the garbage cans down. You just said."

"Well, there you go," said Walt. "It doesn't get much use."

Anna knew that voice. It was the one he used for *fine*.

Behind him, in the front room, the piano started. Ruth always played something at the end of the Sunday call, the same slow hymn every week, and until this autumn she had always played it to the end. Now she played a few bars of it, and stopped, and played the same few bars again, and stopped.

"She's practising," said Walt.

Then the piano stopped altogether, and there were footsteps, and Anna's mother was in the doorway.

She didn't come in. She stood with one hand on the frame and looked at the tablet from across the room. Her hair was flat on one side, as if she had slept on it, or had lain down and not slept.

"Walter. Who is that?"

"It's Annie, Ruthie," said Walt. "It's Sunday. Come and say hello."

"Hi, Mom," said Anna. "I like that sweater."

Her mother didn't move. Her eyes went from the screen to Walt and back. When she spoke again, her voice was low and fast, and Anna had heard it before, a long time ago, from the stairs.

"I know who you are. You're from the county. You've been listening through that thing." She pointed at the tablet without coming any nearer. "I take what I'm supposed to take. I don't need anybody's shot. You can write that down."

"Ruthie," said Walt.

"My daughter is fed. She's clean. She goes to school every day with a lunch I made her." Ruth's hand was white on the door frame. "You are not taking her anywhere."

Walt began to tell her again who it was, and Anna stopped him.

When Anna was eleven, her mother had spent an autumn in the hospital, and after she came home a woman from the county came every Thursday for a year with a clipboard. She looked in the fridge to see whether Anna was eating. Nobody told Anna what she was there to decide, but Anna watched from the stairs, and she understood that the woman could take her away, and she waited all year for the woman to tell her mother she was doing a good job. Nobody ever did.

"Mrs. Dorsey," said Anna, "I'm writing it down right now. She's fed, and she's clean, and she goes to school. You're doing a good job. She's staying right where she is."

Her mother looked at the screen, and went on looking.

"You'll put that in your report."

"I'll put it in my report."

Some of the fear went out of Ruth's shoulders. Not all of it. "Well," she said. "Thank you. That's kind." She went back down the hall, and after a moment the same few bars began again.

Walt rubbed his face with his whole hand. He looked like a man who hadn't slept properly in weeks, and Anna understood that he hadn't.

"Don't mind that," he said. "That's a bad morning, is all. She's been a little off since the clinic closed."

"Since what closed?"

He didn't answer, so she asked again. "Dad. Dolores's clinic? What do you mean, closed?"

"First week of September. Sign on the door. Dolores called me herself, and cried." He shook his head. "It's the money. Washington's cutting it, and they shut before it ran out."

"So who's giving her the shot?"

"There's a fella at the county. Pruitt. Calls when he says he will, I'll give him that. He's trying to get her on a list in Columbus." Walt looked at something off the screen. "He says there's places downtown, too. For folks who can't manage. I told him we're managing."

Anna knew what was downtown: a fence by the river, from the water to the railroad tracks, which the county had voted for two Septembers ago. She was on a bench by a hangar again, at the edge of a mown field, with a grey plane going up beyond the trees. *Don't you let them put your mother in one of those places.*

"Columbus is an hour from you, Dad. You don't drive on the highway any more."

"I've got a call in," said Walt. "Annie. I've got a call in. You look after that baby. You don't need this. It's Father's Day, isn't it? Everything's fine here."

His hand slipped on the tablet. It tipped back, and for a few seconds the two of them looked together at the ceiling fan in Dayton. Then the call was gone.

---

Anna stood in the hall with the tablet in her hands and the box at her feet.

*When I am ill, I will tell you I am perfectly well. I will tell you the injection is poison and that you are not who you are. Do not listen to me. Listen to this.*

Her mother had first written that at a bank, in the summer of the virus, so that somebody would know what to do on exactly this morning, and she had written *Anna Novak* on the line for the person who would decide. This August, a month before the clinic closed, she had copied it out again word for word, as she did every three years, and put it back in the piano bench under the hymnal. It was there now, on the other side of an ocean.

In the bathroom Ben was running the water and asking Theo, very patiently, to please sit down. Anna sat on the hall floor beside the box and rang Dolores.

Dolores answered on the first ring, as if she had been holding the phone.

"Anna."

"You said you'd tell me," said Anna. "On the porch. You said, not my father. You."

"I know what I said." Dolores's voice was older than Anna remembered it. "I went round in October. Twice. She talked to me through the screen door, Anna. She said I was the one who brought the poison. Me." She stopped. "Your father asked me not to come again. He said it made her worse. It did, for a day. And every week he tells me she's fine, and every week I let him, because you're carrying, and I didn't want to be the one who put you on a plane."

Anna said nothing. She hadn't known she could be angry with Dolores, and she found the anger had nowhere to go.

"I can't give it to her if she says no," said Dolores. "Nobody can. You know what can."

"The paper."

"The paper. And two doctors, and you." A pause. "Pruitt's a good man. But the county can only do one thing for her, and it's downtown."

"Will you go round again?"

"Tomorrow," said Dolores. "She can shut the door on me. I'll stand on the porch."

---

At bedtime Nora was still thinking about it. She had been in the hall, and heard.

"Mom? Why did Grandma think you were from the county?" She tried the word again. "What's the county?"

Anna tucked the blanket in along her side and said, in Finnish, "*Ei se mitään*." It means *never mind*, and *it's nothing*, and *it's fine*, all at once. It is one of the first things anyone learns in Finland, and she got every sound of it right.

"That was good, Mom," said Nora.

Anna heard it as soon as it was out. It was her father's word, in another language. She had promised herself, at eleven, on the stairs, that she would tell her children the truth about things. She sat down on the edge of the bed.

"Grandma's sick," she said. "Not like a cold. It's a sickness in how her mind works. Sometimes it makes her believe things that aren't true. Today it made her believe I was somebody else."

"She didn't know you?"

"She knows me. She knows exactly who I am. The sickness made her believe I was a different person. Do you remember her hand, when she played the piano with you? How it shook?"

Nora nodded. "It's her medicine."

"That's right. She has a shot in her arm every month, and it keeps the sickness away. The place where she got it closed. So she hasn't had it for a while, and the sickness has come back."

"Who's the county?"

"A long time ago, when I was eleven, Grandma was sick like this, and she had to go to a hospital. After she came home, a lady came to our house every week to check that I was all right. Grandma was afraid the lady would take me away from her. So when she's frightened, she thinks it's that lady again."

Nora thought about this with the knitted rabbit tucked under her chin.

"Would the lady take you away?"

"No. Grandma was doing a good job."

"Is Grandma frightened now?"

"Yes," said Anna. "I think she is."

"Can she come here?" Nora sat up. "Dad says there's a room for everybody."

"That room's for everybody who lives here. Grandma lives in Ohio." Anna smoothed the blanket. "Finland has rules about who can come and live. For Grandma to come, I'd have to be a Finn first. I'm learning."

Nora gave this the weight it deserved.

"You're not very good yet," she said.

"No," said Anna. "Not yet."

---

Above the kitchen sink, as above nearly every kitchen sink in Finland, there was a cupboard with no floor: the wet plates went in a rack inside and dripped into the sink behind a closed door. Anna had lived with one for four years, and she still dried every plate with a towel before she put it away, as her mother always had.

Ben came in from the children's room. "They're both down. You know you don't have to dry those."

"Nine Sundays," said Anna. "The clinic closed the first week of September, and he's sat there nine Sundays and told me about the gutters. Dolores went round. Mom wouldn't open the door to her." She put the plate away. "Nobody can give her the shot if she says no. Only if two doctors see her and the paper in the piano bench says yes for her. And the paper says me." She took out another plate. "I could go. Walk her into a hospital with it."

Ben had known since the call that she would say it. He had worked out the answer in the bathroom, and it came out the same every time.

"You're thirty weeks," he said. "They'll fly you to thirty-six, with a letter. Then you're home by Christmas or you're having her there, and nothing pays for that over there."

"I know."

"Declan covered me three days in June, when Theo had the flu. There are two of us, Anna. Six weeks, and somebody in San Francisco notices the machine only needs one." He picked up a towel. "Antti would have the kids. That's not the problem."

"You could go instead."

"She'd think I was the police," he said, gently. "And the paper doesn't say Ben."

"And she can't come here." Anna said it before he could. "I looked it up again, while you did the bath. With the course, it would have been the August after Theo turned four. Now it's the August after he turns five, and only if I pass an exam I haven't even sat. And then I can start asking."

The rule had changed the autumn before last, while she wasn't looking. Finland had chosen a new government their first spring here, with the party that wanted fewer foreigners in it, and that party had done what it said it would.

She dried the plate she was holding, though it was dry.

"I promised him," she said.

"Who?"

"Dad. The spring we were there, at the Wright brothers' field. He asked me not to let them put her in one of those places, if anything happened to him. Like the one downtown. He wouldn't take *I'll try*. So I promised." She put the plate away. "He told me not to tell Mom. I didn't tell you either."

Ben stood with the towel in his hands.

"Two years," he said.

"You'd have said we'd do it together."

"Yes."

"That's why." She turned round. "Keeping it might mean taking all of us back there, Ben. Your job, the kids' school, everything. I wasn't going to have you say yes to that on a bench you weren't even on."

He didn't answer at once. He thought of a folder on Brynner's machines, and a woman with her hair coming loose saying all six of them were all right, and the two years he had never told her about it either. He found he couldn't stay hurt for long. He was hurt anyway.

"If you go," he said, "I'll manage."

She looked at him. He meant it. He would manage the way he did everything, without a word: every morning in front of a screen with one other face on it, Theo on his knee, Nora walking herself to school in the dark, until the day an email came from San Francisco and the six months began. He would never tell her what it cost. He would never say she shouldn't have gone.

She knew whose way that was. She had grown up in that house.

The baby moved, low down, a slow roll under the towel. Anna put her hand flat over the place and kept it there.

Her mother had written that paper so that somebody would stop her. Her father had asked for her word so that somebody would keep her out of those places. They had both chosen Anna. And Anna was standing in a kitchen in Helsinki with her hand on her daughter, and however she said it to herself, it came out the same: her daughter, or her mother.

"I'm not going," she said.

Ben didn't tell her she was right. She would have hated him for it, and he didn't know if she was.

---

When he had gone to bed, she rang the house. It was the middle of the afternoon in Dayton. Her father answered on the fifth ring, as if the phone had surprised him.

"Annie? Everything all right?"

"Dad, I'm not coming," she said. "Not before the baby. I can't. I wanted you to hear me say it."

"Nobody's asking you to, honey. We're—"

"Don't," she said. "Please. Don't say it."

There was a long silence from Dayton. She could hear the ceiling fan, and further off, where the piano should have been, nothing at all.

"She's asleep," said Walt at last. "First time since Thursday. On the couch, in her clothes. I didn't like to move her."

It was the truest thing he had told her in nine Sundays. She sat in the dark kitchen and held on to it.

"Dolores is coming tomorrow," she said. "Let her in, Dad. Even if Mom shouts. Let her in, and don't tell her anybody's fine."

He didn't say anything. She could hear him breathing.

"All right," said Walt. "I'll let her in."

Neither of them said goodbye for a long time, and neither of them said *fine*.
