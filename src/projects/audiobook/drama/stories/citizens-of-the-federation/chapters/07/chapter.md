# Chapter 7: Gate 12A

At twenty to seven on a Monday morning in January 2030, Sergeant Joel Flory of the Ohio National Guard stood ten feet from an airman of the United States Air Force, and both of them were trying to keep their feet warm.

Between them was a row of concrete blocks across the lanes. Behind the airmen, a slab of steel had tipped up out of the roadway when the trucks appeared, so that nothing could drive through. This was Gate 12A of Wright-Patterson, the big Air Force base on the eastern edge of Dayton, and on a weekday morning it was usually full of cars going one way, in to work. A canopy roofed the lanes, lit white underneath, so that the airmen and the Guardsmen stood in a box of light with the dark all round it. Behind Joel, the company's trucks were parked across the road. Behind the trucks, headlights ran back toward Dayton as far as he could see, every car in its lane with its engine running, full of people who were going to be late.

The captain had the reason in a plastic sleeve. It was one sheet of paper, signed by the Governor of Ohio. It said that the base belonged to the President sworn in at Philadelphia, who had won the election, and that the Guard would hold it for him. On the other side of the blocks an Air Force colonel had a reason too, from the general in the headquarters behind him: the base belonged to the President in Washington, who had been President before the election and had never left, and who said that a President stays until a count can be trusted.

There had been two Presidents for a year. In Ohio people called each other Counts and Stays. It had started as a joke.

Joel had voted for the one in Philadelphia, and most of the company had too. In the truck from the armory in Springfield, the captain had told them how this would go. Enough of them on the road, a paper in a sleeve, and a colonel on the other side deciding he had better things to do with his career. "Nobody here is going to shoot anybody," the captain said. "Look like you mean it."

So Joel stood at the blocks and looked like he meant it, with his rifle across his chest and his toes going numb in his boots.

The airman across from him was about twenty. He had the wrong gloves for it, thin ones, and every few minutes he took the right one off and put his bare hand in his armpit, and then remembered where he was and put the glove back on. His name was sewn over his pocket, but the Air Force sewed its names in a brown thread that disappeared into the camouflage, and under the canopy lights Joel couldn't read it.

The airman could read Joel's. The Army's thread was black.

"Flory," the airman said, after the first ten minutes. It was the first thing either line had said to the other. "You from around here?"

"East side," said Joel. "Ten minutes."

"Green Bay." The airman said it like an apology. "Everybody said Ohio'd be warmer."

"Who said that?"

"Everybody in Green Bay."

Joel laughed. He hadn't meant to. A few of the men on either side of him looked over, and the airman looked pleased with himself and then looked at the colonel, in case he had done something wrong.

The colonel and the captain talked across the blocks with their breath going up between them. The colonel went into the gatehouse to make a phone call and came out again. The captain made a phone call of his own. Nobody moved. The sky over the hangars went from black to dark grey. A woman in one of the stopped cars leaned on her horn for a long time, and then let go.

At about twenty past seven the airman turned to look at the colonel, the light fell across his chest, and Joel could make out the brown thread. KASPRZAK. He tried to say it to himself and couldn't. He decided he'd ask.

Then a rifle fired.

Under the canopy, the sound came off the roof and the concrete and the gatehouse windows all at once, and it came from everywhere. Joel thought it was behind him. Then he thought it was to his left. Kasprzak's head turned. His rifle came up. Afterwards Joel could never say whether it was coming up at him or only coming up, because anyone's hands go to what they're holding when a gun goes off near them.

Joel fired twice.

Then everybody was firing. It went on for nine seconds.

When it stopped, the first thing Joel heard was a car alarm, out on the road. The second was the captain, shouting cease fire, though it had ceased. Joel was still aiming over the blocks, at nothing. Kasprzak was on his back on the far side, with his rifle beside him and one glove off.

Joel started over the blocks. Lieutenant Mercer took him by the back of his vest and pulled him down, hard, and held him there, saying his name. On the other side an airman was kneeling over Kasprzak, working at his chest with both hands. Joel watched the hands until somebody stepped between.

Eleven people died at Gate 12A, Guardsmen and airmen. The Guard pulled back down the road before eight. The base stayed exactly where it was. The road was closed all morning, and thousands of people were late for work.

---

Carol Flory heard it on the radio at a quarter to eight, in her kitchen, with her coat on and a piece of toast in her hand.

On Saturday Joel had rung to say that his company had been called up and he couldn't tell her what for. That he couldn't tell her had told her something. She had spent Sunday not ringing him.

The radio said there had been shooting at a gate of Wright-Patterson, between the Guard and the Air Force, and that there were casualties. It didn't say how many, or whose.

She rang him. *This is Joel. You know what to do.* She rang again and listened to the whole of it, for his voice. Then she put the toast down, since there was no point in it now, and went to work, because the visitors' window at the District opened at nine and there would be people at it.

There were fewer than usual. Half the roads on the east side were closed. Carol had kept the window for nearly three years, since a supervisor opened her file and found that the county's records and the state's no longer agreed about her, and moved her somewhere without a computer. She kept the only list of who was inside the District there now, in a green ledger she had ruled herself. That morning she wrote in it as neatly as on any other, with her phone face up on the counter beside the ledger, where she could see it without picking it up.

Lucille Odom, who met the new arrivals at the inner door, brought her a coffee at ten and put it down beside the phone. She didn't ask. She had a television in her building.

At eight minutes to twelve the phone lit up.

*Im ok. Dont watch anything*

He never put in apostrophes, and Carol had been correcting him since he could spell. She read it until the screen went dark, and woke it, and read it again, and for once she didn't mind about the apostrophes at all.

---

The video came out the next morning, and Gary Pruitt, the caseworker, brought it down to her window. He stood at the counter with it a long time before he turned it round.

"Carol," he said. "Isn't this your boy?"

It was nine seconds long. A woman on her way to work had been sitting in the stopped line on the road for forty minutes, with her phone in a holder on the dashboard, and when the first shot came she had reached for it. So it started a second too late. It showed the white box of light under the canopy, a little crooked, and the trucks, and men moving. Then, for most of the nine seconds, it showed one young man side-on behind a rifle, firing, near enough to the road that his name could be read off his chest. Then the phone dropped into the footwell, and the woman said *Jesus, oh Jesus*, and it ended.

Carol watched it once and gave it back.

His ears were red. He had never in his life worn a hat.

"I'm sorry, Carol," said Gary. "It's everywhere."

"Don't read me the comments."

"I wasn't going to," said Gary, who had been going to.

It was everywhere. By Wednesday night forty million people had watched it, and it would have been hard to find two who had watched the same thing.

That week the same nine seconds went round America under two captions. Under one, it was a Guardsman returning fire on soldiers who had opened up on him first, and it was called HERO. Under the other, it was a militiaman firing on airmen at their own gate, and it was called BUTCHER.

Within an hour of the video coming out, someone had paused it on the clearest frame and drawn a red circle round the name on the young man's chest. FLORY, in black thread. Nobody drew a circle round the airman's name. In no frame of it could the brown be read.

That night, in the drill hall at the armory in Springfield, where the company had been kept since Monday afternoon, Private Danny Ruhl sat on his camp bed and watched it. He was nineteen. He had fired too, at the gate, and didn't know at what. He watched the nine seconds, and the phone started them over, and he watched them again.

Joel came and sat on the next bed. After the fourth time he reached over, took the phone out of Danny's hand, and put it face down on the blanket, and kept his own hand on it.

"Eat something," he said.

"I'm not hungry, Sarge."

"Didn't ask if you were hungry."

Danny ate half a packet of crackers, because Joel sat there until he did. Then he lay down with his boots on. Joel stayed on the next bed with his hand on the phone, which buzzed under his palm every few seconds, all night, with strangers.

---

The company's phones had been taken from them on Monday afternoon and handed back on Tuesday, which most of them agreed afterwards had been a mistake. Joel had given his statement twice to a major from Columbus, and both times had said the same thing: he had heard a shot, he didn't know where from, he had seen the airman's rifle come up, and he had fired.

On Wednesday a woman from the Governor's office came to see him in an empty classroom off the drill hall. She was about his age, in a good wool coat, and she was kind to him, and she had a camera operator waiting in the hall.

"A minute," she said. "Ohio needs to hear from you. All you'd have to say is that you returned fire."

"I don't know who fired first, ma'am."

"Sergeant." She leaned toward him. "We're the lawful side. The count's the count. We didn't start this."

"I don't know who fired first."

"Then you returned fire."

"I fired," said Joel.

She wrote something down, and thanked him for his service. The camera operator went home.

That evening the company watched the Governor on a laptop on a folding table in the drill hall. Joel had grown up on that face. The first spring of the pandemic, when he was sixteen, she had been on the kitchen television every day with the day's dead, and his mother had believed her like scripture. Now she stood in front of two flags and read from a paper. Ohio's soldiers, she said, had been fired upon, and had returned fire.

She hadn't slept since Monday; it was in her face. She believed what she was reading. Joel could see that she did.

Later that night the news put up the names of the dead, with their pictures. The airman from Green Bay was Eli Kasprzak, and he was twenty. His mother sat at her kitchen table in Wisconsin with a school photograph of him in a hockey jersey, holding the frame in both hands, and kept her voice steady to the end.

"He hated the cold," she said. "Can you believe that? A boy from Green Bay."

She said his name as *Kasper-zack*. Joel had been saying it wrong in his head for two days. He said it right, once, under his breath, on a camp bed in a drill hall in Springfield, where nobody heard.

By Thursday there was a poster of his face on telephone poles along Third Street in Dayton, printed off somebody's printer, with HERO under it. By Thursday night someone had added BUTCHER underneath in marker, on most of them, and someone else had crossed that out on some.

---

On Thursday, Carol's picture went up beside his.

It came to her first as a message from a number she didn't know. It was her old name, nothing else. She hadn't heard it said in years, and she had never once seen it on her own phone, and it went through her before she could do anything about it.

By the afternoon there were three pictures of her going round beside the one of him. The first was her county ID, taken on a bad day. The second was from a county newsletter of nearly twenty years before, which some office had put online and nobody had ever taken down: a young clerk with short hair, in a tie, with her old name under the photograph. The third was a page of the *Dayton Daily News* from 2013. When Carol changed her name, Ohio law had required her to print both names in the newspaper, side by side, a month before the judge would let her keep the new one, in case anybody objected. Nobody had. The newspaper had kept the page.

Under the pictures were the things people say. Carol didn't read them. She had read the first one, and that had been enough to know the rest.

Other people put her picture up too, the ID one, with a heart beside it and a flag in pale blue and pink, and called her the hero's mother. A woman from a group in Columbus wrote to ask whether she would speak on the statehouse steps on Saturday. Carol wrote back, *No, thank you,* because she had been brought up to answer letters.

For sixteen years, the work of Carol Flory's life had been to be a file nobody had any reason to open. That week forty million people opened it.

That night Miss Bea Yancey rang. She ran the choir at the Methodist church on Salem Avenue, where Carol sang tenor on Wednesdays, and she said the tenors had been flat without her, and that she was to come next week if she had to be carried.

On Friday morning, when she went out to the car, someone had sprayed her old name across her garage door, in red, in letters as tall as she was. On the front step, someone else had left a bunch of supermarket flowers, still in their cellophane, with a card tucked in that said THANK YOU FOR YOUR SON. It was impossible to tell which had come first.

Carol took the flowers inside and put them in water, because they were flowers, and put the card in her coat pocket, and drove to work.

At the window, under the clock, she looked at her phone and found that the county hadn't paid her. Then she opened the ledger and began the day, because there were people in the line.

Gary came down at eleven.

"It's the bank," he said. "Washington's told it not to pay any government that answers to Philadelphia, which is us, and the Governor's told it to pay us. So it isn't doing anything. They'll have it sorted by Monday." He hesitated. "I saw your door. Somebody sent it to me. I want you to know I'm on your side, Carol."

"Which one's that, Gary?"

He thought about it, honestly, which she liked him for.

"Yours," he said.

---

Joel came home on a pass on Saturday night, and slept in his old room, and on Sunday morning Carol made him eggs.

He ate them standing at the counter, as he had since he was fourteen.

"Sit down and eat something," said Carol.

"I am eating something."

"Sitting down is part of it."

He sat down. He looked older than he had at Christmas, and thinner, and not as tired as she had expected, which frightened her more.

After breakfast they went out to the garage door. Carol had bought the paint on Saturday, a grey as near the door's old colour as the hardware store had, and two rollers, and a brush for the edges. There was frost on the drive. The can said not to use it in a frost. They used it anyway.

Joel had been nine when Carol's two names went into the newspaper. Before that, the old one had been the name on the mail, and he had called her by another word, and then for a while by no word at all, until they settled on Carol. He took the brush and went round each of the red letters first, carefully, inside the edges, before he picked up a roller. In the cold the paint went on thick and dragged, and the red showed through the first coat as pink.

Across the street, Dale Hutto came out to the end of his drive in his slippers with a coffee. Dale had been an engineer on the base for thirty years. There was a sign in his yard that said STAY, and since the summer the street's children had been telling it "Good boy" on their way to school.

"I'm sorry about your door, Carol," he called. "That's not right. I won't have that on this street."

"Thank you, Dale."

Dale looked at Joel's back. He opened his mouth, and shut it again, and went inside. Joel went on rolling.

"He works on the base," said Joel.

"Thirty years."

"Was he there? Monday?"

"He was in the line, two cars back from the gate," said Carol, without stopping the brush. "He was late for work."

Joel rolled the same square three times.

"They put up the one from the newsletter," he said.

"They did."

"That's the tie I gave you."

Carol had known he would see it. She hadn't known he would remember. It was green, with yellow ducks on it, and he had chosen it himself at seven, for Father's Day, from a rack at the mall, while she pretended to look at something else.

"You had very strong views about ducks," she said.

"You wore it every Thursday."

"For a year. I wore it for my picture in the newsletter. Clerk of the month." She picked up the other roller. "I liked that tie."

"They put it up to make fun of you."

"I know why they put it up," said Carol. "I still liked the tie."

They did the top half of the door together, Joel reaching the part she couldn't. The pink went under the second coat.

"A woman from the Governor's office wanted me to say they fired first," he said.

"Did you?"

"I don't know who fired first."

"So you didn't say it."

"I said I fired."

Carol didn't tell him it wasn't his fault. She didn't tell him he'd had no choice, or that anybody would have done the same. His captain had told him those things all week, and the major from Columbus, and strangers on his phone who called him a hero.

"Yes," she said. "You did."

He put the roller in the tray.

"He asked me if I was from around here," Joel said. "The kid across from me. Before. When it was still just standing."

"What was his name?"

"Kasprzak. Eli."

"Spell it."

He spelled it. Carol took the card out of her coat pocket, the one that said THANK YOU FOR YOUR SON, and wrote the name on the back of it with the pen she always had, in the square, careful hand she used for the county, and asked him how old.

"Twenty."

She wrote that down as well, and put the card back in her pocket, and picked up the brush, and went and did the corner he had missed.

---

Joel's pass ran out at five. He drove back to Springfield in the last of the light, and Carol stood on the drive and watched the car go to the end of the street and turn.

By the time she went in, the door was grey all over. If you knew what it had said, and the light came at it from low enough, you could still read it. Most of the street knew. In time it would weather, like anything.

On Monday morning, unpaid, Carol opened the visitors' window at nine. The truck from Indiana had come with the District's food as usual; it was paid up to the end of the month. Dolores had rung on Sunday night to ask whether the window would be open on Thursday, for Mrs. Dorsey's arm, and Carol had told her it would. She took the card out of her pocket and put it in the back of the green ledger, where she kept the things that had no column.

Then she looked up at the first person in the line.

"Name," she said, "and who you're here to see."
