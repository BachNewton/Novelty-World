# Chapter 4: Father's Day

Across the street, the school had its flag up. Finland keeps Father's Day in November, in the dark, with the flags out, and five years in, Ben Novak still found that faintly wonderful.

His daughter had brought him coffee before it was light, most of it still in the cup, along with a card he wasn't allowed to open until dinner. Then she stood beside the bed and watched him not open it.

---

In the afternoon he went down to the basement for the crib.

The baby was due in January, and Anna wanted the crib up before Christmas. It was under the building, behind a steel door as thick as his hand, in a room with concrete walls and air vents and enough space for everyone who lived upstairs. Most big buildings in Finland have one. In peacetime people keep their skis there, and their bicycles and summer tyres, and, in the Novaks' case, Theo's old crib in pieces, tied up with string. A laminated notice inside the door says what to do if the peace ends: the residents will have three days to clear their things out.

In their first month in the building, Ben had worked through that notice with a dictionary, a word at a time, and then he had gone upstairs and not mentioned it to Anna.

Nora had come down to hold the door. She was seven now, in her first year of school, and the door was heavy enough that she needed both hands and her shoulder. She held it like a job.

"What is this room for?" she asked.

"Skis," said Ben.

It was true, and it was the only part of the answer he wanted her to have.

---

By dinner Nora had been waiting all day, and she had decided to read the card to him herself.

He could have read the Finnish himself, given a minute and nobody watching. But Nora held it up in both hands, facing him, as her teacher held things up at school, and he let her.

"Dear Dad," she read, putting it into English as she went. "You are the best dad in Finland and in America. You are very good at losing at Uno. You are not good at singing."

Ben could see that the part about singing wasn't on the card. She had added it on the way, because it was true. He told her it was a very good card.

"I know," said Nora. She leaned over to her little brother, Theo, and said something in Finnish, so fast that Ben understood none of it, and Theo laughed until he had to put down his fork. Ben looked across at Anna. She shook her head. She hadn't caught it either.

If you had asked Ben, he would have said this didn't bother him, and he would mostly have meant it. Nora had caught Finnish at daycare, the way children catch everything there, and Theo had been born into it. Ben and Anna were still studying it out of a textbook in the evenings, and Ben had begun to suspect that the two of them would be visitors in it for the rest of their lives.

Then Ben's phone lit up beside his plate. He had promised not to look at it at the table, and he looked anyway. Most people did, that autumn.

On the screen, army trucks were crossing one of Pittsburgh's yellow bridges.

"They've sent the Guard into Pittsburgh," he said. "There's trouble at the District."

"Do we know anybody in Pittsburgh?" Anna asked.

"No."

Ben was relieved by the answer, for as long as he let himself be. He had once watched a District's gate on a screen all night, four minutes behind, and he knew what the trucks were for. Anna wasn't relieved at all. She was already making the drive in her head: west out of Pittsburgh on the interstate, four hours, and then her parents' kitchen in Dayton. She knew exactly how short four hours was.

She reached over, turned his phone face down, and left her hand on it. Ben saw how long the hand stayed there, and said nothing.

"Read Theo the part about singing," she said.

Nora read it again, in Finnish this time, and Theo laughed even harder. Ben, to prove Nora right, sang. For the rest of dinner Anna laughed at everything the children said, always a moment after they did, and Ben heard the moment every time, and sang louder.

---

At six Anna propped the tablet against the fruit bowl, and there was her father, already waiting. It was eleven in the morning in Dayton.

"There's my girl," said Walt. "How's that baby? You look big."

"Thanks, Dad. Happy Father's Day."

Walt told her that Father's Day was in June, and Anna told him that here it was today, and the flags were out for it. He shook his head at Finland, as he did most Sundays. "Dark by four, and Father's Day in November."

Nora leaned into the picture on her way to the bath and waved at him with her whole arm, and he waved back until she had gone. Then he wanted to know about the big box he could see in the hall behind Anna.

It had come on Thursday, a cardboard box the size of a small suitcase, with the government's name on the side. Nora had already been through it, one thing at a time. Anna carried the tablet out to the hall, past the crib leaning in pieces against the wall, and showed him: a snowsuit, sheets, tiny socks, a hat with ears, a picture book, a thermometer for the bath. A mattress lay in the bottom, because the box was also the bed.

"Every baby gets one," she said. "Every baby in the country. All you have to do is let the nurse see you early."

Walt looked at it all. "When you were born," he said, "we got a bill."

"Got a name yet, for that baby?" he asked.

"We've got a list. It has to work in two languages."

"Walter works."

"It's a girl, Dad."

"Doesn't stop it working."

He laughed, and shifted in his chair, and she watched him do it. In the last few years the weakness from his polio had come back, in the good leg as well as the bad one. The doctors said that happened sometimes, decades later, and couldn't tell him why.

"So what's new?" she asked. "How's Mom?"

"Well, we had the election," said Walt. "You see we got the doctor in? For governor. The one from the TV, that first spring."

Anna had seen. Every afternoon at two, that spring, the doctor had come on television to tell Ohio how many were sick and how many had died, and to stay home. The whole house had watched her from the kitchen table, Walt with a pencil, writing the numbers down.

"She kept us alive, that spring, her and you," said Walt. "I figured I owed her the one vote. Your mother didn't go."

"She didn't vote?"

"Didn't feel like it. First one she's missed since Nixon."

"Is she all right?"

"She's fine. She won't eat much. Soup, if she opens the can herself." Walt moved on. "We had rain Tuesday, so I didn't get to the gutters, but they'll keep. The Henderson boy took the garbage cans down for me. Your mother's been at her piano."

Over his shoulder, in the front hall, something stood by the door. Anna leaned toward the screen.

"Dad, what's that behind you? Is that a walker?"

Walt glanced back at it. "That's for the garbage cans. For wheeling them down to the street."

"The Henderson boy takes the garbage cans down. You just said."

"Well, there you go," said Walt. "It doesn't get much use."

Anna knew that voice. It was the one he used for "fine."

Somewhere behind him, the piano started. Ruth always played them something on the Sunday call; it was part of the arrangement, like the hour. For a long while now it had been the same slow piece every week, and until this autumn she had always played it to the end. Lately she played a few bars of it, and stopped, and played the same few bars again, and stopped.

"She's practising," said Walt.

Then the piano stopped for good, and there were footsteps, and Anna's mother was in the doorway.

She didn't come in. She stood with one hand on the frame and looked at the tablet from across the room.

"Walter. Who is that?"

"It's Annie, Ruthie," said Walt. "It's Sunday. Come and say hello."

"Hi, Mom," said Anna. "I like that sweater."

Her mother didn't move. Her eyes went from the screen to Walt and back. When she spoke again, her voice was low and fast, and Anna had heard it before, a long time ago.

"I know who you are. You're from the county. You've been listening through that thing." She pointed at the tablet without coming any nearer. "I take what I'm supposed to take. I don't need anybody's shot. You can write that down."

"Ruthie," said Walt.

"My daughter is fed. She's clean. She goes to school every day with a lunch I made her." Ruth's hand was tight on the door frame. "You are not taking her anywhere."

Walt began to tell her again who it was, and Anna stopped him.

When Anna was eleven, her mother had spent an autumn in the hospital. After she came home, a woman from the county came to the house every Thursday for a year, with a clipboard, to decide whether the child should stay. Anna had watched those visits from the stairs, and she had waited all year for the woman to tell her mother she was doing a good job. Nobody ever had.

"Mrs. Dorsey," Anna said, "I'm writing it down right now. She's fed, and she's clean, and she goes to school. You're doing a good job. She's staying right where she is."

Her mother looked at her for a long time.

"You'll put that in your report."

"I'll put it in my report."

Some of the fear went out of Ruth's shoulders. Not all of it. "Well," she said. "Thank you. That's kind." And she went back down the hall, and after a moment the same few bars began again.

Walt rubbed his face with his whole hand. He looked like a man who hadn't slept properly in weeks, and Anna understood that he hadn't.

"Don't mind that," he said. "That's a bad morning, is all. She's been a little off since the clinic closed."

"Since what closed?"

He didn't answer, so she asked again. "Dad. Dolores's clinic? What do you mean, closed?"

"First week of September. There was a sign on the door. Dolores called me herself. Nineteen years she's been giving your mother that shot, and she cried on the phone." He shook his head. "It's the money. Dolores says most of her people are on the Medicaid, the insurance for folks with nothing, and Washington's cutting it. They closed before the cuts could close them."

The clinic's patients wrote no letters to Congress about it. A good many of them did not vote, for the same reasons the clinic had been looking after them.

"So who's giving her the shot?" Anna asked.

"There's a fella at the county. He's calling a place in Columbus for us, every week, to get her on their list." Walt looked at something off the screen. "He says there's places downtown, too. For folks who can't manage. I told him we're managing."

Anna knew what was downtown. Dayton had voted for a District of its own two Septembers ago, and built it by the river where the old warehouses had been, with a fence from the water to the railroad tracks. It had filled faster than they could build it. She had seen it on the news, and so had he.

"Columbus is an hour from you, Dad. You don't drive on the highway anymore."

"I've got a call in," said Walt. "Annie. I've got a call in. You look after that baby. You don't need this. It's Father's Day, isn't it? Everything's fine here."

His hand slipped on the tablet. It tipped back, and for a few seconds the two of them looked together at the ceiling fan in Dayton. Then the call was gone.

---

Anna stood in the hall with the tablet in her hands and the box at her feet.

Her mother had schizoaffective disorder. When she was ill, she believed things that weren't so and heard things nobody had said, and her moods ran too high or too low to live inside. She never forgot who anyone was. She knew them perfectly well, and believed they were someone else, and when she was frightened, her fear went back to the worst year it knew, to a woman with a clipboard, and whoever was nearest became her. When she was well, she was the sharpest woman in Dayton and the hardest to please: she went through the church bulletin every Sunday with a red pen, and the pastor thanked her for it. She had first been ill in her twenties. Since Anna was a young woman, Dolores had given her the injection once a month, and she had been well.

The medicine left the body slowly, over weeks. A person could miss one injection and seem fine. She could miss a second and seem a little off. The clinic had closed in September, and it was November.

In the bathroom, Ben was running the water and asking Theo, very patiently, to please sit down.

---

At bedtime Nora was still thinking about it.

"Mom? I heard Grandma. Why did she think you were from the county?"

Anna tucked the blanket in along her side and said, in Finnish, "*Ei se mitään*." It means never mind, it's nothing, it's fine, all at once. It is one of the first things anyone learns here, and she got every sound of it right.

"That was good, Mom," said Nora.

Anna heard it as soon as it was out. It was her father's word, in another language.

---

Above the kitchen sink, as above nearly every kitchen sink in Finland, there was a cupboard with no floor. You put the wet plates in the rack inside, and they dripped into the sink behind a closed door.

Anna had lived with one for five years, and she still dried every plate with a towel before she put it away, as her mother always had.

Ben came in from the children's room. "They're both down," he said. "You know you don't have to dry those."

"September," said Anna.

"What?"

"The clinic closed the first week of September. That's nine Sundays. Nine Sundays he sat there and told me about the gutters."

Ben leaned against the counter. "How was she?"

"She thought I was from the county. The Thursday woman. She told me her daughter was fed and clean, and would I please not take me away." Anna picked up another plate. "So I said I wouldn't. I told her I'd put it in my report."

"Oh, Anna."

"Don't," she said. "I've got four more plates."

"Okay." He picked up a towel. "So we find someone. A nurse who comes to the house, an agency, somebody. We can pay for that. That's a thing we can actually do."

"Who? Tell me who. I've been standing here trying to think of one name. Everybody I knew there has moved away. And she won't let a stranger near that arm."

"I know."

"You know what happens here when a clinic moves? A letter comes, in two languages I can't read, and I still know which tram to take. In Dayton, a sign goes up on a door." She put the plate away and took out another. "I could go. For a month, just to set something up. They let you fly until thirty-six weeks."

"You're at thirty-one," said Ben. "Give it a month and they won't let you on the plane home. So you have her in Dayton, with your dad and his walker, and I'm here with the kids, watching it on a tablet." He put the towel down. "I've done that, Anna."

"Flights don't stop, Ben."

He could have reminded her of a spring when they had. "No," he said. "I know they don't."

He picked the towel up again, dried a glass and put it up in the cupboard with no floor, where it would have dried by itself. "I don't know what to tell you. I never had this. My folks were gone before we left. I've got a headstone in Toledo. It doesn't call on Sundays."

Anna went on drying the same plate. "There's a word for her now," she said. "Did you know that? I heard a man say it on the American news, a man in a tie. Dim. Like a light bulb. He said, well, the dims have to go somewhere. And I thought, huh. And I went and cut up Theo's apple."

Ben had heard the word once before, from the man it was about.

"That's why we left," he said. "That's exactly why we left."

"Don't tell me why we left. I was the one at the kitchen table."

"And you were right."

"Don't."

"No, you were. Our daughter told her brother a joke tonight in a language I can't follow, and tomorrow she'll walk herself to school in the dark, and nobody here will think anything of it. You did that. I'm not going to say it wasn't right."

"I know it was right," said Anna. "That isn't it. It's that I'd do it again. Put me back at that table tomorrow, knowing all this, and I'd fill it out again."

"We asked them to come," said Ben.

"We asked them once. He said no, and I said okay, Dad, and I went inside." She stopped drying. "And I was so relieved, Ben. I never told you that. I was so relieved."

"Say we asked again," he said, gently. "I'm not saying no. I'm asking what she'd have here. It's dark at four. She doesn't know a soul. On a bad Sunday she thinks you're the county. At least there she knows the house."

"She has a piano there."

"We could get a piano."

"It won't go in the lift."

"I'd carry it," said Ben.

He hung his towel on the oven door. "Come to bed. We've got the maternity clinic on Tuesday. We're having a baby in January, Anna."

"In a minute. I just want to finish these."

---

She finished them, and hung up the towel.

On the refrigerator door was Nora's Europa. It was finished. Nora had decided so in the summer, when the orange crayon got too short to hold, and by then there were more cracks on it than anyone had counted. The ice was still the paper.

Anna stood at the counter with one hand at the small of her back and opened the laptop. It was ten at night. In Dayton it was three in the afternoon, and still light.

The page she wanted was for the relatives of Finnish citizens, and Anna wasn't one. She had lived here long enough to apply to become one. What she didn't have was a certificate to say that her Finnish was good enough, spoken and written, and she had been studying for that exam for three years. Nora could have passed the speaking half of it at four.

She kept reading. The page said that an elderly parent could be thought of as dependent on a grown child who lived in Finland. It said that money alone was not enough. It said that a permit was not usually granted to someone who could get treatment in her own country. In Ohio there was a list in Columbus, and a man at the county calling it every week, and Anna could hear exactly how that would read to someone in an office in Helsinki.

And it said that permits like this were meant, above all, for a parent who moved to Finland at the same time as her grown child.

Anna read that sentence twice. She thought about the porch.

Further down, the page explained how to apply. The form could be filled in online. But the applicant would have to come in person, to prove who she was.
