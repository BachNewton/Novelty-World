# Chapter 4: First Winter

In December the sun came up over Helsinki at about a quarter past nine and went down a little after three, and in between it never climbed much higher than the roofs. On the shortest day there were less than six hours of it. Anna Novak had read all this before they came. What she hadn't understood was that the dark was not something that came in the evening. The dark was where you lived. The day was something that visited.

Ben took Nora to daycare at a quarter to eight, under the streetlights, in a snowsuit so thick she walked like a small astronaut. The Finnish word for daycare is *päiväkoti*, which means day-home, and Nora's was the low yellow building Mikko had found for them, round the corner from the apartment, with a sandpit and a fence and the sea past the fence. In the morning dark the sea was only a place where the lights stopped.

The children went outside there twice a day, whatever the weather was doing, and nobody thought this needed explaining. The littlest ones slept outside, too, after lunch, in a row of prams under the windows, with their faces in the cold and a teacher looking out at them every few minutes. The first time Ben saw the row of prams, on a grey day with sleet in it when he had come early to fetch Nora, he stood at the fence and counted them, as if one might be missing.

Most mornings the same father arrived at the gate when Ben did, on a bicycle with studded tyres and a little girl in a seat on the back. He locked the bicycle, walked the girl in, came out again, said *moi*, which means hi, and rode away. Ben said *moi* back.

Then, on a morning in December, the man stopped beside him at the gate.

"Aino says that Nora says you are afraid of the dark," he said, in careful English.

Ben laughed. He had said it to Nora on the walk the week before, as a joke, and she had reported it to her friends, in Finnish, as news.

"Only a little," he said.

The man considered this.

"November is worse," he said. "December has the lights."

He meant the candles and the paper stars in the windows all up and down the street, lit from four in the afternoon. Ben had noticed them, and hadn't known they were for this.

"You are from America," the man said. "Where?"

"Ohio. Toledo."

The man nodded, as if that settled something. "Kotka," he said, with the same nod. Ben had no idea where that was, and the man had no interest in whether he did. His name was Antti Laine. He said so, shook Ben's hand, and cycled off into the dark with his front light swinging.

Ben found that he liked him. He had been raised by a grandmother who believed most talk was a waste of breath, and there was something about a man who said a thing once and then left you alone with it that felt to Ben, unexpectedly, like home.

---

On Sundays at six in the evening, Anna propped the tablet against the fruit bowl, and there were her parents, at eleven in the morning, back from church.

"Dark yet?" said Walt.

"Since three."

"What do you do all afternoon?"

"Turn the lights on, Dad."

Walt shook his head at Finland, as he did most Sundays. He had found it on a map in the paper, nearly as far north as Anchorage, Alaska, and had decided it would be a good place for tomatoes if only they'd turn the sun up. He asked whether Ben was minding the ice, and said his own leg was fine. Nora showed him a paper star. Theo, nine months old, tried to grab the tablet, and Walt held his hand up to the screen so that Theo would have something to grab at.

At the end, Ruth played. She had played the same slow hymn at the end of every call since they left. Anna didn't know it, and had never asked what it was; it was enough that her mother played it all the way through. Then Anna said goodbye and folded the tablet's cover over her parents, and the kitchen was very quiet, and it was a quarter past six and had been night for three hours.

---

On the Monday, the school next door to the daycare held its celebration for Independence Day. Finland had been independent of Russia for a hundred and five years that week, and the school invited the daycare's children, and any parents who liked, to sit at the front of the gym.

Anna went, with Theo on her hip, because it was something to do on a Monday.

The gym smelled of floor polish and wet wool, and paper flags were taped along the wall bars, blue crosses on white. The daycare's children sat cross-legged on the floor in front, Nora in a blue paper crown, waving a paper flag at her mother with great energy. Behind them sat the schoolchildren in rows, for once very still. A teacher spoke for a while. Anna understood one word, *Suomi*, which is what the Finns call Finland, and stood at the back with the other parents and smiled when they smiled.

Then the older children stood up on a row of benches to sing. Someone had left a printed programme on the chair beside her, and Anna could read that much of it: *Finlandia-hymni*.

It was slow and grave, and it climbed. The children sang it the way she had sometimes heard people sing at funerals, as if it belonged to them. Down at the front the teachers sang too, and a woman in a blue dress sang with her eyes shut, and nobody in the gym moved at all. Anna felt it go through the room, whatever it was, and stop at her, the way a conversation in another language stops at the edge of the table. She couldn't follow it in. She held Theo, who had fallen asleep against her neck, and waited for it to be over, and was sorry when it was.

Then the whole gym rose for the national anthem, all at once, as if somebody had pulled a string. Anna was the last on her feet, by a second, with a sleeping baby and nobody looking at her, and she stood through every verse feeling that second.

The next evening, on Independence Day itself, the windows across the street filled with candles at six o'clock, two to a window, as if the whole street were keeping watch. Ben had bought a blue one and a white one, and they put them in their own front window, and felt, for an evening, like people who lived there.

---

In December Ben carried the flattened moving boxes down to the basement. Under the building, behind a steel door as thick as his hand, there was a room with concrete walls, air vents and a row of storage cages, where people kept their skis and summer tyres. A laminated notice was screwed to the inside of the door. Ben held his phone up to it and let the phone turn it into English, a few lines at a time.

The room was a shelter, built for everybody who lived upstairs, because the law said a building this size had to have one. If the authorities ever ordered it, the notice said, the residents would have seventy-two hours to clear their things out of the cages, so that the room could be what it had been built for.

Ben read it twice. Then he put the boxes in their cage and went upstairs, and didn't mention it to Anna. Everybody in this building had lived their whole lives within a few hours' drive of Russia and kept their skis in this room. He decided that if they could, he could.

---

In January Kela, the government office that pays out nearly everything in Finland, sent a letter that Anna couldn't read. She held her phone over it and the phone read it for her: Finland would pay her a few hundred euros a month to stay at home with Theo until he was three. She had never in her life met a form she couldn't get through. Now every form in her life was one.

The same week, she went to the employment office about the free Finnish course for newcomers. The counsellor who saw her was a woman about her own age, from Estonia, who had needed two years to learn Finnish herself. "For you," she said, kindly, "longer."

The course was five days a week, from the morning to the middle of the afternoon, for about a year. It was only for people in their first three years in Finland. Theo would go to daycare all day; the city would find him a place. "With Finnish, in two years you could work," said the counsellor. "Not a dentist's desk at first. But a desk."

Anna had not let herself think about a desk since August. She thought about one now: a counter, a phone, a waiting room full of people and their appointments, and her in charge of all of it.

"Some mothers wait," said the counsellor. "Then they find a class in the evenings. With small children, evenings are slow. Years." She said the rest as plainly, the way you mention that a coat has no hood. "Your permit goes with your husband's job. And to be a Finn one day, you need five years here and a certificate in the language. Without the certificate, the years don't count for anything."

On the tram home Anna looked out at the dark. She remembered the other page, the one she had read at one in the morning in her parents' kitchen, which said that only a Finnish citizen could ask for a parent to come, and only for reasons Finland thought were serious enough. She put it away again. It was a page about terrible things, and nothing terrible had happened.

---

She had until the course started in March to decide.

She knew what the course would get her: a way into the country her children were already living in. Her own money, someday, and her own permit, not one that hung on a company in San Francisco that had changed its mind twice in two years about where its people should work. Friends she could talk to in something more than nods. A desk.

And she knew what it would cost, because every afternoon she pushed the pram past the row of prams under the daycare windows. Theo was ten months old. He was the baby her parents had held for six months and then waved off down the street. If he went to daycare all day, someone else would hear his first word and watch him walk. Someone else would know which cry meant hungry. Everything in her that had grown up in Ohio said that a mother who left a baby in a room full of babies all day so that she could go to school had her priorities wrong. She knew it was only Ohio talking. It talked loudly.

Most afternoons she took Theo to the playground park down the road, a fenced yard with swings and a little wooden hut, where the city kept a woman on staff to keep the hut warm for parents at home with small children. The mothers who came were mostly Finnish, on the same allowance as Anna. Some would stay home until their children were three, and some were going back to work when theirs turned one, and nobody seemed to think anybody else was doing it wrong. They were friendly to her in English and went back to Finnish with each other, and Anna sat in the warm with Theo on her knee and listened to a language go past her like weather.

The woman who kept the hut was in her sixties, with a grey braid. She spoke to Anna in Finnish every day, slowly, as if Anna were going to understand it any day now, and would not switch to English, though Anna was fairly sure she could. She knew Theo's name. She said it the Finnish way, *Teo*, because Finnish has no *th*. She said it every time he came in, and he had started turning his head when she did.

On Thursday evenings the building's sauna, down in the basement next to the laundry, was theirs for an hour, booked on a sheet by the door. Ben ladled water onto the stones the way Mikko had shown him, and the heat came down like a hand, and Nora climbed the benches in raptures, and Anna sat on the bottom one with Theo asleep on her chest, warm to the bone for the one hour in the week that she was.

At night, in bed, she asked Ben.

"Finns do it," said Ben. "Nora loves it there."

"Nora's three."

"He'll be one." He was quiet for a while. "I'd have loved a place like Nora's, when I was little. Somebody who was glad to see me at eight in the morning."

Anna turned her head. Ben's grandmother had raised him from the age of three and had done exactly her duty by him, and this was the most Anna had ever heard him say about what that had been like.

"That's not him," she said. "He's got me."

"I know he has. I'm only saying a daycare isn't the worst thing in the world."

"When I was eleven," said Anna, to the ceiling, "a woman came every Thursday to see if anybody was looking after me." She didn't say the rest, and Ben didn't need her to. She had decided at eleven what kind of mother she would be, on the stairs, watching a woman with a clipboard look in the fridge.

"Whatever you choose," said Ben, "I'm not going anywhere."

It was a promise about Ben. She knew he meant every word of it. It wasn't a promise about Brynner.

On the Sunday call she told her parents about the course. Walt's face changed.

"All day? A baby?"

"Half of Finland does it, Dad."

"They're only little once, Annie," said her father. "That's all I'm saying."

Ruth leaned into the picture. "Take the course," she said. "You'll go out of your mind in that apartment by March. I would. Fifteen ways to end a noun! I'd have it marked up in red in a month." She sat back. "And he'll be fine. You'd be surprised what a child gets through."

Anna knew which autumn her mother was thinking of, and that she was asking, in the only way she ever would, to be told that it had been all right. Anna couldn't tell her that. She said nothing, and Ruth went to the piano and played the slow hymn.

---

Theo had his first birthday on a Saturday in February, and the next day Ruth played him "Happy Birthday" down the line with all the extra notes, and he clapped at the wrong times.

Anna decided on the Tuesday after, and it wasn't the birthday that did it. It was the balcony.

She had begun, without telling anyone, to put Theo out on the balcony for his nap, as the Finns did, bundled in the pram with only his face out to the cold. He slept longer out there; everybody said so, and it was true. That afternoon she stood on the warm side of the glass door and watched him sleep. Snow was coming down past the railing, a little at a time, and settling on the pram's hood. Theo's cheeks were red and his mouth was open, and every so often he frowned at something in a dream.

She thought of a room across the city, full of people saying sentences after a teacher, while someone in a yellow building by the sea looked out of a window every few minutes at a row of prams, and one of them was this one.

And she made herself think about the page.

If she took the course in March, she could have the certificate in time to be a Finn the August after Theo turned five. If she let it go, there would be the blue textbook she had bought, with its cheerful drawings of Finns buying bread, and an evening class when the children were older, and the counsellor's word for how long that took. Years. Every year she spent at this window was a year added to the time before she could ask Finland for her mother. It would only matter if something terrible happened. She knew how her father said *fine*. She had found her mother at the piano at five in the morning in the summer of 2020. Terrible things happened.

She stood at the glass and held both of those things, and looked at her son.

She wanted to be the one at the window. It wasn't Ohio talking, or not only. It was the oldest thing she knew about herself, and she was tired, in this country where she understood nothing, of arguing with the only thing she was sure of.

That evening she wrote to the counsellor to say no. Then she opened the blue book at the first chapter, and read it with her lips moving, and got halfway down the second page before Theo woke.

In March, at the playground park, Theo let go of the bench in the hut and walked four steps, wobbling, into the arms of the woman with the grey braid, who had been holding them out to him for a week. She said something to him in Finnish, delighted, and he laughed. Anna was there. She saw all four steps.

---

In the middle of March, America moved its clocks forward an hour for the summer, and Europe didn't do it for another two weeks. So for two Sundays the call came at five instead of six, because Walt's eleven o'clock didn't move for anybody. It was the first time Anna had thought of her parents' time as something that could drift away from hers.

That month wooden boards went up along the streets, plastered with the faces of people running for parliament, each face with a number beside it, because in Finland you vote for a person by writing their number. Neither Anna nor Ben could vote; that was for citizens. Anna had learned the Finnish numbers first, out of the blue book, and she could read every number on every board, and not one word.

---

The election fell on the first Sunday in April, which was also Palm Sunday, and in Finland on Palm Sunday small children dress up as witches.

Nora had been told all about it at daycare and had told the rest of them, at length. In the morning Ben and Antti painted freckles on Nora and Aino with Anna's eyebrow pencil and tied scarves under their chins, and the two witches set off up the stairwell with willow twigs the daycare had decorated with coloured feathers, to knock on every door in the building, say a rhyme, give the twig away for a blessing and get a chocolate egg in return.

Anna stayed home with Theo, and a few minutes later the doorbell rang. Two small witches from upstairs stood on the mat, a boy and his even smaller sister, and the boy recited at her very fast, holding up his twig: *Virvon, varvon, tuoreeks, terveeks*, and a good deal more. Anna understood none of it. She knew, from Nora, what came next, and she didn't have any chocolate eggs. She gave them each a clementine, which they accepted with dignity, and they gave her the twig and went off down the stairs whispering.

She put the twig in a glass on the kitchen table. She found that she was absurdly pleased.

Up on the fourth floor, Ben and Antti stood on the landing while the witches worked a door.

"My grandmother did this in Viipuri," said Antti, watching them. "Before the war. She said there they did it properly." He was quiet for a flight of stairs. "Viipuri is Russia now. Everybody had to go. She was sixteen. She took her mother and a sewing machine." Another flight. "She lived sixty years in Kotka. If you asked, she was from Viipuri."

Ben thought about it all the way down to the ground floor. He thought about Anna, who would be from Dayton, he suspected, in exactly that way for the rest of her life, and about himself, who was from Toledo only because a person had to be from somewhere.

At six they called Dayton. Walt wanted to know who was winning. Nora, still freckled, held up her chocolate eggs to the screen one at a time.

The polls closed at eight. By midnight the result was clear: in second place, a few seats behind the winner, was a party that had campaigned to let fewer foreigners into Finland.

---

On the Tuesday, Finland joined NATO. That afternoon, on the news, soldiers in Brussels raised its flag beside the flags of the thirty countries already in the alliance.

That morning at the gate, Antti had told Ben before Ben had looked at his phone.

"Today we are in," he said. "All my life I was against it. In my head. Nobody asked me." He watched the children go in through the yellow door. "Then Ukraine."

Ben asked about the election, and whether the party in second place meant people like him.

"Not like you," said Antti.

He didn't look anywhere in particular. At the far end of the fence a tall man in a dark coat was crouched in the slush, doing up a little girl's boots, and Ben had nodded to him every morning since August.

The man straightened and came past them to the gate, and for the first time he stopped.

"You are Nora's father," he said. "Nora is teaching Hamdi English. Hamdi is teaching Nora Somali." He considered Ben. "I think they are mostly teaching each other the rude words."

Ben laughed, and the man smiled, and said his name was Abdullahi, and went off down the street to the bus stop.

"My brother voted for them," said Antti, when he had gone. "In Kotka. He drives a truck. He is not a bad man." He got on his bicycle and sat on it a moment without riding away. "I don't know what he is."

That evening Ben went down to the shelter to get the bicycles out of their cage for the spring. He stood for a while in front of the laminated notice on the door. He could read some of it now, without the phone. It said exactly what it had said in December, and in December it had been about skis.

---

By April the light had come back so far that it was still light at eight in the evening.

On the Saturday before Easter, because Antti had told Ben to, the Novaks took the ferry from the market square out to Suomenlinna, the old sea fortress on its islands at the mouth of the harbour. It was fifteen minutes each way, and it ran all year, through the ice, on an ordinary tram ticket.

Theo had never been on a boat.

He stood on the bench by the window with Ben's hands round his middle and the deck trembling under his feet. When the engines went deep and the ferry pulled away from the quay, he went completely still. Then the water opened out, grey and wide and wrinkled by the wind, with gulls hanging over it, and a great white ship bound somewhere across the sea slid out past them with all its windows shining, and Theo began to laugh. He laughed at the wake. He laughed at the gulls. He put both hands flat on the window and laughed at the sea.

"*Laiva*," Nora told him, pointing at the big ship. She said it in Finnish, because Finnish was what she spoke to her brother now, without deciding to. "Teo. Katso. *Laiva*."

On the island he stood on the quay and looked back at the ferry, and when it pulled away without him, he wept with his whole body, as if it had been his and been taken. They walked a little way along the fortress walls with him crying on Ben's shoulder, past old cannons pointing out to sea at nothing, and caught the next ferry back, because there was no point in anything else. Theo stood at the window again, and laughed again, and somewhere in the middle of the harbour he fell asleep standing up, against his father's chest, with one hand still on the glass.

---

On Easter Sunday they called Dayton at six. Ruth had played two services that morning, and was tired, and pleased with herself. Walt wanted to know how the boat had been.

Theo, in his high chair, pointed at the screen, at nothing, at everything, and said, very clearly, "*Laiva*."

It was his first word that anybody could be sure of.

"What's he saying?" said Walt.

"It's Finnish," said Anna. "It means ship."

"Well, how about that," said Walt, and he said it slowly, the way he had said *greenhouses*.

"Spell it," said Ruth.

Anna spelled it for her. She could do that one. Her mother wrote it on the back of the church bulletin and read it back to her, letter by letter, to be sure she had it right.

"That's a good word," said Ruth, and went to the piano.

On the table beside the tablet, the witches' twig stood in its glass with its feathers. Theo was saying his word to the fruit bowl. Ben had Nora on his knee, and Nora was teaching him something in Finnish, patiently, the way her teachers taught her things. The blue book lay on the counter where Anna had left it on Thursday, open at chapter three.

Theo had learned his first word from his sister, in a language his mother couldn't speak, and his mother had been in the room to hear it. That was what she had bought, at the window, and what she had paid for it was on the other side of the screen, more than four thousand miles away, sitting down at the piano.

In Dayton, her mother played the slow hymn to the end. Every note was in its place.
