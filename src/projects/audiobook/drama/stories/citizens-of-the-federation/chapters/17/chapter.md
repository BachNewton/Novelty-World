# Chapter 17: The Shelter

Eight summers after Alma was counted, the skis came up out of the shelter.

The order came in July, on a Thursday. Every shelter in the country was to be ready for use within seventy-two hours, as the law had always said it might have to be, and so every building in Finland spent a long weekend carrying its winter up its stairs. Ben Novak carried the family's skis up sixty-four steps, two pairs at a time, and the summer tyres, and a box of Nora's school drawings that nobody could bring themselves to throw away. He was sixty-six. He stopped on the landings, and pretended he was reading the noticeboard.

Underneath the building the storage cages were unbolted and stacked against a wall. In their place went bunks, three high. Two plywood cubicles went up in a corner, each with a dry toilet in it and a roll of bags. Beside the steel door, a tent of grey fabric was fastened to the wall, so that anybody who came in once the air outside was bad would come in through it, and not straight into the room. On the far wall was the machine that would breathe for all of them: a fan, a filter the size of an oil drum, and a crank at the height of a man's hip, for when the power went.

It was the room Ben had shown his daughter Nora when she was seven, with her shoulder against the door, holding it open like a job. *Everybody who lives upstairs*, he had told her.

---

The war had begun over Augments, in the years Alma was growing up, and had long since stopped being about them. America was one country again, as countries often are when somebody else is the enemy. Finland was in it the way a house is in a storm. Nobody in Helsinki had been shot at. The sirens were tested at noon on the first Monday of every month, and people in the street stopped and waited for them to finish, and then went on.

In March, somebody in another country had put Finland's register of Augments on the internet, every name and every address, and for a week half of Helsinki looked up its own street. Most of the building had known about Alma for years by then. She had stopped coming fourth in anything the spring she registered. But knowing a thing and seeing it on a screen beside your own address are different, and after March, people on the stairs stood aside for her a little further than they needed to.

Alma was twenty-six. She taught the piano, the way her grandmother had, to children who came up the stairs after school and sat at the treble end of Ruth's old bench. She still lived at home, because of the piano and because of the war. She still ran at five every morning, as fast as she liked.

---

Every shelter is built to a number.

It is worked out, when the building is designed, from the size of the building above it: so many people for so much floor, which is to say the number of people the building was meant to hold. The bunks are counted to it, and the toilets, and the water. The machine on the wall is sized to it. It pulls in air from outside, cleans it through the filter of anything that should not be breathed, and pushes it in at the rate that number of people needs. When the power fails, somebody turns the crank, at a steady pace, in shifts, and the air comes in at the same rate as before. It does not come in any faster because there are more people to breathe it.

The number was written on the first page of a binder that hung on a hook by the door. The binder belonged to Seppo Rantanen from the third floor, a retired heating engineer whom the housing company had appointed to be in charge of the shelter, and whom the rescue department had sent on a course. In July Seppo had gone round every flat with the binder and written down who would be sleeping there if it came to it. In wartime a building holds more people than it was designed for. Sons had come home from flats in the north of the city. Somebody's mother had come from Kotka. When Seppo added it up, the building came to two under its number. By August, with a cousin come from Lahti and a baby born on the fifth floor, it was at it.

He said so at the housing company meeting, in the laundry room, on a light evening in July. Somebody asked what happened if more people came. Seppo said what the rescue department had told him: a building's shelter was for the people who lived in it, and the city had shelters in the rock for everybody else. The meeting voted that the door would close at the number. It was a sensible vote, made in daylight, and nobody in the laundry room could picture the door.

---

On Sunday the seventeenth of August, Nora came for lunch.

She was thirty-four, a physicist at the university, and she lived across the city and came every Sunday whatever the war was doing. Theo's chair was empty. He had been called back to the navy in June and was somewhere out on the gulf on a ship that laid mines, and he wrote when he could, which was seldom.

At ten to twelve the sirens began.

It wasn't the first Monday. It wasn't a test. The sound rose and fell, rose and fell, for a whole minute, over the whole city at once, and in the kitchen nobody moved until it stopped. Then Ben turned on the radio.

A weapon had come down on the Estonian coast, on a naval harbour across the gulf, about a hundred kilometres to the south. The wind was from the southwest, as it mostly is. Fallout, the dust thrown up into the cloud, would come down over Helsinki from late in the afternoon. Everybody was to go to their shelters. Anybody forty or under was to take an iodine tablet.

Nora went to the window. The flat faced the sea. Far out, low on the horizon where Estonia would be, there was a cloud like a summer thunderhead, which didn't move the way one should.

"What is it?" said Alma, who could see the brightness of the sea and nothing on it.

Nora told her. She told it exactly, without improving any of it.

On the radio, within the hour, two governments said it had not been them.

Anna got the box of iodine tablets out of the cupboard over the sink. They came in foil, as everything in Finland does, and she pressed one out for Nora and one for Alma and heard each one break through, and didn't press any for herself or Ben. Her mother had pressed her own tablets out of foil every morning for the last ten years of her life, to hear them break.

They went down at one, with water and blankets and a bag each, and the radio. Alma went first, with her hand on the rail, and counted the stairs under her breath on the way down. She had not needed to count them since she was eight.

---

By three o'clock the shelter was full to its number.

Seppo stood by the door with the binder open and ticked off each family as it came in. He was sixty-nine, with reading glasses on a cord, and he took it as seriously as anything he had ever done. The door stood open still, for the air, and because the radio had said four. The tent by the door was rolled up and tied. Somebody's baby was crying on a bottom bunk, and its father, a young man from the fifth floor called Jari, walked it up and down between the bunks and said *shh* to it in the voice of a man who was frightened and didn't want the baby to know.

Nora was at the door. Nobody had asked her to be. She stood with her shoulder against it, the way she had at seven.

At twenty to four there were footsteps in the corridor outside, a man's and two small ones, and Antti Laine came round the corner with a child by each hand.

Antti had known the code to the front door for thirty years. He was Ben's age, still tall, gone grey rather than white, and he was out of breath. The children were his daughter Aino's: a girl of five called Linnea, in a raincoat, and a boy of two called Otto, who had been carried most of the way and had only just been put down. Aino was a nurse. The hospitals had called everyone in when the sirens went, and she had brought the children to her father across the street and gone. Their father was with the army in the east.

Antti's building, across the street, was older than any of them, stone, with a cellar and no shelter. He had walked the children to the shelter in the rock that served their part of the city, twenty minutes there. Its doors had closed at three. It was full.

He stopped in front of Seppo. He didn't look at Ben, or at Anna, or at anybody he knew.

"Not for me," he said, in Finnish. "For these two."

He asked it once. Then he waited, the way he always had, and left them alone with it.

---

Seppo didn't pretend he hadn't heard. He took his glasses off.

"I can't," he said. He said it to Antti, not to the room. "We're at the number. The machine gives the air it gives. At the number, by the third day everybody in here has a headache and the smallest ones are breathing hard. That's in the book. That's at the number." He put his hand flat on the binder. "And there are four hundred people in the old houses round this one, Antti, with nowhere. If I open the door for two, there'll be a third in ten minutes, and I won't know how to tell him no."

"I know," said Antti.

Linnea was looking at the bunks. Otto had sat down on the concrete and was examining his own shoe.

Seppo looked at them for a long time. Then he put his glasses back on.

"Two small ones," he said, "on one place. I'll answer for that. Not more than that."

So the two children would need one place between them, and every place in the room was taken. For them to come in, somebody already inside would have to go out. Nobody said it. Everybody worked it out at once.

It was Jari who said it, with his baby on his shoulder. He didn't say it unkindly. He said it the way frightened people say the thing everybody is thinking, so as to have it over with.

"She can go up." He didn't look at Alma. "It's on the register what she is. They're built for this, they say. She'd be all right."

The woman from the second floor said, "Jari," sharply. Nobody else said anything. Anna saw several of them look at the floor, and understood that they were not ashamed enough to disagree.

"He might be right," said Alma. "I'll go."

---

"No," said Ben. "I'll go."

"You'll both stop," said Anna. "I'll go. I'm sixty-four. I've had mine."

Alma turned toward the sound of her mother's voice.

"If you go up there," she said, "you'll be dead in a month. Dad too. I might not be." She said it plainly, the way you read out a timetable. "That's all of it. There isn't any more."

"You don't know that."

"No. Nobody does." Alma stood very straight, the way her grandmother used to sit at the end of a hymn. "I'll tell you what I know. I cut myself and it's gone by the morning. I've not been ill since I was eight. That's my blood and my skin. They make themselves again. My eyes didn't. Whatever doesn't make itself again, I've got no more of than you have." She took a breath. "So I don't know what I'm built for. But I'm the one in here with a chance."

Nobody else offered, and nobody spoke of drawing lots. Every grown-up in that room could count. A lot drawn with Alma standing there would only send somebody up to die so that nobody had to say her name.

"Then I'm coming with you," said Nora, from the door.

"Then you'd be up there for nothing, and Seppo would have a place empty and the whole street at the door." Alma turned her head toward her sister. "Somebody has to hold the door, Nora. You're good at it."

Antti said that he would go back across the street, to his cellar.

"Come up with me," said Alma. "Your cellar's got windows at the pavement. And I'm not sitting up there for a week with nobody to argue with."

Antti considered this. Then he nodded, once.

It was twenty past four. The radio, on a shelf beside the machine, was saying that the dust had begun to come down on the southern islands.

Alma came across the room to her mother. She came close, as close as a kiss, which was the only distance at which she had ever seen her mother's face, and looked at it.

"I've decided," she said, quietly, so that only Anna would hear. "I'm not asking you to. I'm asking you to say I can." She waited. "If you say no, I'll stay. I mean that."

Anna had told her once, at the kitchen sink, that she had decided for her mother with a paper and for her daughter with a pen, and that she would not do it a third time. Alma had heard her. And Alma was handing it back to her anyway, here, with Seppo looking at his watch and a little girl in a raincoat waiting by the door.

She could say no. Alma would stay, and keep her word, because she always had. The door would close on Antti and two small children going back across the street to a cellar with windows at the level of the pavement, and Anna would have her daughter beside her for every day of whatever came, and she would see that girl's raincoat every time she shut her eyes for the rest of her life.

She could go herself. She would very likely die in the third week, of what the dust did to old blood, and Alma would sit nine days in a sealed room knowing her mother had gone up in her place. It was the kindest thing Anna could think of, and it was deciding for Alma a third time, in the worst way there was.

Or she could say the word Alma had asked for, and give her daughter the best chance anyone in the room had, because her daughter had asked for it, and then live with having said it.

On the back of a paper in a piano bench, long ago, her mother had written: *You are going to have to do something terrible. I would like it in writing that I said you could.*

"You can," said Anna. "Go on. You can."

Alma put her forehead against her mother's for a moment. Then she went.

At the door Linnea had hold of her grandfather's coat with both hands. Antti crouched, and unhooked her fingers one at a time, and told her in Finnish that he would be upstairs with Alma, in the Novaks' flat, which was true. Then he picked Otto up off the floor and handed him in to Anna, and stood up, and followed Alma round the corner toward the stairs. Anna heard her daughter begin to count them.

Nora closed the door. It was as heavy as it had been when she was seven. It took both her hands and her shoulder, and when it was shut Seppo turned the levers, one, two, three, four, and wrote something in the binder.

---

The power went that evening. After that the air came in by the crank.

It took a steady arm, at the pace of a slow walk, and anybody could do it for twenty minutes and nobody could do it for long after that. Seppo made a list. Ben turned it far longer than his turns, and at two in the morning Jari came and took the handle out of his hands without a word, and turned it until four.

On the first night there was a knock on the door. Three blows, with a fist, not loud. Seppo sat up on his bunk. Nora stood. Seppo shook his head at her, and she stood there with her hand flat on the steel, and nobody in the room said anything at all. It didn't come again. Nobody ever knew who it was.

Linnea slept on Anna's bunk, with Otto between them. In the night she woke and asked in Finnish where her grandfather was, and Anna told her: upstairs, in the Novaks' flat, with Alma. It wasn't as safe up there as down here. They were keeping each other company.

"Is it safe for Alma?"

"Safer for Alma than for most people," said Anna. "Not as safe as here."

Linnea thought about that, and went back to sleep with her fist in Anna's sleeve.

The radio spoke every hour, and every hour it gave numbers for each part of the city: the dust's strength in the air outside, which meant nothing to anybody, except that the numbers fell. Anna found a pencil in her bag and wrote them down on the back of an envelope, one under another. Her father had sat at his kitchen table in the first spring of the virus and written down the day's dead in a notebook from the plant, one under another, the way he had once written the count off the line.

On the third day, as the binder had said, everybody had a headache. The children breathed hard in their sleep. Nobody spoke of it.

On the fourth day Nora sat down beside her father at the crank.

"You told me there'd be a place for everybody who lives upstairs," she said.

"I did," said Ben.

"She lives upstairs."

"She does."

He had nothing else to say, and he didn't try. He went on turning, and after a while Nora put her hand over his on the handle, and they turned it together.

The woman from the second floor came and sat by Anna that evening, on the edge of the bunk. She was past eighty now. For ten years she had stopped Anna on the stairs every few months to ask how the little one was, and Anna had told her *a trial, in Canada*, and she had said it was a miracle.

"I heard her counting the stairs," she said. "The winter she was eight. Every night, up past my door. Nobody comes back from that."

"No," said Anna.

"I told everybody it was a miracle." The old woman looked up at the ceiling, toward the four floors above them. "A miracle happens once. Whatever they did to her in Canada is still in her blood, up there, today. I'm glad it wasn't a miracle."

---

On the ninth day the radio said that people could leave their shelters for short periods.

Seppo turned the levers back. Nora opened the door and held it, and the air of the corridor came in, stale and ordinary and smelling of the laundry room.

Anna went up first. She had never counted the stairs in thirty-one years in that building. She counted them now, sixty-four, out loud, and at the top she stopped with her hand on her own front door, and couldn't open it.

Then she did.

They had made a nest in the windowless hall in the middle of the flat, out of every mattress in the place, with the bath full of water and the radio on the floor. Antti was sitting up against the wall in Ben's dressing gown. Alma was lying with her head on a cushion, very thin, with her eyes open.

"Mom," she said, before Anna had said anything. She knew her mother's step.

They had both been sick the first night, Alma said, over and over, and then they hadn't been. They had been well enough since to argue. Antti had told her about Kotka, the whole harbour, every ship he had ever seen go out of it as a boy. She had told him the plot of every opera she knew, and he had said they were all the same plot.

"They are," said Antti.

Anna sat down on the mattress and held her daughter. Alma's hair, against her cheek, was as thick as it had always been.

---

It came out in the third week, in handfuls, on the pillow. That was when the fever started, and the bruises that came up on her arms from nothing, the way the doctors had said they might.

The hospital was full of the same thing. Alma lay in a corridor, because the wards were full, and Anna sat with her on a folding chair. Aino brought them blankets, between shifts, in a mask, and didn't stop long, because her father was two floors up.

Antti died on the twenty-third day, of what the dust had done to his blood. Aino was with him. She was on duty that night and arranged it so. He had told her, she said afterward, that he would not have asked for himself, and she had told him she knew.

Ben heard it from Aino in the corridor, at the end of her shift. In thirty years he had rung Antti at midnight once, the spring Anna flew to Ohio, and Antti had said *Yes. What time?* before Ben had finished asking. Ben went out of the hospital and walked a long way round, past the low yellow daycare by the sea where the two of them had first nodded at each other in the dark, and stood at its fence where the bicycle with the studded tyres used to be locked. He stood there until he was cold. Then he walked back, and sat down by Alma's trolley, and told her, because she would ask.

Alma's blood fell further than the doctor had ever seen anyone's come back from. Then it came back.

The doctor was young and very tired, and she sat on the end of Alma's trolley and turned the page of numbers over and over, as if it might say something different on the back.

"I've no idea why you're alive," she said. "I've read about you. That's not the same." She was honest, because Alma asked her to be. The blood and the skin and the lining of the gut were the parts of a body that make themselves new, and Alma's had made themselves new faster than anybody's she had ever seen. The lens of the eye doesn't. Nobody's does. It is made once, and keeps what is done to it. If it was going to cloud, it would cloud slowly, over a year or two. She couldn't say if it would.

Alma thought about that.

"I saw Mom's face this summer," she said. "At the door. Close to." She held her hand an inch from her own nose, to show the doctor how close. "So I've had this summer's."

---

She came home on a Friday at the end of September, in a wool cap of her grandmother's, and climbed the stairs with Ben beside her at her pace, stopping on the landings. On the stairs a neighbour coming down flattened himself against the wall to let her by, further than anybody needed to.

It was Jari. He had his baby in a sling on his chest. He looked at Alma for a moment, and then at the step under his feet.

"Thank you," he said, to the step, and went on down.

Alma, who could not see his face, said nothing. Ben thought, afterward, that she had decided he hadn't earned an answer yet, and that she was probably right.

That week the messages that had been stuck somewhere in the wires since August came through, all at once, out of order. One was from Theo, from the ship: *All of us fine. The sea's grey. Tell Alma I'm coming.* One was from Declan, in Belfast, written eleven days before: *We're here. Are you? Eight o'clock.*

Ben read that one several times. Then he wrote back, *Eight o'clock*, and watched the small wheel turn in the middle of the screen until it went.

---

In October the order was lifted, and the skis went back down. The war had ended by then, people said afterward, though nobody could agree on which day. Elsewhere in the world it had ended the way it had come down on the Estonian coast, many times over, on cities, and the counting of that would go on for years.

Ben carried them, two pairs at a time, and Anna went with him to hold the door. The bunks were folded. The tent was rolled up and tied. The cages were back in their rows, with the building's winters in them. On the wall the machine stood quiet, with the crank at the height of a man's hip, and on its hook beside the door hung Seppo's binder.

Anna took it down and opened it. On the first page was the number. She looked at it for a while.

She closed the binder and hung it back on its hook.

Ben came out of the cage with his hands empty. The door was heavy, and Anna held it with her shoulder until he was through.
