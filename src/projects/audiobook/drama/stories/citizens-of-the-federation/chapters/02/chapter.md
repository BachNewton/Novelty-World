# Chapter 2: Responsible Party

It is Thursday, the eighteenth of March, two thousand and twenty-seven. It has been four months and ten days since the Sunday call on Finnish Father's Day.

America moved its clocks on Sunday. Europe won't move its own for another ten days. So for these two weeks of spring the ocean is an hour narrower, and Helsinki is six hours ahead of Dayton, Ohio, instead of seven. Nobody arranged it. Anna Dorsey has noticed it, because she is awake for most of the hours there are.

Anna's third child was born on the fourteenth of January. Her name is Alma, which is a name in Finnish and a name in English. Walter Dorsey said he'd had an aunt. The cardboard box from the Finnish state has been a bed for nine weeks.

In America, Congress has let the Federal Employment Act lapse. It was the law that promised everyone in a Sanctuary District a job interview. The Guard is in three more cities than it was at Christmas.

On the ninth of March, in Dayton, Walt Dorsey fell. Ben Novak landed in Ohio on Monday. A father in Finland has a hundred and sixty paid days of his own, for his child and nothing else, so Ben is using his vacation. He has come to do something about his mother-in-law, and he has a return ticket for Sunday.

This is Montgomery County Form S-1, Application for Sanctuary Placement. It has eleven sections. A trained clerk can finish it in eleven minutes, in any order she likes. The processing center is in the old county Job Center, by the river on Edwin Moses Boulevard. Last spring the county moved out of the building, and then moved back in, behind the fence.

---

Section one. Name of applicant.

Everyone who works in the building starts the day the same way, with the right hand flat on a glass plate, while the Interface looks up who they are. Carol Flory has worked for this county for twenty-three years. This week, when she lays her hand down, it takes a moment longer than it used to, and a small amber light comes on beside her name.

Her driver's license expires on the ninth of April, which is her birthday. Ohio has withdrawn the form that let the license say F. The new one will take its letter from her birth certificate, and the courts in this district have ruled that a birth certificate records a birth. A county login hangs on a state ID, and when the two disagree, the Interface has a process. Carol trained the others on it.

At ten past nine, Gary Pruitt comes to her counter with a folder. He is the county's caseworker for this side of Dayton.

“Morning, Gary,” Carol says, before she looks up. “You've got your sorry face on. Who have you brought me?”

“Ruth Dorsey, seventy-six,” says Pruitt. “Miami Valley Hospital discharged her this morning. This is her son-in-law, Mr. Novak.”

Ruth is in her good coat, buttoned to the neck, sitting very straight. Ben is holding her purse.

“I'm Carol, and I'm your clerk,” Carol says. “I've got forty-one of these today and a bad knee, so we'll go at the knee's pace. Ma'am, can you tell me your name?”

Ben answers for her. He has started to spell Dorsey when Carol thanks him and tells him she asked Mrs. Dorsey. He begins to explain that Ruth isn't able to, and Carol stops him again, kindly.

“I know what she's not able to,” Carol says. “It's in the packet. This field says, as stated by applicant. Mrs. Dorsey?”

“Mrs. Walter Dorsey,” says Ruth. “Good morning, miss. You've come from the county.”

“I have,” says Carol.

“It's Thursday,” Ruth says.

“It sure is. There's a line here that says, known as. What do people call you, Mrs. Dorsey, when they like you?”

You should know about this field, because almost nobody does. It is optional. It was added years ago, after a man in another county was buried under a name that nobody at his funeral had ever called him. Most clerks tab past it.

“My pupils call me Mrs. Dorsey,” Ruth says. “My husband calls me Ruthie, and he is not to be encouraged.”

It is the first sentence Ben has heard from her in four days that sounded like her. Carol types, Mrs. Dorsey.

---

Section two. Last fixed address.

On the night of the ninth of March, Walter Dorsey got up in the dark, and the good leg, which had carried him for seventy-four years, did not hold. He broke his hip on the kitchen floor at about eleven. His wife came in and looked at him for some time. She went away, and came back with the afghan from the sofa, and spread it over him. Then she went to the front room. He could hear her from where he lay.

He lay there for seventeen hours. Wednesday is collection day, and at four in the afternoon the Henderson boy came up the drive with the empty cans. He is sixteen, and nobody pays him. He saw the kitchen light on in broad daylight, and he went and looked in through the glass.

“I don't understand why the hospital can't keep her,” Ben says. “Anybody can see she needs a hospital.”

“To hold her, the law says she has to be a danger to somebody, or unable to meet her own needs, and those needs can't be met in the community,” says Pruitt. “She hasn't hurt anyone. And the county attorney's written opinion is that the District is in the community. It has a bed and a ration.”

The attorney was asked a plain question, and he gave a plain answer. The District is inside the county, with an address, beds, meals and a fence. Since his opinion was filed, nobody who can be sent to the District has needs that can't be met in the community.

“Nine beds in ten at the state hospitals go to people the courts send,” Pruitt says. “I'm the fella Walt told you about, Mr. Novak. I've called about her every week since October. She's a hundred and sixty-third on the list in Columbus, and I've got two hundred and twelve families. I'm not telling you that so you'll like me.”

---

Section three. Category.

It is one letter. The form offers E for employable, D for diminished, and R for restricted. The staff say gimme, dim and ghost, because they say it forty-one times a day. A person's letter decides her building, her line and her gate, and once it is on her, it is very hard to change. Ruth Dorsey's was chosen at the hospital, before she arrived. It is D.

“Now I need your hand on the glass, Mrs. Dorsey,” Carol says. “It's cold and it's rude, but it doesn't bite. I'll go first. Like that.”

Ruth looks at Carol's hand on the plate, and doesn't put down her own. Instead she takes Carol's hand off the glass, in both of hers, and turns it over.

“Spread your fingers, please,” says Ruth. She measures from the thumb to the little finger, the way she measured forty years of children in her front room. “That's a tenth, easily. That's a man's hand, miss.”

Ben looks at the floor. Gary Pruitt looks at his folder. Carol Flory has been told this at the Kroger on Wayne Avenue, at a funeral, and by her mother. She has never before been told it by someone measuring her reach.

“Yes, ma'am, it is,” Carol says.

Then Ruth leans in close, and her voice drops, and she is frightened. “There's someone in there with you. I can see him, behind your eyes. He has been listening to all of this.”

Ben starts to apologize to the room. Carol doesn't look at him. She looks straight at Ruth.

“He's gone, Mrs. Dorsey,” she says. “He left thirteen years ago.”

“Good,” says Ruth. “He looked tired.”

She is still holding the hand. “Who told you to stop?”

“Stop what?” Carol asks.

“Nobody gets a hand like that to carry groceries.”

Carol starts to laugh, and doesn't quite manage it. “We weren't piano people,” she says. “Old German Baptist, up by Covington. No instruments, not in the house and not in the meetinghouse. Nobody has ever asked me that before. I would have been good, wouldn't I?”

“You'd have been a nuisance, dear,” says Ruth. “The good ones are.”

Then she lays her own hand on the glass, as if on a keyboard, and the Interface finds her, and on the cold plate her fingers begin to move. Four bars. They stop. The same four bars.

“What's she playing?” Carol asks.

“I don't know,” Ben says. “It's the same little piece, for months now. On the piano, on the table, on the hospital tray. She never gets past that part.”

Carol taps the rhythm on the counter with one nail. “That's Be Still, My Soul. The Lord is on thy side. I sing the tenor line on that with the Methodists on Salem Avenue, Wednesday nights. They were short of tenors, and they didn't ask. It's a Finnish tune, you know. Sibelius wrote it. Everybody here thinks it's ours.”

“It's from Finland?” Ben asks. He asks it very quietly, and nobody answers him, because the form has another section.

---

Section six. Dietary requirements.

On Monday night, Ben opened the kitchen cupboards in Dayton. He counted sixty-one cans of soup, stacked labels out by a man who had once kept a parts crib at Delco. There was nothing else. On Tuesday he took the bus to the Kroger on Wayne Avenue, and from the aisle he called his wife.

He read her the prices. Eggs were seven dollars and forty cents a dozen. A piece of salmon the size of his phone was nineteen dollars. She didn't know what eggs cost in Helsinki. She had never looked. Nora had eaten salmon soup at school that day, for nothing, and complained about it.

“Is there anything near them?” Anna asked. “Anything he can walk to?”

“There's a dollar store four blocks down,” Ben said. “Nothing fresh. There's a cooler of bologna. Anna, you know the walker? It has a wire basket on the front. He's been going down there on that leg, with the basket, and coming back with soup, because she won't eat anything she didn't hear open. That's what it was for. It was for the cans.”

Anna didn't say anything for a while. On the line there was the music of a supermarket in Ohio, and the breathing of a baby in Finland.

The District's food comes from a contractor in Indiana that also feeds the state's prisons: eighteen hundred calories a day, on a tray, for those who still have a ration card. A woman who eats only what she hears open won't eat from a tray. At the counter, Ben says so, badly, and Carol understands him anyway.

“A medical diet takes a doctor,” Carol says. “The District has one, three mornings a week. A religious diet takes a check mark. What is she?”

“Lutheran,” says Ben. “She played the organ at her church for thirty years.”

“Lutherans eat anything. That's no help to us.” Carol scrolls. “Kosher comes sealed. Two wrappers, opened in front of you. You can hear it crack.”

The form does not ask anyone to prove a faith. The county's lawyers decided long ago that a form may not test what a person believes, so a check mark is a statement, like a name. At twenty past ten on a Thursday morning, Ruth Dorsey, who played the organ for the Lutherans for thirty years, becomes Jewish, for the wrappers.

“Carol,” says Pruitt.

“She's Jewish until somebody checks, Gary,” Carol says, without looking up. “Nobody has ever checked.”

---

Section four. Consent to placement.

Pruitt turns the screen toward Ben. “I've got this filled in as voluntary, Mr. Novak. She signs, she's a resident, and nobody goes to court. If she can't sign, the county files for guardianship on Monday. Then she's a ward, with a lawyer who has sixty others, and he sells the house to pay for her keep. A ward stays until the county says. I'm telling you which one I'd want.”

Carol slides the signature pad across the counter and holds out the pen. The humor has gone out of her voice. “Mrs. Dorsey, this one's for you. Right there, by the X.”

Ruth takes the pen and looks at the line, and becomes very alert. “Is this the report?”

Nobody answers her quickly enough, and she goes on. “If I put my name here, she stays. That's the arrangement, isn't it? She's fed and she's clean. You needn't take her anywhere. You can take me instead. Put it down that I came quietly. Where do I sign?”

She thinks it is the autumn of nineteen ninety-six. She is offering herself to the county in exchange for a girl of eleven. It would take four seconds. It would be legal enough, and nobody would ever look at it again. Ben has been awake since Monday, and he has a ticket for Sunday, and he finds, standing there with her purse, that he is holding his breath, and that he is hoping.

Carol reaches across the counter and holds out her hand, palm up. “I'm going to need that pen back, Mrs. Dorsey.”

Pruitt says her name, the way he says it when she is about to cost them both an afternoon. “Then it's a ward on Monday,” he says. “You know it is.”

“She thinks she's buying a child, Gary,” says Carol. “I won't take that signature. I can hold her on a medical until five tomorrow.” She is already standing, on the bad knee. “Mr. Novak, walk with me. I get ten minutes and a Danish.”

---

Section five. Next of kin.

“I was going to let her,” Ben says. “You saw that. I just stood there.”

“I saw,” says Carol. “I've let four hundred people do it. Yesterday I sent a man with no feet up to a third floor. Today I didn't feel like it. Tomorrow I might.”

Ben makes himself ask it with the word the staff use. He has never said it out loud before. “What's it like in there for a dim?”

“Diminished capacity is seven syllables,” Carol says, “and I say it forty-one times a day. You want it honest? A dim keeps her ration card about a day and a half. Then she's hungry. Your mother-in-law is a nice old lady who thinks I'm the county, and I am.”

She eats the Danish. Then, in the same breath, she asks him whether Ruth ever wrote anything down.

“Write what down?” Ben asks.

“Who decides,” Carol says. “Ohio has a paper for it, a declaration for mental health treatment. You name your proxy while you're well, and it beats a guardian. A woman who's been locked up once and come home, with anybody sensible around her, has one. They know what's coming.”

“How do you know that?” Ben asks. “It isn't on the form. Gary didn't know it.”

“Because I have one,” Carol says. “Not that kind. Same glove box. So does everybody I know. Who my next of kin is, who's not allowed in the room, what name goes on the stone. My son's in the Guard. He's twenty-three. He calls me Carol, because we tried the other words. He knows where it is.”

She folds the wrapper into a small square. “You write down who you are while somebody's still obliged to read it.”

“Ask the husband,” she says.

“He'll tell me everything's fine,” says Ben.

“Sure he will. So don't ask him if there is one. Ask him where it is.”

---

The nursing home is in Kettering, and it takes two buses. This is Walter Dorsey's fifth day of the twenty that Medicare pays for in full. After that it is more than two hundred dollars a day. The surgeon pinned the hip, and said the leg would not bear him again. The polio has come back a little for the muscles that breathe. At night he sleeps in a mask that does some of his breathing for him.

“How's she eating?” Walt asks, before Ben has sat down.

“They're working on it,” Ben says. “Walt, where's the paper?”

“What paper's that?” says Walt, who knows.

“The one she signed, about who decides. I'm not asking you if there is one.”

Walt looks at him for a long time. “Who told you to put it that way?”

“A woman at the county,” says Ben.

“Piano bench,” Walt says. “Under the hymnal. Dolores drove her down to the bank to get it stamped, two years ago this month. She does it every three years, like the car.”

Ben asks him why he never said. Ruth has been in a hospital for eight days, and the paper was in the piano bench the whole time.

“Fifty years,” says Walt. “I carried that woman up the stairs at Good Samaritan on this leg. You go on and read it. You read whose name she wrote down.”

Then, from long habit, he starts to tell Ben to tell Annie everything's fine. He gets as far as everything's, and the rest doesn't come.

“Tell Annie,” he says.

---

It is nine in the evening in Dayton, and three in the morning in Helsinki, where the light lasts past six now. Anna is sitting up in the dark with Alma on her chest and the phone against her knee. In the front room in Dayton, Ben lifts the lid of the piano bench, and under the hymnal there is an envelope from the bank.

Inside is the state's form, filled in, signed and stamped. Where the form leaves a space for any other instructions, Ruth has gone on writing, past the space and onto the back of the page. Ben reads it aloud. After the first line he stops hearing himself.

“The eleventh of March, two thousand and twenty-five. I am in my right mind, and I have a notary here to say so, which is more than most people can claim.”

“I have schizoaffective disorder. When I am ill, I don't know that I am. That is the whole of it, and it has taken me fifty years to get it into one sentence. I have taken it up with God on a regular basis for the same fifty years. I still go. Somebody has to play.”

“When I am ill, I will tell you I am perfectly well. I will tell you the injection is poison, and that you are not who you are. Do not listen to me. Listen to this. Hold my arm if you have to. I have been held before, and I would rather be held than go where I go.”

“I name my daughter, Anna Dorsey, to decide. Not my husband. Walter would carry me up a mountain on that leg and tell you we were fine. Somebody must look after him too. Anna is the only person in this family who has ever read a form to the end.”

“She has gone to live in the country that hymn came from. I looked it up. So at eleven on Sundays, when they call, I play it, as far as I can get. She has never said anything. That's all right. I never told a pupil everything, either.”

“Annie. You will have to do something terrible. I would like it in writing that I said you could.”

For a while the line carries only the sound of a nine-week-old, breathing.

“Anna?” Ben says. “It's that tune. The four bars. A woman at the county knew it. It's Finlandia. She's been playing it to you.”

“I know what it is now,” Anna says. “They played it at Nora's school in December, in the gym, for Independence Day. I stood there holding my coat, and I thought, that's pretty.”

She wipes her face with the heel of her hand, and sits up, and moves the baby higher on her chest. “Send me the form.”

Ben tells her it's three in the morning.

“She says I read to the end,” Anna says. “Send me the form.”

She reads it on a phone, one-handed, in the light from the street. It takes her forty minutes. In section ten she finds what nobody has told them. A resident may be released only to a responsible party with a fixed address. A ward has no responsible party but the county.

“Every memory care place you called is nine thousand dollars a month,” Anna says, “and wants her calm before they'll take her, and she won't be calm without the shot. Nobody will give her the shot without somebody to sign for it, and the only clinic that will see her this month is inside the fence. If I sign, she goes in. If I don't, she goes in anyway, and nobody on earth can get her out. So I sign.”

Ben starts to tell her she doesn't have to do it tonight, and gets no further.

“I filled out the forms to leave her, Ben. I can fill out this one.”

---

Section nine. Community resources offered and declined.

Friday, nine in the morning in Dayton, three in the afternoon in Helsinki. Ben has the phone flat on Carol's counter, on speaker, between a new Danish and the plate of glass.

“Ms. Flory? This is Anna Dorsey. I've read your form.”

“Nobody reads the form,” says Carol.

“Section nine comes filled in,” Anna says. “Community resources offered and declined. Nobody offered my mother anything. A sign went up on a door. I need the form to say what happened, because Finland won't take an elderly parent who can get treatment in her home country, and I've been reading that sentence since November. If a county document says she can't, it's the first piece of paper I've had.”

Carol scrolls. “There's a dropdown. The third one down says, none available. In twenty-three years I've never seen anybody pick it.”

“Why not?” Anna asks.

“Because it goes to a supervisor,” Carol says. “That's what it's for. It's the one field in the whole form that goes to a human being, and she opens it up, and she looks at who filed it.”

“Is that a problem?” Ben asks.

“It's Friday,” says Carol.

She is forty-eight. She took her name at thirty-five, out of the county staff directory, because there were nine Carols in it, and nobody had ever looked at a Carol twice. For thirteen years the work of her life has been to be a file that no one has a reason to open. Nobody in the room knows this. She moves the selection down two lines. The amber light is still on.

---

Section eleven. Responsible party.

“County of residence,” Carol reads. “I've got all eighty-eight in Ohio, and I've got Other.”

“Other,” says Anna.

“You understand what this makes you,” Carol says. “Once she's in, you're the only person who can ever get her out.”

“I know,” says Anna. “I read section ten.”

“It's on your screen. Sign with your finger. It looks terrible. Everybody's does.”

In a kitchen in Helsinki, beside a cupboard with no floor, Anna Dorsey signs. It is very quick.

“Is she there?” Anna asks, and before Carol can answer, asks her not to put Ruth on. “Would you tell her something, from the county? Tell her it's in the report. Tell her she's doing a very good job, and the girl is staying right where she is.”

“Yes, ma'am,” says Carol. “I can put that down.”

She puts it down. Then she comes around the counter on the bad knee, to the bench where Ruth has sat all morning with her purse on her lap, and says it to Mrs. Dorsey word for word, with the clipboard held where it can be seen.

“Well,” says Ruth. “Thank you. That's kind.”

---

The Montgomery County Sanctuary District runs from the river to the rail line. It was built for nineteen hundred people, and it holds forty-two hundred. Most of what the fence closed in was west Dayton already, which waited years for a grocery store and in the end bought shares in one of its own, a co-op on Salem Avenue. Some of the owners are now inside.

In Building C there are eleven working toilets for nine hundred residents. The women's floor has a bolt on the inside, which the women fitted themselves, because of the ghosts. Some of the job interviews have been promised for four years. The Act that promised them is gone, and nobody has told the list. On the second floor there is a man who died in the night. He will be collected before noon.

Lucille Odom meets the medical holds at the inner door, for an extra tray. She is sixty-two, and she ran an elementary school kitchen for nineteen years, until the school closed. The rule that you must work for your food stamps used to stop at fifty-four. Since two years ago it stops at sixty-four. It asked her for eighty hours of work a month, and there wasn't any. It gave her three months without it. Then it took the food, and then the rent went.

Ben carries the purse as far as the inner door, which is as far as he is allowed. Ruth takes it from him there, and offers him her hand, the way you would at the end of a lesson.

“Young man, you've been very kind,” she says. “You're the tuner.”

Ben has been in Ohio for four days. On the first of them, he would have told her his name.

“Yes, ma'am,” Ben says. “She's holding her pitch beautifully. I'll come by again in the spring.”

“Do,” says Ruth. “And wipe your feet.”

Lucille looks at the ration card in Ruth's hand. “That your card, baby? They're going to have that off you by supper. Hold still. Let me see your neck.”

“Are you the new pupil?” Ruth asks her.

“Yes, ma'am, I am,” says Lucille. “Mind that step, now. It's bad all the way up.”

She takes the lace out of her left shoe, threads it through the ration card, and ties it around Ruth Dorsey's neck, inside the collar, with a knot from nineteen years of apron strings. She doesn't mention it. For the rest of that day she walks with one shoe loose.

---

Ben flies home on Sunday. On Monday, the twenty-second, a woman of seventy-one comes to the visitors' window on two buses, with a small cooler bag and a prescription the District's nurse practitioner has signed on Anna's word. It is Dolores. She has kept her nursing license current since the clinic closed, for no reason she could have given you.

The visitors' log has three boxes: family, legal and clergy. Dolores is none of them. Carol Flory, whose review has not come up yet, ticks clergy.

“Ruthie, it's Dolores,” she says. “Give me your arm.”

“You're late,” says Ruth.

“I know I am,” says Dolores.

It has been seven months. After that long, the medicine has to be started over: one dose now, and a second a week later, so Dolores will come again on the twenty-ninth, when Europe has moved its clocks and the ocean is its proper width. It will be weeks before anyone knows what comes back, and it may not all come back. Ruth Dorsey signed for that as well, in a good year, at the bank.

In Helsinki, a form has been filled in online. It has a county document attached, in English, which says, none available. The applicant must still go in person, to prove who she is. In a queue in the county building in Dayton, a supervisor has a field to open.

There is nobody at the house now, and nobody to fill the cans. On Tuesday night the Henderson boy walks them down to the curb anyway, and on Wednesday, after school, he brings them back up the drive, so that from the street it will look as if somebody's home.
