# Chapter 8: The Winter You're Ten

At ten o'clock on the first Tuesday morning of February 2030, Ruth Dorsey was due at the Finnish embassy in Washington, to prove that she was who she said she was.

In Helsinki that was five in the afternoon. Anna stood at her kitchen counter with the folder open, watching the clock on the oven come round to it. It was already dark. Across the street the school had its flag up again, this time for the poet who wrote the words of Finland's anthem. Anna had learned that for her exam, and found that she liked knowing it.

Everything was in the folder. On top was the immigration service's decision, which she could read now without a dictionary: her mother could come and live in Finland. Under it was the county's paper from Dayton that said *none available*, and under that the booking for a flight Anna would not be taking. The card that went with the permit was waiting in Washington. The embassy would hand it to nobody but Ruth, once it had seen her face and taken her fingerprints.

It had been planned since November, and it had been simple. On Monday afternoon Dolores would have collected Ruth from the District's window, on a release Anna had signed in December, and the two of them would have taken the night bus east: Dolores with her bag on her knees, Ruth in her good coat. Anna would have flown in on Sunday and been waiting at the embassy door by a quarter to ten. On Wednesday she would have flown home with her mother.

The buses stopped first. "Nobody's running east, Anna," Dolores said on the phone, in the last week of January. "Not past Columbus. The man gave me my money back and said try the train. I said, what train?" Then the airline wrote to Anna, very politely, about her flight. Then on Friday she rang the embassy, and a recording in Finnish, Swedish and English told her that its services were suspended until further notice.

Nobody had written to cancel the appointment. As far as any office knew, it was still at ten. So at five o'clock Anna stood at the counter and let it go by, because it seemed wrong to be doing anything else while it did.

Then she rang the home in Kettering, as she had every day since Sunday.

Her father had lived there since his fall, nearly three years. On Sundays at eleven an aide held a tablet up for him, a young woman who said "Okay, Mr. D" when it was time to go, and he told Anna that the food was terrible and he was fine. The last call had been two Sundays ago, while the buses were still running. He had told her to get her mother out of that place and not to worry about him, because he had the television, and the girl, and the food was terrible. Anna had never been able to decide how she felt about taking her mother an ocean away from her father. He had decided it for her, as he decided most things, by saying he was fine.

Last Sunday nobody had picked up at eleven. Nobody had picked up since. The home ran on government money, and since January nobody could say which government's, and Anna supposed the people who answered its phone had stopped being paid. The number rang now with its long American ring until it stopped by itself.

Nora came in from her room with Alma behind her. Nora was ten, and since New Year she had been answering one question in the house, whenever anyone asked it and often when nobody had.

"Eight," she said, and held up eight fingers for her sister.

Alma was three. She counted them, and got to eight on the second try, and looked delighted with it.

"Eight days," Nora told her in Finnish, and then again in English, for her mother, out of politeness. "Then they're home."

For a moment Anna heard it wrong, and thought she meant her grandparents. She meant the Shango.

Nora's tablet lay on the table, and on it was the picture the Shango had sent back that week, which she had made her background: the Earth and the moon together, seen from a long way off, a blue dot and a grey one. She could cover the whole Earth with a fingertip. Sometimes she did, to see it come back.

Anna closed the folder. The clock on the oven said four minutes past five. As far as anyone at the embassy would ever know, Ruth Dorsey had not turned up.

---

On Friday night there was ice in Dayton.

Anna found it on a map. On Saturday morning the power company's map of Kettering had a red shape over the home, and over the streets round it. In any other winter the crews would have had the lines up again in a day. All Saturday the red shape stayed where it was, and on Sunday morning it was still there.

Her father's breathing machine plugged into the wall. It had a battery. Anna looked up how long the battery lasted, and wished she hadn't.

At three on Sunday afternoon she rang Carol Flory.

She had the number because of the release. Carol kept the visitors' window at the District, and in December she had rung Anna twice about the forms, and both times called her ma'am. Anna had only ever been a voice to her, on Ben's phone at a county counter, nearly three years before. In January she had seen Carol's face for the first time, on every phone in the world, beside a young man's.

Carol answered on the second ring.

"Ms. Flory, it's Anna Dorsey. Ruth's daughter."

"I know who you are, ma'am."

"I'm sorry. It's Sunday. I don't have anybody else in that county who picks up." Anna had rehearsed the rest, and said it as plainly as she could: the home, the ice, the machine. "He'll be expecting us at eleven. We always call at eleven. I need somebody to go over there and hold up a phone."

Carol asked for the address, and Anna heard her write it down.

"Kettering," said Carol. "I've got enough gas to get there. We'll worry about back."

"Let me send you something for the gas."

"No, thank you," said Carol.

---

Ben took Theo and Alma to the sledging hill in the park, to give the afternoon something to do. Nora stayed. She sat at the kitchen table with her homework and did some of it, and every so often she looked at her mother.

"Three," she said once, to nobody.

The others came in at half past five with snow in their boots, and Ben looked at Anna and didn't ask. At six she propped the tablet against the fruit bowl, where it went on Sundays, and a minute later it rang.

Carol's face came first, close and grey in a poor light, with her reading glasses on their chain. "I'm here," she said. "I've found him."

She turned the phone round.

It was a long room with one window, and every bed in the place had been pushed into it, toward the light. There were old people in the beds with their coats on, and a young woman in scrubs and a parka going from one to the next. Anna knew her voice. She was the one who said "Okay, Mr. D." The light from the window was white and thin. In the bed nearest it was Anna's father, under three blankets, with his cap from the plant on his head. On the table beside him was the mask, and the machine it belonged to, with no light on it.

Somebody had combed his hair.

"Hi, Dad," said Anna.

His eyes opened. It took him a moment to find the screen.

"There's my girl," said Walt. His voice was a thread. Then, before anything else: "How's she eating?"

Anna didn't know. She opened her mouth to say so, and Carol answered for her, from behind the phone.

"She's eating, Mr. Dorsey. I see her every week. She's got eleven pupils."

"Eleven," said Walt, slowly, to keep it.

Nora had come round the table. She leaned in beside her mother, close to the screen, and said in the careful English she kept for him, "Grandpa, they're coming home on Wednesday. The Shango. It's the winter I'm ten."

It took Walt's eyes a moment to find her.

"Wednesday," he said.

Then he looked up, past the screen, at the woman holding it, and gave her the message he had always sent with anybody going Anna's way. "Tell Annie everything's fine."

"I know, Dad," said Anna. "I know it is. Everything's fine here too."

He closed his eyes. Anna took the tablet from against the fruit bowl and held it close.

---

Carol held the phone.

She held it for a long time. Walt slept, and his breath went slowly in and out under the blankets, and the aide in the parka came and went. Anna talked to him. She hadn't decided to; it was something for her voice to do. She told him that Theo had lost a tooth and wanted paying for it in two currencies. She told him the snow was up to the windowsills and he'd have hated it. Then she told him that she was going to grow tomatoes this summer, in pots on the balcony, and that he would have to tell her how, because she had never once paid attention.

In time the picture began to shake, and Carol changed arms. Later the other arm tired too, and the picture tipped back, and for a few seconds Anna was looking at a ceiling in Kettering: square tiles, a brown stain in one of them, and a light that wasn't on.

"Sorry," said Carol, and Walt's face came back.

Ben was standing behind Anna's chair. He heard Carol say sorry.

The breathing had changed. Carol could hear it, there in the room, and Anna could see it without being sure of what she saw. The breaths came further apart, and each one seemed to cost more.

Nora had gone to brush her teeth and come back, because she couldn't think of a reason to be anywhere else. She stood at her mother's shoulder in her pyjamas and looked at her grandfather's face in the white light. Then she said something to him in Finnish, which was her own language and not his.

"*Hyvää matkaa*, Grandpa."

Nobody put it into English.

A little after that, there was a long space in Walt's breathing, and then there wasn't any more of it.

Carol saw it first. She said, "Anna," and that was all. Holding the phone in one hand, she reached down with the other, out of the picture, and Anna saw her hand come to rest on her father's, on top of the blankets. It was a big hand, and it stayed there.

Anna found that she was thinking about his hair: that somebody had combed it for the call, and that he would have wanted to know.

---

Carol stayed on long enough to say the practical things. The aide would stay with him. A home on the next road had a generator, and the aide was going to ring them about the others. Carol would try the funeral homes, and one of them would pick up, sooner or later.

"Will you tell my mother?" said Anna. "I don't want her to hear it from the county."

"I'm the county, ma'am," said Carol. "But I'll tell her myself. Tomorrow, at the window, first thing."

"Tell her he asked how she was eating. Tell her that was the first thing he said."

"I'll tell her that first," said Carol.

Her battery was going, and she said so, and the call ended. Anna went on holding the tablet up in front of her in both hands, at the height she had been holding it for two hours, with nothing on it now but her own face. Ben took it out of her hands.

Theo was in the doorway. He asked his sister something in Finnish, and Nora answered him with one word. Anna understood them both.

Late that night a message came to Ben's phone from Declan, in Belfast, who had seen Ben's note to the team.

*Sorry for your trouble, Ben. That's what we say. Tell Anna.*

A minute later: *I'm up, if you're up.*

Ben didn't ring him. He found it helped to know he could.

On Monday afternoon a message came for Anna from Carol, written at the window, with every apostrophe in it.

*I told your mother this morning. I told her the first thing he asked was how she was eating. She asked me whether he'd said everything was fine. I told her he had. She said, "He would." Then she went back to Building C. She had a lesson at ten.*

---

Anna had forgotten it was Wednesday.

She had spent Monday and Tuesday on the phone to Kettering, to a funeral home that answered and a bank that didn't, and had learned that her father would be kept until he could be buried, and that nobody could tell her when that would be. At seven on Wednesday evening she was at the kitchen table with a list, and Nora went past her into the other room and turned on the television.

The Shango had been gone for nearly six years. In that time it had been further than anything with people in it had ever been, and turned round, and come back on the day it said it would.

It came down in the Pacific, off California, where the plan had always put it. Nobody had had to decide which of America's two Presidents it was landing under, because the sea belonged to neither of them, and the ship that went out to fetch it belonged to the company that had built the capsule. Within the hour each President welcomed the crew home on behalf of the United States. Both statements thanked the many countries that had built the Shango and sent it. Neither mentioned the other President.

Anna heard the Finnish commentator through the wall, and Theo asking a question, and Alma asking the same question louder. She got up and went to stand in the doorway.

On the screen, a capsule hung under its parachutes over a grey sea, very small, and then not so small, and then it hit the water and sat there rocking, scorched down one side, while boats came at it from three directions. Nora was on the floor, as close to the screen as she was allowed. Ben was on the sofa with Alma, and Theo beside him with his new gap in his teeth.

It took a long time. The boats fussed round the capsule. A crane on the ship lifted it out of the sea and set it on the deck, and men in white suits with masks over their faces went round it with hoses. Then the hatch opened.

They came out one at a time, and none of them walked. Each was lifted from the hatch by two men and set in a chair, and the chairs were carried down the deck, past the cameras, toward a long white box on the far side of the ship with a window in its side.

"Why are they carrying them?" said Theo.

"They can't walk yet," said Nora, who had read about it. "Their legs forgot."

"Forgot what?"

"Being heavy."

The crew waved from the chairs. Some of them were crying, and one man was laughing so hard that the men carrying him had to stop. Nora counted them under her breath in Finnish. Eight had gone, and eight came home.

The fourth was Renée Picard. Her hair was short now, and she was much thinner than the young Frenchwoman who had waved on her way to the rocket on Nora's fifth birthday. As her chair went past, somebody held a microphone out to her, and she took it.

"We're home," she said. "You can all go to bed now."

Anna laughed out loud, because her father would have. It came out of her before she knew it was there, and she put her hand over her mouth and went back into the kitchen.

---

She was still in the kitchen when the rest of the news came. Nora brought it, with the remote still in her hand.

"They have to stay in the white box," she said. "For weeks. In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case of what they brought." Nora was breathing fast. "They brought something, Mom. Not from Europa." She sounded a little offended about that. "From Io. It's another moon. It's yellow, and it has volcanoes. Nobody was even looking there. They brought something home from it in a box, and it's alive."

In the whole history of the world, nobody had ever found anything alive anywhere but here.

Anna went back to the doorway. On the television, the Finnish commentator said it, and then said it again in other words, and then a third time. Even in Finnish, Anna could hear that she was checking. On Ben's phone, people in a dozen countries were already afraid of it, and other people were already in love with it.

Later in the evening they showed Renée again, in her chair behind the window of the white box, talking to a reporter on the deck through a microphone. The reporter asked her what it was.

"We don't know yet," said Renée. "It's very small, and it's alive. The first time we shone a light on it, it moved toward the light. By the tenth time, it didn't bother." She looked at the reporter through the glass. "Something in there was paying attention. I may be wrong. I've had a long time out there to talk myself into things. But until somebody shows me it isn't someone, I'd like us to be careful with it."

---

When the others had gone to bed, Nora came back into the kitchen with the tin from her room where she kept the things she had stopped using and wouldn't throw away.

In the bottom of it, among a dead watch and some shells, was the orange crayon. It was shorter than her fingernail. Nora took a sheet of plain paper from the printer and sat down across from her mother, and held the stub between the tip of her finger and her thumb, which was the only way it could be held. She leaned over the paper with her tongue between her teeth. Then she pressed, hard, once, in the middle.

Anna watched her do it. At the end of another table, in Dayton, her father had once watched Nora start the first crack.

Nora looked at the mark for a while. It was small and orange and very bright, and there was nothing else on the paper at all. She had left the rest of it alone. Her grandmother had taught her that.

"What is it?" said Theo from the doorway. He was supposed to be asleep.

"It's what she found," said Nora.

"It's a dot."

"It's very small," said Nora. "That's the size it is."

She put the drawing on the refrigerator door, beside Europa, under the same magnet, and went to bed. There was a crumb of orange left on the table. Anna didn't wipe it up.

Before she went to bed herself, she took a picture of the drawing and sent it to Carol Flory, to show her mother at the window. A small wheel turned in the middle of the screen, and turned. Then it went through.
