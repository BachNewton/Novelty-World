# Chapter 8: Responsible Party

On a Tuesday night in April, a little before eleven, Walt Dorsey got up to see that the back door was locked, as he had every night for fifty years, and his good leg, which had carried him since he was five, didn't hold. He went down on the kitchen floor, and his hip broke under him.

Ruth came in at the noise and stood in the doorway in her nightgown. He told her to get the phone. She looked at the phone on the wall, and then at him, and he watched her decide that it belonged to the county and was listening. She had believed that since the autumn.

"They'll hear," she said.

She went away and came back with the blanket off the sofa, and spread it over him carefully, as if she were tucking in a child. Later, he couldn't have said how much later, she brought a can of tomato soup and the can opener and set them on the floor by his hand, side by side. Then she went into the front room, and he heard her start the slow hymn, and stop, and start it again.

He couldn't open a can lying on his back. He tried for a while, for her sake.

In the drawer under the phone, still in its box, was the button on a cord that Anna had sent from Finland at Christmas: you pressed it, and somebody came. He had told her it was a fine idea, and that the cord would catch on the walker, and that anyway he was fine. He thought about the button for some of the night. For most of it he thought about whether Ruth had eaten, which was how he had always lived.

He lay on the kitchen floor for seventeen hours.

The Henderson boy found him. Caleb was sixteen now. For years he had taken the Dorseys' garbage cans down to the street on Tuesday nights and brought them back up on Wednesdays after school, and every Wednesday Walt came out on the porch and tried to give him a dollar, and Caleb wouldn't take it. That Wednesday nobody came out. Caleb stood on the porch with the odd, empty feeling of a dollar not refused. Then he went round to the kitchen window.

---

It was eleven at night in Helsinki when Caleb's mother found Anna's number on the list taped inside the Dorseys' cupboard door.

Anna was up with Alma, who was three months old and had views about the night. She stood in the dark kitchen with the baby on her shoulder and asked the questions in the order that mattered: which hospital, which floor, where her mother was. Her mother was in the same hospital, two floors above her father, behind a door that locked. She had told the police she was perfectly well.

In November, thirty weeks pregnant, Anna had chosen not to fly. Nobody could stop her now. By midnight she had a seat on the seven o'clock plane, with one change, and Dayton by the evening. Alma would come with her. Alma fed from her and would take a bottle from nobody.

Ben didn't say he would manage. He rang Antti, at midnight, which in four years he had never done, and Antti said, "Yes. What time?" before Ben had finished asking. Then he rang Belfast, and Declan said he'd take Ben's mornings for as long as it took and Ben wasn't to argue.

Tomorrow was Nora's birthday. She would be eight.

She came out of her room at five, while Anna was buckling on the baby carrier in the hall, holding a sheet of paper flat against her chest. Every birthday since her fifth, the day the Europa crew launched for Jupiter, Nora had drawn her grandfather a space picture for his fridge. This year's she had drawn last night under the covers with a torch, because it had to go today: a ship coming in toward a planet with stripes, moons round it like marbles, and in the ship's window eight round faces.

"It's early," said Nora. "It still counts."

Anna sang to her in the hall in her coat, very quietly, so as not to wake Theo: in English and then in Finnish, to the same tune. Then she put the picture in her bag between two books, and went.

---

The house in Dayton was cold and very clean.

The blanket was still on the kitchen floor. Beside it stood the can of soup and the opener, where nobody had wanted to be the one to move them. Anna stood in the doorway with Alma asleep on her chest, and couldn't go in.

Then she saw the fridge.

There was no fridge left to see. There were the birthday pictures: the rocket going up, Jupiter's cracked white moon, the ship on its way with its eight faces. Round them, overlapping, held on with every magnet in the house, was every other drawing Nora had ever put in an envelope for Ohio: rockets, mostly, and a great many astronauts, and one of Grandpa, taller than the house, with one leg done in silver crayon. The first picture was where Walt had put it three years ago, at the height of a five-year-old's eyes, under the magnet from the plant. The rest climbed the door from there, as the girl who drew them grew.

Anna opened the cupboards, because her hands needed something to do. There were sixty-one cans of soup, stacked with the labels facing out, and nothing else. In the front hall the walker had a wire basket on the front, and four blocks away there was a shop that sold everything for a dollar or two. She saw her father going down there all winter on one good leg and one bad one, with the basket rattling, so that her mother would have something she had heard being opened.

*That's for the garbage cans.*

In the front room the piano stood with its lid up. Under the seat of the bench, under the hymnal, was the paper with her name on it. All winter it had needed a doctor to look at her mother, and her mother wouldn't open the door to one. Now there was a doctor two floors above her father. Anna looked at the bench and didn't open it. She told herself she was too tired to read anything tonight, and took the baby up to bed under the ceiling where the plastic stars had been.

---

In the morning her father was in a bed on the fourth floor with a pin in his hip. Before she had sat down he said, "How's she eating?"

"Dad."

"She won't eat off a plate. She has to hear it opened." He was looking past her at the door. "Is she eating?"

"I'll find out." Alma woke and looked at him. "The button was in the drawer, Dad."

Walt looked at the baby. He didn't say he was fine. It was the first time in her life Anna had watched him not say it, and it was worse.

She gave him Nora's picture. He held it at arm's length and read every word of it.

"Put it with the others," he said.

Two floors up, her mother sat by a window in a hospital gown with her hair uncombed, and looked at Anna with a polite and terrible care.

"You're very like her," said Ruth. "They've done well."

Anna had read the words a hundred times in her mother's writing: *I will tell you that you are not who you are.* Reading them had been nothing like hearing them.

Ruth looked at the baby, and for a moment something else was in her face. "She's warm enough," she said. It wasn't a question. It was the look she gave children coming in out of the snow for their lessons. Then the politeness came back. "You can tell them I didn't sign anything."

The county's caseworker was waiting in the corridor: a heavy, tired man called Gary Pruitt, who had rung Walt every week since the autumn exactly when he said he would. He walked Anna to the elevator.

"She won't take the shot," he said. "Not from the doctors here, not from Dolores. A pharmacist can give it now, with a prescription, but not to somebody who says no. Nobody can. And the hospital lets her go Monday. They can only keep her if she's a danger, or can't look after herself in the community." He pressed the button. "Last year the county asked its lawyer whether the District counts as the community. It's got beds and meals and an address, so he said it did. Since then everybody who can be sent there can look after herself in the community."

"And a care home?"

"Want nine thousand a month, and want her settled first, and she won't settle without the shot. Medicare's the old folks' insurance, and it pays for hospitals, not for that. There's a hospital in Columbus with a list. She's a hundred and sixty-third." He looked at her. "And who's in that house with her tonight?"

Anna had a ticket home for the next Sunday, a baby on her chest, and a husband in Helsinki with two children and a job the family's permits hung on.

"So she goes downtown on Monday," said Pruitt. "All I can tell you is whose word she goes in on. She can sign herself in. Then she's like anybody in there: she can ask to leave, and she can say no to whatever they offer her, the shot too. Or there's a paper. Dolores says there is. Then the doctor in there and one other person look at her and write down that she can't decide for herself, and you decide. You can say yes to the shot, and you're the one who can sign her out." He watched the numbers over the elevator door. "Or there's neither, and we go to court, and she's the county's. She gets a guardian with sixty others like her. He sells the house to pay for her keep. And then there's nobody on God's earth who can sign her out."

The elevator came. "The one other person's me, as it happens," he said, faintly embarrassed. "I'm licensed for it. Nobody ever asks a caseworker. I'll be at the counter Monday at nine."

That night Anna walked past the bench again on her way up to bed. *Don't you let them put your mother in one of those places.* If her mother signed herself in, it would be her mother's doing. If the paper came out of the bench, it would be Anna's. She knew which one she was hoping for, and she was ashamed of it, and she went on hoping.

---

On Sunday evening Dolores came to the house with a pot of chicken adobo, because she couldn't think what else to do, and sat at the kitchen table across from the fridge.

"Carol will do the form tomorrow," she said. "You met her on my steps, with her boy."

"I remember. She brought you groceries."

"Be good to her this week." Dolores looked at the drawings on the fridge rather than at Anna. "She came to my clinic at thirty-five for a letter she needed, to start living as herself, and I didn't turn her away. That's all I ever did. She's been mine since." She turned her cup round on the table. "The state's reissuing every driver's licence with the sex you were born with. Hers came yesterday. Fourteen years it said F, and now it says M, and the county's records still say F. The county's computer checks its people against the state's."

"What will they do?"

"Look at her," said Dolores. "Twenty-three years she's worked for the county, and she's spent every one of them being a file nobody had a reason to open."

---

The county's placement office was in the old Job Center by the river, inside the fence now. Ruth came to it on Monday morning straight from the hospital, in her good coat, buttoned to the neck. She hadn't said a word in the car.

Carol was behind the counter, with a pen clipped to the neck of her cardigan. She was quicker than Anna remembered, and she looked as if she hadn't slept.

"Anna," she said. "Dolores's steps, the first spring of the virus. You brought soup." She took in the baby, and Ruth, and Gary Pruitt with his folder. "I'm sorry it's here."

Anna could read a screen upside down, which she had learned at a dentist's front desk. A yellow banner ran across the top of Carol's: *Identity record under review*. Carol didn't look at it once.

She opened the form. *Application for Sanctuary Placement.* Eleven sections.

"Ma'am, can you tell me your name?"

Anna started to answer, and Carol stopped her, kindly. "This one says *as stated by applicant*. Mrs. Dorsey?"

Ruth's hand was tight on her purse. "You're the county," she said.

"I am. Can you tell me your name?"

"Mrs. Walter Dorsey." Ruth looked at the window, at the door, at Pruitt's folder. "I don't know who gave you that. I didn't sign anything."

"There's a box here that says *known as*," said Carol. "It's optional. Hardly anybody fills it in. What do people call you, when they like you?"

Ruth was quiet so long that Anna thought she hadn't heard.

"My pupils call me Mrs. Dorsey," she said. "My husband calls me Ruthie. He's not to be encouraged."

Carol typed *Mrs. Dorsey*.

While she worked, Ruth's fingers had begun to move on the edge of the counter, as they did on any flat surface now: the same few bars, over and over, stopping in the same place. Carol tapped the rhythm back with one fingernail, and hummed the next bar under her breath.

Ruth's hand stopped. She reached across and took Carol's hand in both of hers, turned it over, and spread the fingers wide, as she had measured every new pupil's hand in her front room.

"You've got a pianist's hands," she said. "A tenth, easily. Who told you to stop?"

"Stop what?"

"Playing. Nobody's given hands like these to carry groceries."

Carol began to laugh and didn't quite manage it. "We weren't piano people. Old German Baptist, up north of here. We sang, but no instruments, not in the house and not in church. Nobody's ever asked me that." She looked at her own hand lying in Ruth's. "I'd have been good, wouldn't I?"

"You'd have been a nuisance, dear. The good ones are." Ruth let go.

"I know that one you were playing," Carol said, after a moment. "Methodist hymnal, number five thirty-four." She didn't say any more, and went back to the form.

Section six was diet. The District's food came on trays, from a company in Indiana that also fed the state's prisons.

"She won't eat off a tray," said Anna. "She thinks people put things in the food. She only eats what she hears being opened."

"A medical diet takes the doctor, and he's in on Thursdays," said Carol. "A religious diet takes a check mark. What is she?"

"Lutheran. She's played the organ at her church most of her life."

"Lutherans eat anything. That's no help to anybody." Carol scrolled. "Kosher comes sealed. Two wrappers, opened in front of you. You can hear it crack."

The county's lawyers had decided long ago that a form couldn't test what a person believed, so a check mark was a statement, like a name. At twenty past ten on a Monday morning, Ruth Dorsey, who had played the organ for the Lutherans most of her life, became Jewish, for the wrappers.

"Carol," said Pruitt.

"She's Jewish until somebody checks, Gary. Nobody has ever checked."

Section nine came already filled in: *Community resources offered and declined.*

"Nobody offered her anything," said Anna. "A sign went up on a door." She made herself say the rest. "Finland won't let my mother come unless there's no treatment for her here. It may be years before I can even ask. But when I can, I'll need a paper from the county that says so."

"There's a dropdown," said Carol. "The third choice says *none available*. I've never seen anybody pick it. It goes to a supervisor, and she opens it on a Friday and looks at who filed it." She glanced up at the yellow banner, for the first time, and then moved the choice down two lines and saved it.

The printer under the counter pushed out a page. Carol initialled it, folded it in three, and slid it across to Anna. Under the county's seal it said that the community resources available to Ruth Dorsey were none.

"That's your copy," she said. "The county keeps the other one."

Section four was consent.

"Did she ever write anything down?" said Carol.

"It's at the house," said Anna. "In the piano bench." It came out flat, as if it were somebody else's.

"Then we can wait for it. She stays in the infirmary here, and on Thursday the doctor looks at her, and Gary does, and she goes in on your word." Carol turned the signature pad round on the counter. "Or she signs."

Ruth had heard the word *doctor*. Her face sharpened.

"Is this the report?" Nobody answered quickly enough, and she went on, fast and low. "If I put my name here, she stays home. That's the arrangement, isn't it? She's fed. She's clean. She goes to school with a lunch I made her. You don't need to take her anywhere. You can take me instead." She held out her hand for the pen. "Put down that I came quietly."

Carol looked at the hand for a long moment.

"I know what she thinks she's buying," she said.

"Carol," said Pruitt.

"If we wait for Thursday, a doctor writes down that she can't speak for herself, and from then on everybody speaks for her." Carol didn't raise her voice. "She's sitting here asking for a pen, Gary. I'm not going to be the one who tells her she can't have it."

Anna said nothing. If her mother signed, her mother would have done it, and Anna wouldn't have put her anywhere. She stood at the end of the counter and willed Carol to take it, and knew she was willing it. Carol looked at her once, and Anna had the feeling of being read to the end, like a form.

"She can say no to the shot in there," said Carol. "She's said it to Dolores all winter."

"It'll be different in there," said Anna, who didn't believe it.

Carol held out the pen to Ruth. "Right there, Mrs. Dorsey. By the X."

Ruth signed. Under her name there was a box: *Intake officer attests that the applicant understands the nature of voluntary residence.* Carol ticked it with her own pen, and wrote her initials beside it, small and neat.

At the inner door, as far as Anna was allowed to go, a woman in a hairnet was waiting, with a ration card of her own on a cord round her neck. Her name was Lucille Odom, and she had lived inside since the winter. She met the new ones at the door, because when she came in nobody had met her.

"That your card, baby? They'll have that off you by supper. Hold still. Let me see your neck."

"Are you the new pupil?" said Ruth.

"Yes, ma'am, I am. Mind that step. It's bad all the way up."

Lucille bent and took the lace out of her own left shoe, threaded it through the hole in the ration card, and tied it round Ruth's neck, inside her collar, with a quick hard knot.

"Apron strings," she said to Anna, without being asked. "Twenty-six years I cooked for four hundred children a day, at the school on my street. Then they shut the school, and the rent didn't shut." She tucked the card in under Ruth's collar. "Now I eat off a tray from Indiana. One of these days they're going to let me at a stove in here, and you'll smell it from the river."

For the rest of that day she walked with one shoe loose.

---

On Tuesday Dolores came with a cooler bag and a prescription, and they brought Ruth to the little room behind the visitors' window.

"Ruthie," said Dolores. "It's me. Give me your arm."

Ruth put her left arm behind her back. "You're the one who brings the poison."

Dolores asked three times, in the voice she used on frightened people with their sleeves down. Then she put the syringe away and came out to the car park, where Anna was walking the baby up and down beside the fence.

"She's a resident," said Dolores. "She said no. That's the end of it, unless."

Pruitt came out behind her. The paper could still be used, he said. The doctor would see Ruth on Thursday, and he would, and they would write down that she couldn't decide for herself. He didn't hurry the rest.

"On Monday Carol put her initials to it that your mother understood what she signed. On Thursday the doctor and I would put ours to it that she can't. Same week, Mrs. Novak. Same file." He looked at the fence. "Only one of those can be true."

---

That night, in the front room, Anna lifted the seat of the piano bench.

Under the hymnal was the envelope from the bank: the state's form, signed and stamped. On its last page was her own signature, as the person who would decide. She had signed that page every three years since they left, in front of a notary at the American embassy in Helsinki, and posted it back. On the front were the words she had carried in her head for nearly seven years. She knew where the commas were.

She turned it over. The back was written to the bottom in red ink, the ink her mother used for correcting other people's forms: three short pieces, each dated, the old ones copied out again every time she signed.

*August 2020. Annie. If you are reading the back, you have done what the front says, or you are about to, and you feel like a traitor. You are not one. You are going to have to do something terrible. I would like it in writing that I said you could.*

*August 2023. The slow hymn I play you on Sundays is called Finlandia. "Be Still, My Soul." Sibelius wrote the tune when Finland belonged to Russia and wasn't allowed to say so. I looked it up. I play it to the end when I'm well. If I ever stop in the middle, you will know before your father tells you.*

*August 2026. Your father's good leg is going, and he will tell you it isn't. Whatever you have had to do to me, don't let him tell you it was wrong. He let me stop once, because he loved the way I played. You love me some other way. That's why it's your name.*

It was the second one that undid her. She had heard that hymn once in a school gym in Helsinki, with paper flags on the wall bars, and had not known it. Every Sunday since they left, her mother had played it to her down the line at suppertime, and Anna had listened to it the way she had always listened to her mother's playing: for how her mother was. She had never listened for what it said. *Be still, my soul.* Her mother had been sending it across the ocean every week, and Anna had been checking it for wrong notes.

She sat on the bench with the paper on her knees until the street lights went off.

---

On Wednesday Dolores asked her to supper. Carol was there, and Joel, home from a weekend's drill with his hair cut to nothing, and the two of them argued across the table about the last spring roll as if it were the only thing in Ohio worth arguing about. Joel won, by eating it. Carol called him a heathen and laughed until she had to put her fork down.

After supper Carol took Alma without asking and walked her up and down Dolores's front room, singing to her under her breath, slow and plain, with no tune Anna knew. Alma, who took nothing from strangers, went to sleep.

"Meetinghouse," said Carol, when she noticed Anna watching. "A whole meetinghouse and no piano. You'd be surprised what a room can do with nothing." She turned at the end of the room and came back. "My sister Naomi's still up there, on the farm. She can't say the name I chose, and I asked her never to say the other one, so she manages with neither. You'd be surprised what a person can do with nothing, too."

They sat on Dolores's steps afterward, where Anna had first met her, seven years before.

"Gary rang me," said Carol. "He tells people what he's written."

"If I use it—"

"Then on Friday there's a doctor's paper in that file beside my initials, and that's my job." Carol said it plainly, as a fact for the file. "I'm not telling you so you won't. I'm telling you so you know."

"Will you tell them she understood?"

"No. She didn't. I knew it when I ticked the box." Carol turned the pen over in her fingers. "I took her word because it was hers, and I'd take it again, and I'll tell whoever asks me that."

"Then what do I do?"

Carol looked at her for a while and didn't answer. Then she stood up and put the pen back in her collar.

"Give me that baby back before you go," she said. "I've got about four more minutes of her in me."

---

Anna sat in her father's car outside the dark house with the engine off and Alma asleep in the seat behind her.

If she did nothing, nothing would happen to anybody. Her mother would keep her arm behind her back and eat out of sealed packets, a free woman in a room by the river, and tap the first bars of the hymn on a table and stop. Carol would keep her counter, until the morning somebody got round to the M.

She saw a bench by a hangar at the edge of a mown field, and a grey plane going up beyond the trees, and her father not looking at her. She saw his shoulders come down when she said *I promise*. It was the only thing he had ever asked her for.

She saw Carol at Dolores's table with her fork down, laughing at her son. She saw two small neat letters beside a ticked box, and twenty-three years at the county behind them.

She went into the house and took the envelope out of her bag, where it had been since Tuesday night, and put it back in the piano bench, under the hymnal, and closed the lid. She sat on the bench in the dark. It was very quiet. It was the quietest she had ever known that room.

Then she lifted the lid again and took the envelope out, knowing whose job she had just ended, and whose word she had just broken, and went up to bed.

---

On Thursday morning the District's doctor examined Ruth in the infirmary, and then Gary Pruitt did, separately, as the law said, and each wrote down that she did not have the capacity to make decisions about her own treatment. Anna signed under them as the person named. The District gave her a form that made her the responsible party. She could sign her mother out to any address, on the day her mother was well enough to go and there was somewhere to go to. Today there was neither. She was also the one who had signed her in.

Ruth wouldn't give the arm. She said Anna wasn't her daughter. She said the injection was poison. She said everything the front of the paper had said she would.

An aide held her, gently, the way you hold someone who is going to hate you for it. Anna held her mother's right hand and read the paper to her, all of the front, in a voice that kept wanting to go and didn't. *Do not listen to me. Listen to this.*

Dolores took the left arm.

"This one's mine," she said. "You told me so yourself."

It was done before Alma, asleep on Anna's chest, so much as stirred. It would be weeks before anybody knew how well it would work this time. Ruth had known that, too, in a good year, at the bank.

---

On Friday the supervisor opened the file.

Carol rang that evening. Four things had been on one desk at once, she said, and any one of them alone would have got her a talking-to. A woman recorded as able to sign on Monday and unable on Thursday. A Lutheran on a kosher tray. *None available*. And her own record: F in the county's files, M on the licence the state had just sent her, which she hadn't asked for and couldn't change.

They had let her go. The four things were the letter of it.

"I knew on Monday," said Carol. "When I picked up the pen."

"I'm sorry," said Anna.

"I know you are." A pause. "Your none-available paper stays in the file. I checked."

---

On Saturday Anna drove her father's car out to Kettering, the next town over, where her father had gone from the hospital to a nursing home to learn to stand on the pin in his hip. She told him all of it: her mother's signature and Carol's initials, Dolores and the arm, the paper from the bench, and her own name under the doctor's.

Walt turned his face to the window and looked at the parking lot. She didn't ask him for anything, and he didn't give it.

An aide came in, a girl not much older than Caleb Henderson, and filled his water jug, and clipped the call button to his blanket where his hand would find it. "Okay, Mr. D.," she said, as if the two of them had agreed something, and went out again.

"How's she eating?" he said at last.

"Out of sealed packets, Dad. They think she's Jewish."

He laughed before he could stop himself, and then stopped, and didn't look round.

"She wrote your name," said Walt. "Not mine."

His hand came across the blanket without his looking, and found hers, and held on hard enough to hurt. He went on looking at the parking lot. He held on while a car pulled out below and another took its place, and when he let go, he wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist, quickly, like a man who has never been caught at it.

Anna flew home the next morning.

---

The Montgomery County Sanctuary District had a piano.

A church had given it, an upright with two dead keys, and it stood against the wall of the day room. It was kept locked, because a piano was equipment, and equipment was locked. Using a piece of equipment for its intended purpose required a signed request, reviewed on Mondays. Using the top of it to fold laundry required nothing, and so for its first two years in the District that was what it was for.

In June a request came in on the county's form, with two of the form's own commas corrected in red. Under *Purpose of use*, it said *Lessons*. Under *Number of participants*, it said *As many as come*.

It was approved on the second Monday, because nobody could find a rule against it.

After that, on Tuesday and Thursday afternoons, there were children round the piano in the day room, whose families had nowhere else to be. On the bench sat a woman of seventy-one with a ration card on a shoelace round her neck, keeping her left hand moving so that nobody would see it shake. The children played the twinkling one that every child learns, with one finger, getting most of it.

"Again," said Ruth.
