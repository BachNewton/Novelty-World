# Chapter 16: Counted

Alma Novak was eighteen the spring Finland decided to count its Augments, and she went on running before breakfast as if it hadn't.

She ran at five, when it was already light and the shore path below her old daycare was empty. She had run it every morning since she was twelve, and she knew it with her feet: the loose board on the little bridge, the place where the rocks came in close, the bench where the path met the road. She saw it the way she saw everything, as brightness and edges. The sea was a wide pale sheet on her right, with the darker line of the islands across it. A gull was a grey mark that moved. A person was a shape, coming or going, and until it was a few steps away it had no face.

When the path was empty she ran as fast as she could. Nobody had ever seen how fast that was. She listened as she went, for footsteps, or the jingle of a dog's collar, and when she heard one she dropped to the pace of an ordinary girl out for a jog, breathing harder than she needed to, and kept it up until they had passed.

She had not been ill since she was eight. Every February, when half her class went down with the flu, she stayed home for a day and drank tea at the window, and coughed when her friend Venla rang. In the autumn she had cut her thumb on a tin in the kitchen, and by morning it had closed to a pink line. She wore a plaster over it for a week, changing it every day for one just like it. In gym she came fourth. She had worked out at eleven that fourth was the place nobody remembered. Being an Augment, for Alma, was mostly this: a careful habit of being a little less, kept up all day, like holding in your stomach.

It had not given her back her eyes. The network had mended what was failing in her at eight, and nothing that was already gone. She read with the page an inch from her face, or with her phone's voice in her ear. She couldn't read the number on a bus until it had stopped in front of her, and she had not seen her mother's face clearly since she was six, except from as close as a kiss. Everything else came quickly. She had learned her grandmother's minuet in a week, and read braille faster than the woman who taught her, and lately, at the piano, she sometimes had to slow her hands down so that her ears could keep up.

The piano was hers now. Ruth had died three winters before, at eighty-seven, in her own bed and in a good year, with her tablets in their foil on the table beside her and her left arm still Dolores's. Nobody had ever found Dolores. The commissions in America had heard from a great many men who wanted to go home, and not one of them had said a word about a nurse on the road to a pharmacy. Alma still sat at the treble end of the bench, out of habit, and left the bass end empty. On Sundays at six, the hour Ruth had kept, she gave Carol Flory her piano lesson down the phone to Toronto, because somebody had to. Carol said Alma was stricter than her grandmother, which from Carol was the highest praise there was.

---

Alma had known what she was since she was eight, and for most of those years the word had belonged to other people. It was the word Elias had called Minea at school because she won every race. It was the word on the news, about countries that changed children before they were born and countries that kept lists of anybody who had been changed.

Then, the year she was fifteen, she heard a laugh on the television.

She was in the kitchen, and her father had the news on in the next room, and a crowd was roaring somewhere hot, and over the top of it came a laugh, a big one, with the speaker's head thrown back. Alma knew it before the commentator said his name. She had last heard it in a lounge in Toronto, over the bass of the twinkling one played wrong on purpose.

"There are enough of us now," said Khan, to the crowd, in the voice that had told her he'd never been anything else, so he couldn't say what it was like. "Nobody here has to ask anybody's permission for anything."

The crowd roared again. The commentator said that he now ruled more than a quarter of the world, from South Asia to the Middle East, where Augments governed and nobody else had a vote, and that the countries around his were at war with him, and some of them with each other. Her father turned the television off and came and stood in the kitchen doorway, and neither of them said anything about it, then or afterward.

People had started calling it the Eugenics Wars. Nobody in Finland had been shot at yet. The sirens were tested at noon on the first Monday of every month, as they always had been, and since the winter nobody laughed at them.

Finland's law came that winter. Every Augment living in the country was to come in by the last day of May and be entered on a register. Anybody who came in by then and told everything, where and when and who, would not be prosecuted in Finland for how they had come to be, and nor would the parents who had signed for them. After that day the register's office could search everybody's health records, and hiding an Augment from it was a crime. The register would be shared with the countries Finland stood with. Any child born to a registered Augment would be entered at birth.

The minister guessed there were a few hundred Augments in Finland, and said on the news that the register would show the Finns at last what they were dealing with. Across the city from his office, one of the few hundred was practising a minuet with the window open.

---

Joy had written to Alma on every birthday since Toronto. The cards were in braille, which Joy had learned for the purpose, and they had mistakes in them that Alma had never corrected. For the first two years they came with Canadian stamps. For eight years after that they came with stamps from somewhere else, and no return address.

The letter that came on a Thursday in May wasn't a card. It came to her phone, from a number she had to look up, and her phone read it to her in its flat kind voice, at her desk, with the door shut.

*Alma. When Canada started its own register, Khan and the others came here, to his country, and I came with them. I nursed them from when they were small. I can't stop now because they're grown. In Canada one of them was taken off a train at the border because his blood came back wrong, and held nine days in a room with no window. Here nobody takes them off trains. That's all I ask of a country now.*

*The clinic closed the winter we left. Some of the families followed us here. Most stayed home and said what your mother says.*

*There is a way for you. A train through two countries that haven't taken a side in the war, and a plane from the second. If you want it, answer by the twentieth and somebody will tell you the rest. They would take you. Not your family. I asked.*

*I won't tell you it's good here. There have been no massacres; I looked, because I'm my mother's daughter and I would have gone looking. People like you decide things. The others don't, and nobody votes, and everybody works for him one way or another. You would too.*

*But you'd never hide again. There's a track by the river here that stays lit all night, and people like you run on it as fast as they can, and nobody looks twice. You'd be in a room for the first time in your life where you weren't the only one.*

*And the doctors here can do more for eyes than they could in Toronto. I won't pretend I didn't put that in on purpose.*

*He says to tell you he's thought of a better answer, and he'll give it to you if you come.*

*Joy*

Alma played the part about the track three times. Then she sat with her hands flat on the desk and tried, as she had at intervals all her life, to remember her mother's face from before she was six. What she had was a mouth, and a laugh, and a way of tucking hair behind one ear. The rest was a brightness at a kiss's distance.

She knew what she would have to do to see it. She would have to go somewhere her mother couldn't follow, and look back.

---

On Sunday at six she propped her phone on the music rest, and Carol came on in Toronto, at eleven in the morning, in the flat she had kept since Joy went, with Joy's winter coats still in the closet.

"Scale," said Alma. "Both hands."

Carol played it. She was sixty-seven, and she had been learning for thirteen years, and she was very slightly better than when she started.

"Your fourth finger's asleep," said Alma. "Again."

At the end Carol put her hands in her lap.

"Joy wrote to me too," she said. "She said she'd written to you."

"The form asks who was there," said Alma. "On the register. Where, and when, and who signed for me, and who else was in the room." She had read all eleven pages with her nose against the screen. "If I put Toronto, they tell Canada. If I don't put Toronto, it's a lie, and the hospital here has it in my file anyway. *Treated in a trial in Canada.* Mom told them that."

"I know what it asks. I read it before you did."

"I could leave you off."

"And they'd find me on the Institute's staff list in an afternoon, and you'd have lied on a government form for nothing." Carol's voice didn't change. "I said *no* to an inspector so that you could be on nobody's list. That was mine, and I knew what it cost when I said it. If you put yourself on one, that's yours. Don't you put yourself on it for my sake, and don't you keep off it for my sake."

"They could send you to prison."

"They could." Carol was quiet for a while. "And I'll tell you something for nothing, since we're being honest. I don't want to go. I'm sixty-seven. I've got a bad hip and a hymn I can nearly play, and a lesson on Sundays. I'd like that on the record too." She took a breath. "But I crossed one bridge running, with my life in a bag, and I'm not crossing another."

Alma didn't say anything.

"When I changed my name," said Carol, "the law made me print both of them in the newspaper, side by side, so anybody could find them. They found them, when they wanted to. That page went everywhere my son's face went. The worst of it wasn't being found. It was being found under the wrong name." She put her hands back on the keys. "So if you write me down, you spell it right. I've been printed wrong. Right is better, even on a charge sheet."

"Carol."

"Again," said Carol. "The scale. I've got ten minutes, and then I'm going to eat something."

---

Nora came for supper, as she did on Sundays. She was twenty-six now, a physicist at the university, working on something about the shape of empty space that she could explain to Alma and to nobody else. Theo had come in from the coast on the evening train, smelling of the sea. He was twenty-three, and he counted what lived in the Baltic for the university: weed, mussels, fish, and the green summers of algae that came a little earlier every year. He had a letter from the army beside his plate. He had done his service in the navy at nineteen, and now he was called back for two weeks in June, to be reminded of it.

"You'd be better at it than me," he told Alma.

"They won't let me."

"That's the law's loss," said Theo, and meant it.

Alma told them about Joy's letter, all of it, the track too. Nora put her fork down.

Ten years before, at this table, Nora had wanted to tell everybody. She had kept the secret anyway, every day of the ten years.

"I wanted it said," said Nora. "I wanted you on the front of a newspaper with your times from the running track under you. I didn't want it written down by them." She looked at her hands. "And I'll tell you something worse. Since the law came in I've lain awake wanting you to register, because then I could stop lying to everybody I work with. That's me. That's not you. I've wanted this for ten years, and now it's here I'd give anything for you to go back to being nobody's business."

"I can't go back to being nobody's business."

"I know."

"We count the cod," said Theo. "In this sea. Every year since before I was born. It's the only reason there are any left: somebody counted them and saw how few there were, and they stopped the boats." He took some more potatoes. "And before that, the counting's how everybody knew how many there were to catch." He looked up. "It's the same count. It depends who's holding it."

"That's not helpful, Teo," said Nora.

"I didn't say it was helpful. I said it was the same count." He thought for a moment. "Which one could you do every day? Not the day you do it. Every day after."

---

On Wednesday Alma came into the kitchen late and found her parents at the sink.

Her mother still dried every plate with a towel before she put it in the cupboard over the sink, though the cupboard had a rack in it built to drip them dry. She had learned it from Ruth. Her father was holding the other end of the towel, more or less.

He started before Alma had asked anything, as if he had been waiting all week with it.

"Stay," said Ben. "Stay here and don't go in. Nobody's found you in ten years. They won't search everybody's records the week after; they'll do it slowly, and badly, the way governments do everything. There's an election in four years. The people who wrote this law won't be there forever." He put the plate down. "But a register will. That's what I know about records, Alma. They outlive whoever made them. Somebody keeps them, and keeps them, and one day somebody else needs them for something nobody had thought of yet."

"There'll be other registers. Every country you can get to by train has one, or it's writing one."

"Then you're no worse off here than anywhere." He didn't let her answer. "And it's not only you. If you never say Toronto, Carol's never in it. Nor are the families who went there after us and said *a trial*, like your mother. You go in that office, and the first thing anybody in Canada learns about that building comes from you." He was speaking faster than she had ever heard him. "If they find you, we're the ones who answer for it, and we will. I've been a criminal since I signed my name in Toronto. I've got used to it."

"Mom hasn't got used to it," said Alma.

Anna went on drying.

"The woman on the second floor still asks me how the little one is," she said. "Every few months, on the stairs. She's asked me for ten years. And I tell her *a trial, in Canada, we were lucky*, and she says what a miracle, and I go on up." She put the plate away. "I'm very good at it now. I've had a lot of practice."

"Would you stop, if I registered?"

"I'd stop. I'd have nothing left to lie about."

"And if I go east?"

Anna turned round with the towel. Alma couldn't see her face from across the kitchen, only the shape of her against the window, and how still the shape was.

"I decided for my mother once, with a paper she wrote," said Anna. "I decided for you once, with a pen. I'm not doing it a third time." Her voice was steady until it wasn't. "I'll tell you one thing, and then I'm going to bed. Whatever you choose, I signed that form knowing it was wrong, and I'd sign it again tomorrow, and if anybody ever asks me, I'll say so. In any country."

When she had gone, Ben stood at the sink with the towel.

"She means it," he said. "I mean mine." He folded the towel in half, and in half again. "I watched my grandmother take me to an eye doctor every August until I was twelve, because of a thing nobody would write down. I know what it costs not to say. I'd still rather pay that than watch them write you down."

---

On the last Sunday in May, when the house was asleep, Alma sat down at the piano and didn't play it.

She lifted the seat of the bench instead. Under her grandmother's music there was a card on a shoelace.

She held it up to her eye, close. It was stiff and yellowed and had a photograph on it of a woman with white hair, too faded for Alma to make out. In one corner, in a box, there was a letter, large enough for her to read. *D.* The lace had been somebody's in Dayton, once, out of a shoe. Ruth had worn the card round her neck for more than three years behind a fence, and kept it for the rest of her life, and never said why.

Alma sat with it in her hand, at the treble end of the bench, and went through it again, the way she went through a piece before she played it for anybody.

She could go east. She would see her mother's face, or a doctor would try. She would run on a lit track at midnight as fast as she could go, beside people who could go as fast, and nobody would look twice, and Joy would be there, who had learned braille to write to her and made mistakes in it. She would never cough at a window again for a flu she didn't have. And every day after, she would be one of the people who decided things, in a country where everybody else did as they were told and nobody had asked them. She had been asked. At eight, in her own bed, with a rabbit under her chin. It was the one thing she had that Khan and the others never did, and she could not see how to take it east and keep it.

She could stay, and hide, and her father might be right. She would come fourth every year until she was forty, and change a plaster on a cut that wasn't there. Carol would never hear from anybody in Canada about Toronto, and nor would the families who had followed them there. And her parents would go on, after the last day of May, as criminals in the country that had taken them in, waiting for a letter, because of her. Her own file was the easy one. A girl given a few years to live at six, and well at eight, after a trial in Canada: she would not be at the bottom of anybody's search.

Or she could go in, and be counted. Carol would be on a list in Canada, put there by Alma's own voice. Every child Alma ever had would be written down at birth, before it could be asked anything, and might one day hold that against her. And her father was right about that too: one day somebody would want the register for something nobody had thought of yet.

She held the card closer, until the *D* filled her eye.

Somebody had counted her grandmother, at a counter, with a letter of their own choosing. If Alma hid, they would count her anyway, one day: a line in a hospital file, a recovery that made no sense, a knock. They would write her down as something they had found. The minister wanted the Finns to see what they were dealing with. If they were going to look, she would rather they looked at her, now, by her own doing: a girl who came fourth, and couldn't read the number on a bus, and taught a woman of sixty-seven the piano on Sundays, and had a name.

It didn't make any of the rest of it go away. She knew that. She put the card back under the music, and closed the bench, and went to bed, and lay awake until it was light enough to run, and didn't.

---

On Monday morning Nora walked with her to the police building, where the register had an office on the ground floor. They went the whole way without saying much. The door was glass and heavy, and Nora got to it first and held it.

Inside there was a machine that gave out paper tickets with numbers on them, as there is in most offices in Finland. Alma held hers an inch from her eye. It said 214. A screen on the wall would call it, and she would not be able to read the screen, so Nora watched it for her.

There were five other people in the room. A woman sat with a baby asleep on her knee, and kept one hand on its back, flat, the whole time, as if it might be taken off her. Alma watched the baby, as well as she could, until the screen called somebody else's number.

When the number came up, Nora stood with her, and Alma said, "I'll go by myself," and Nora sat down again.

The clerk was a woman about her mother's age, with reading glasses and a careful voice. She went through the form with Alma line by line, in Finnish, and typed what Alma said.

Name. Date of birth. Where the change was made: *The Noonien-Singh Institute, Toronto, Canada.* When: *10 April 2035.* Who consented on her behalf: *Anna Novak. Ben Novak.*

"Was anybody else present?"

Alma opened her mouth, and nothing came.

She could see Carol as plainly as she could see anything: at the keyboard in the flat with Joy's coats in the closet, with a bad hip and a hymn she could nearly play, saying she didn't want to go. *Nobody else* was two words. The clerk would type them, and Carol would have her Sunday lesson next week, and the week after. They would find her on a staff list in an afternoon anyway, Carol had said. Alma didn't know whether that was true, or only something Carol had said so that she could do this.

The clerk waited. She didn't hurry her.

"Carol," said Alma. "C-A-R-O-L." Then the surname, one letter at a time, and she had the clerk read it back, and it was right. That was a woman's name on its way to Canada now, and Alma had put it there, and it was spelled right, and none of that made it any lighter.

There was no line on the form for whether the person being registered had been asked. Alma had looked for one in all eleven pages. When the clerk turned the printed form round for her to sign, Alma took her own pen, put her face down close to the paper, and wrote in the margin, small and neat, *I was asked. I said yes.*

The clerk looked at it, and at her, and didn't say anything. She stapled it to the rest.

A nurse took her blood in a little room at the back, from the crook of her elbow, and pressed a cotton ball on it. When she lifted the cotton to put the plaster on, she stopped. There was nothing there to put it on. The nurse looked at Alma's arm, and then at Alma's face, for a long moment. No stranger had ever looked at her like that, knowing. The nurse put the plaster on anyway, over nothing, and Alma said thank you.

Back at the desk the clerk had one more line.

"The name you're called by," she said. Finnish records have the line, for whichever of your first names you actually go by. "You've only got the one. I can leave it."

"Put it anyway," said Alma.

The clerk typed *Alma*, and printed a receipt with a number on it, longer than the one on the ticket, which would be Alma's in the register for as long as there was one. She held it out. Alma reached for it, and her fingers touched the clerk's, and the clerk snatched her hand back so fast that the receipt fell on the desk between them.

For a moment neither of them moved. Alma couldn't see the clerk's face well enough to read it, but she heard her breathing, quick and shallow, like somebody a strange dog has come too close to. Then the clerk said, "Sorry. I'm sorry," and pushed the receipt the rest of the way across the desk with one finger, and didn't look up again.

Out in the waiting room, Nora stood up.

"Well?"

"I'm on it," said Alma.

On the steps outside she gave Nora the receipt to read, because she couldn't, and Nora read her the number twice, slowly, without improving it at all.

---

Their father was sitting on the bottom stair of their building when they came in, in his work clothes, though he should have been at work. He stood up.

Alma held out the receipt. He took it and read the number aloud, once, holding the paper at arm's length. Then he gave it back, and said the number again, without the paper.

"There," he said. "If anybody ever comes to the door with that, they'll find somebody here who knows it too."

He went up the sixty-four stairs beside her, one at a time, at her pace, though she could have run them, and didn't say anything else until the top.

---

On Tuesday she ran at five. At eight she was going to tell Venla.

The path was empty, and then it wasn't. Near the little bridge she heard a dog's collar, and a man's step behind it, coming toward her round the bend where the rocks came close.

She didn't slow down. For the first time since she was eight she ran past a stranger as fast as she could go, with her arms going and her breath easy, and the board on the bridge banged under her once, and she was gone along the shore.

She couldn't see the man's face, and he could see hers. He had stepped right off the path to let her by, onto the rocks, with the dog pulled in close against his legs, and he stood there watching her all the way along the shore. She let him.
