# Chapter 13: The Settling

Two autumns after the narrow book went to Toronto, Ruth Dorsey was teaching the piano again, in a house that had never held one.

The keyboard was paper. She had drawn it herself on a length of brown paper from the feed store, with a ruler and a carpenter's pencil, two and a half octaves with every black key filled in solid, and taped it along the oilcloth on Naomi's kitchen table. Naomi's church kept no instruments in its houses. Naomi had looked at the paper for a whole evening, and decided at last that an instrument was a thing that made a noise.

Naomi's granddaughter Lydia was nine. She came every afternoon when her chores were done and sat at the table with her white cap pinned to her hair, and played a hymn on the paper in perfect silence, while Ruth sat beside her and listened.

"Your fourth finger's lazy," said Ruth. "Again."

She meant it. Ruth had taught for forty years with her ears, and she had found that if she watched closely enough she could hear a hand on paper too: which finger came late, which note was let go too soon. Lydia had never heard a piano in her life. All she knew of any tune was what Ruth hummed to her. This month she was learning the left hand of a hymn.

Ruth had been well since the spring. She had come to the farm, north of Dayton, in the war, one of nine old people who had nobody, and she had been ill all that first winter and most of the next: awake all night, wiping clean counters, eating only what she heard being opened. Then, toward the end of the second winter, the illness had gone out of her, slowly, as it had a few times in her life before, in the years between: the way floodwater goes down and leaves everything where it was, and muddy.

In April, while she was the one choosing, she had asked Naomi to drive her to a doctor in Troy, the nearest town, and had come home with tablets. Naomi asked the pharmacist to put them up in cards of little foil bubbles, so that every morning Ruth pressed one out herself at the table and heard it break. She would not have the shot. "That arm is Dolores's," she told the doctor, and he wrote down that she declined. Dolores had gone out of the District's gate two summers before to find more of the shot for her, and nobody had seen her since.

---

That was the spring the war ended, on paper. Naomi brought it home from meeting in May, the way she brought everything, in a few plain sentences while she unpinned her bonnet. There would be a count in November, with somebody from each side at every table. The prisoners each side was holding would go home. Anybody who had done anything in the war, for either side, could go free if he told all of it to a commission, in public, under his own name. And the city of Washington, whose people had never had a vote in Congress, and Puerto Rico, whose people had been Americans since 1917 without a vote for President, would become states. Washington's side had never wanted the two new states, because both would vote against it, and it had taken them to keep its own men away from judges. Philadelphia's side had never wanted to let anybody off, and it had taken the amnesty, which covered the men who drowned the pharmacist in Troy, and its own soldiers who had shot Joel on the levee. People called it the Settlement, and then, the way people do, the settling, as if it were a bill.

"So they'll let them all go," said Ruth, "if they say it."

"If they say it," said Naomi.

By a law more than two hundred years old, a new state's star goes on the flag on the Fourth of July after it comes in, so next summer there would be fifty-two. On Carol's old street in Dayton, Dale Hutto still had STAY in his yard, and nobody had told him to take it down.

---

On Sundays, after meeting, the telephone on Naomi's kitchen wall rang, and it was Anna, from Helsinki. Naomi's church allowed a telephone, on the wall, and nothing with a screen. Anna had rung that number every Sunday since the book reached Toronto, and for most of two winters her mother had taken her for somebody from the county. Since the spring it had been Anna. There was no piano to play her anything at the end of the call, so Ruth told her instead what Lydia had got right.

In September Anna read her a letter from Finland.

The permit Finland had given Ruth in the war still stood, and her card was still in a drawer at the Finnish embassy in Washington, waiting for her face and her fingerprints. It had been granted for the war: a mother held behind a fence, in a country fighting itself. The fence had been sold for scrap and the country had signed. Finland would honour its decision until the end of December. After that it would lapse, and anything Anna asked for her mother would be judged as peace judged it.

"So it's now," said Anna, "or I think it's never."

Ruth asked her to read the letter again, all of it, and found a comma in the English that Finland hadn't needed. Then she said she would think about it.

Anna booked a week in October, on her own, and told her mother she wasn't coming to decide anything. She was coming to see her.

---

Carol Flory crossed back into America on a Wednesday, at the same bridge in Detroit she had left by nearly four years before, with one bag, the size a visitor carries.

The American officer read her passport, and then the letter from the commission that had asked her to come, and then the passport again, where the letter beside SEX was not the one she had chosen.

"How long will you be in the United States, ma'am?"

"Eight days," said Carol, and this time it was true.

She sang again now. At Easter, without deciding to, she had opened her mouth in the tenor line and found the note was there. And since the winter she had been learning the piano. At the county's counter, years before, a woman she was signing into the District had taken her hand, spread its fingers, and asked who had told her to stop. Carol had thought about it for nearly five years and then found a teacher, a church organist from Kingston, Jamaica, younger than Joel would have been. She practised in Joy's spare room on a keyboard you plug in, with headphones, so as not to wake Joy after nights. She was fifty-four, and very bad, and it was the most fun she had ever had. All the way down the interstate to Dayton her right hand practised a scale on the steering wheel.

---

The commission sat in a hall of the convention center downtown, where the county had once given Walt Dorsey his vaccine. The chairs were in rows. At a long table sat three commissioners: one named by each side, and a retired judge from Kentucky whom both sides had agreed on, with her reading glasses on a chain. At the back a camera ran for anybody who cared to watch, and a great many people did.

"Do you swear to tell—"

"I'd rather affirm, if it's the same to you," said Carol. She had been raised by people who let their yes be yes and would not swear to anything, and that part of her had never come out.

"It's the same to us," said the judge.

On the Thursday she told them about the District: the letters the county had sorted people under, and the H the Safety Board added later, for people who wouldn't sign its pledge. She told them her son had written the H's in her ledger himself, in his own hand. He would have wanted that said, and she was not going to be the one who didn't say it. She told them he had burned the ledger, and why, and that what was left of it was a book that answered only one question. They asked her for it. She said she would keep it.

On the Friday they came to the levee.

On the commission's list it was one line: *Montgomery County, 14 August 2030, the levee behind the county building, one dead.* Seven men had asked for amnesty for it: the colonel who signed the order, five of the soldiers who fired, and the caseworker who reported him.

The colonel came first. His own side had held him in a jail in Columbus since the spring before, to be tried for it, and he came in his own suit, with a deputy behind his chair. He told it in order, the way a man tells a thing he has told himself many times: the rolls he needed by eight in the morning, and a famous sergeant who had handed him two words in writing that both of them knew were a lie.

"I had a city to hold," he said, "and I needed it to believe an order meant something. If his name bought him that, any name could."

"The commission doesn't require you to say you regret it, Colonel," said the judge. "You may answer or not."

"I know, ma'am." He looked down the room at Carol. "I'd sign it again, on that morning, in that city. I'm sorry for his mother."

He said it the way people are sorry about the weather. Carol had come afraid that she would hate him. She looked back at him and found she had nothing for him at all, and that was worse.

Then the soldiers, one at a time. There had been six. The sixth had been killed on the north side of town that same weekend, when the fighting came. Each of the five had to say what his own hands had done, and most of them said it to the table.

The fourth was Cody Ruhl. Carol knew the name. Joel had talked about him on the phone: the kid who had joined for the college money, who would eat if you sat there until he did. He was twenty-three now, in a suit that belonged to somebody bigger.

"I was in his squad," said Cody. "I was on the levee. I fired."

They were the words her son had said about the gate, to a woman from the Governor's office with a camera waiting in the hall. Carol didn't know whether Cody knew that.

"He walked up the bank ahead of us," said Cody. "There was wheat boiling over at the District, for supper. You could smell it on the wind. And he said, 'She was right. You can smell it from the river.'" He shook his head. "I never knew who he meant."

Carol knew. For years, Lucille Odom had told everybody in the District who would listen that one day they would let her at a stove, and you'd smell it from the river.

Her son had walked up a levee to be shot, and the last thing he said out loud was that a cook had been right about her kitchen.

Gary Pruitt came last. He had broken no law that anybody could name. He had written down what Joel did that night, and handed it in. He had asked for amnesty anyway.

"I don't know if it was a crime," he told the judge. "I didn't want somebody else deciding what I'd written." He read it all out of his spiral notebook, every time in it to the minute. At the end he looked at Carol.

"I told him first," he said. "Before I told them. And afterward I said I'd told them, and he said, 'You did, Gary.'" His hand was shaking. "I thought you'd want to know he said it."

---

In the corridor afterward a young clerk brought her the form. It was one page. Under the line for the levee there were two boxes, *The family does not object* and *The family objects*, and under them a space marked *Statement of the family (optional)*.

Carol read it the way she had read forms all her life, to the bottom first.

"Is there a box for some of them?"

"No, ma'am. It's one act." The clerk was very young, and she said the rest as if she had said it before and had not got used to it. "Every county has one list, all its cases together, and every prisoner from that county is on it. If the family objects, the levee goes to a court. Nobody on Montgomery County's list goes home until the court's done. That's both sides' people, in the jails at Troy and in Columbus. Nine hundred and some." She stopped. "I'm sorry. I'm supposed to say it."

"Has anybody objected?"

"Next county up. A pharmacist's mother, in September." The clerk didn't look away. "Nobody blames her. Troy's still full."

It had to be back by noon on Tuesday.

That evening Carol went to what had been the District, where Lucille Odom still cooked from a stool in the kitchen of Building C, and anybody who walked in got fed. Carol told her what Cody had said. Lucille put down her spoon.

"Well," she said. "I was right." Then she picked the spoon up again and stirred, and Carol sat with her until the pot was done.

Later, on Miss Bea's sofa, Carol rang Joy.

"In the next states over, people are finding out where their dead are," said Joy. "Because some man wants to go home, and he has to tell it first." She was quiet. "Somebody out there knows where my mother went, Carol. That box is the only reason he'll ever say." Then, fast, before Carol could answer: "So don't you dare let them go for my sake. I mean it. Not for me."

---

Anna came up the farm lane on Saturday night in a rented car from the airport at Columbus. She had last been in a room with her mother five years before, in the District, holding her right hand while Dolores took the left.

Ruth was at the oilcloth with a cup of tea. She put it down and looked at Anna.

"You've cut your hair," she said. Then she got up and held on to her, which was not like Ruth at all.

On Sunday, a little before eleven, Joy's car came up the lane, and Carol got out of it.

Naomi was in the yard in her white covering. The sisters had not stood in the same yard for nineteen years. Naomi had never said either of Carol's names, the one she had chosen or the one she had asked never to hear again, and she didn't now. She looked at her sister the way she had looked at the paper keyboard, as if deciding what it was.

"Mother asks if you're eating," she said.

For nineteen years it had come to Carol passed along, never once to her face. This time she could answer it.

"Tell her yes," she said. "Tell her I'm learning the piano."

Naomi took that in.

"I'll tell her you're eating," she said.

Anna came out onto the porch. She and Carol had talked every Sunday for two years, and had not stood in the same place since the spring Carol lost her job for Ruth. Carol held on to her and said nothing, which on the telephone she had never once managed.

Inside, Ruth held out her hand before Carol had her coat off, and took Carol's, and spread the fingers wide on the oilcloth.

"A tenth," she said. "I told you."

"I've been taking lessons."

"Who from?"

"A girl from Kingston who stops me every four bars."

"Good," said Ruth. "Show me."

So Carol sat down at the paper keyboard, in her sister's kitchen, and played the twinkling one every child learns, in silence. Ruth watched her hands.

"Your thumb's late," said Ruth. "Again."

Carol played it again, and her thumb was late again, and Lydia put both hands over her mouth. Carol began to laugh, and couldn't stop. Ruth waited for her to finish, as she had waited for forty years of pupils to stop giggling, and then said "Again," and Carol did it right. In the doorway Naomi stood with a dish towel and watched her sister play an instrument that wasn't one.

---

The hour of the old Sunday call came and went with Carol at the paper keyboard, and nobody needed a tablet for it. They ate at noon. Naomi's kitchen ran on jars, and each lid popped as it came off, and Ruth ate everything.

Lydia passed the beans and looked from one old woman to the other.

"You're Ruth and Grandmother's Naomi," she said. "Like in the Book. Where Ruth goes off to a strange country and gleans in the fields."

"I'm not working in any fields," said Ruth.

Afterward Naomi took the plates and wouldn't be helped, and Lydia went out to the barn, and Anna sat across the oilcloth from her mother.

She had decided on the plane that she would not argue. She told her mother the date in the letter again, and then she said, "It's yours, Mom. I'm not deciding this one. I've done enough of that."

Ruth looked at the paper keyboard.

"Has it got a piano?"

"We'll get one."

"Then it hasn't." She folded her hands. "A real one. Not the kind you plug in."

"A real one."

"She's in the middle of the left hand," said Ruth. "I don't leave a pupil in the middle of the left hand."

"I know you don't."

"Your father's at Woodland." Woodland was the old cemetery on the hill in Dayton, where the Wright brothers are buried. "Dolores saw to it, in the ice, before she went. I've never been."

"We could stop on the way."

"On the way," said Ruth, as if Anna had let something slip. Anna said nothing at all.

"I'll be a stranger," said Ruth. "An old woman who can't read the labels on anything."

"You'll correct them anyway."

"In what?"

"Red," said Anna, and her mother nearly smiled.

"Will they give me my tablets? In the foil?"

"Everything in Finland comes in foil."

"And nobody goes near the left arm."

"Nobody."

Through the window Anna could see Carol and Naomi on the porch steps, not quite side by side.

"You told me Nora was in the hall," said Ruth. "That Sunday, when I took you for the county. That's what she's got of me, and a few Christmases on a screen." She put both hands flat on the oilcloth. "I'd like her to have something else."

"Mom."

"I wrote the last one of these at a bank, in a good year," said Ruth. "This is a good year. I don't know how many more I get. I'd like to spend this one being the one who decides." She looked up. "I'll come."

Anna had crossed an ocean expecting to hear no, and to bear it. She found she had nothing ready for yes. She put her hand over her mother's on the oilcloth, and left it there.

---

There was still the left hand.

When Lydia came back in, Ruth asked Carol to sing the tune of hymn five thirty-four, so that Lydia could hear what her left hand was underneath, and Carol, who had been raised to sing in a meetinghouse with no piano, stood up by the table and sang it. Lydia played the bass on the paper without a sound, all the way through, and Ruth watched every finger and said nothing, which from Ruth was a great deal. At the sink Naomi stood with her back to all of them and didn't turn round until it was over.

Anna, by the window, knew it from the first bar. It was the slow hymn her mother had played her down the line every Sunday for years, the one called *Finlandia*. Once, on the back of a paper in a piano bench, Ruth had written that she played it to the end when she was well, and if she ever stopped in the middle, Anna would know.

It went to the end, in a house with no piano, with nobody playing it.

---

On the porch, before she drove back to Dayton, Carol told her sister about the form.

"If I don't object, they go home," she said. "All of them. The one who signed it, too."

Naomi said nothing.

"You won't tell me."

"We don't go to law."

"I know what we don't do."

Naomi looked out at the lane.

"When I took the wheat into the District," she said, "each side stopped me and took four sacks. That's the fare, I told your boy. We didn't resist it. Twenty got through."

"Joel wasn't a sack of wheat." Carol heard her own voice go up, and let it. "He walked up that bank so those people wouldn't be sorted onto buses. He paid for them once. Now everybody wants the men who shot him to go home free, so the rest can go home, and that's him paying for them again. Nobody asked him the first time either."

Naomi was quiet so long that Carol thought she wouldn't answer.

"No," said Naomi. "He wasn't." Then, to the lane: "I'd not blame you."

They sat on the steps until the light went. Neither of them got up first.

---

On Tuesday, a little before noon, Carol sat at a table in the corridor of the convention center with the form and her own pen, and put the pen on the second box.

She could do it, and be right. The colonel had told a hall full of people he would do it again, and if she ticked the first box he would walk out of a jail in Columbus on Thursday with his coat over his arm, having learned that it cost him a morning in a good suit. Somewhere, some day, there would be another colonel, and he would know from this one that it was free. The pharmacist's mother had looked at the same two boxes and objected, and nobody blamed her, and Carol didn't either. And everybody who needed the first box ticked, the nine hundred and their families, the commission, the whole tired country that had signed, needed her son to buy it for them. He had already bought them the District with his life.

She held the pen there.

If she ticked the box under her pen, five soldiers and a caseworker would stand trial beside the colonel, which nobody she was raised among would ever have asked for, and nine hundred people who had nothing to do with the levee would wait in cells while a court took its time. Joy would wait too, for some man somewhere to want to go home badly enough to tell where her mother was, though Joy had told her not to count that.

The form did not ask her whether she forgave anybody. It asked whether she objected. She had spent her life on forms that asked one thing and meant another, and here was one that meant exactly what it asked.

And whichever she ticked, those seven men had said what they did, in public, under their own names, with a camera at the back of the room. *I fired.* The colonel's *I'd sign it again* was on the record now, and would be when he was dead. Carol knew what it was to have a page printed about you that anybody could find, with the thing on it you couldn't change. She had done nothing, and she had carried that page for nineteen years. They had done something. They would carry theirs.

It didn't make it fair. Nothing on one page could.

She moved the pen up, and ticked the first box.

Under *Statement of the family* she wrote, pressing hard, in the square hand she had used for the county, with one word crossed out and written again:

*I do not object. I do not forgive them either. There is no box for that.*

*Carol Flory, his mother*

She gave it to the clerk, who did not check the name against anything.

---

On the way east that afternoon, Anna stopped at Woodland.

Her father's grave was on the side of the hill, with a small flat stone, and Ruth stood beside it in Walt's cap. Then she bent, with a hand on Anna's arm, and brushed the leaves off his name.

"I'm eating, Walter," she said. "You'd ask."

Then they drove east, toward Washington and the embassy, and the dark came down somewhere past Columbus. Anna turned on the radio for the news, and then remembered, and reached to turn it off.

"Leave it," said Ruth. She had not heard a radio in two years, and once, long ago, one had spoken only to her. She sat with her hands in her lap and listened.

There had been trouble at Mars. The Ares IV, one of the first crewed ships to reach the planet, sent by many countries together, had lost the part of itself that orbited overhead, with its pilot, John Kelly. Nobody could say what had happened to it. The two astronauts on the surface were alive, and alone.

"Your father would have wanted the Mars first," said Ruth.

At a gas station in Pennsylvania the boy at the till told them, as if it were his own news, that a shortstop named Buck Bokai had just broken Joe DiMaggio's record. Ruth told him that was very nice for Mr. Bokai, and the boy was pleased.

Back in the car she asked how you said thank you in Finnish.

"*Kiitos*," said Anna.

Ruth said it, and said it again, getting the first sound wrong and then right, and she went on practising it under her breath through the dark, the way she had practised everything all her life, until she had it.

---

At ten on Wednesday morning, at the Finnish embassy in Washington, a woman behind a counter checked Ruth Dorsey's face against her passport and took her fingerprints, all ten, and handed her a card. Ruth read it twice, in a language she couldn't read, and found nothing to correct. That night she flew to Helsinki with her daughter, to a room with plastic stars on its ceiling.

On Thursday Montgomery County's list was signed off, and the jails at Troy and Columbus let nine hundred and some people go home. Among them was a man of six foot three from Springfield, who had been taken on a school bus two summers before because he looked to somebody as if he came from somewhere else. His name was in the narrow book in Carol's bag, in her son's hand, and she never knew he was among them. In Columbus a colonel walked out of a jail with his coat over his arm.

In none of the counties had anybody yet said a word about a nurse with a cooler bag on the road to a pharmacy. Joy was still waiting.

Carol had waited to see the list signed. Then she drove north, to the bridge at Detroit, and over it. The Canadian officer in the booth took the card that said she could live in Canada for good, and looked at it, and at her.

"How long were you out of the country?"

"Eight days," said Carol.

He handed the card back.

"Welcome home," he said.
