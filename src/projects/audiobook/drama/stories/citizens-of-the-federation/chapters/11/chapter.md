# Chapter 11: The Winter You're Ten

On the fifth of February the school across the street had its flag up again, this time for a poet. Runeberg had written the words of Finland's anthem, and on his birthday every bakery in Helsinki sold little round cakes named after him, with rum in them and a ring of raspberry jam on top. Nora brought two home from school in a paper bag and explained him to her mother, improving him as she went. By the end Runeberg had written the anthem in one night, in the snow, for a bet.

Anna was booking her flight.

On the airline's map America had gone grey in the middle. A year ago the dots had run right across it. Now Chicago was grey, and Columbus, and Cincinnati, and Indianapolis, and Dayton, and every city for hundreds of miles round them, and beside each one in small letters it said *Not currently served*. The nearest the map would take her to Ohio was Washington. She booked Sunday, and Monday night home, two seats.

The second seat was for her mother.

In November a letter had come from Finland's immigration service, three pages of Finnish she could read now without a dictionary. Her mother could come and live in Finland. For six years the reasons had not been serious enough. Now Ruth was behind a fence in a country at war, and they were.

The card that went with the permit was waiting at the Finnish embassy in Washington. They would give it to nobody but Ruth, and only once somebody there had seen her face and taken her fingerprints. The appointment was on Monday the eleventh, at ten in the morning. It was the first the embassy could give her. That winter, everybody in America with a reason to be somewhere else was booking an embassy.

Dolores would bring her. On Saturday evening she would collect Ruth at the District's gate, on the release with Anna's name at the bottom, and the two of them would take the night bus east. The District let a resident out only into the hands of the person responsible for her, or an escort it had on file, and the release named Dolores; and at the checkpoints east, Dolores said, they turned back anybody travelling alone on a District card. The bus went once a week now, on Saturdays, the long way round, by whatever roads were open, and it took a day and a half. Dolores had bought both seats with her own money and wouldn't hear of being paid back.

"I've got money," she said. "I've got nothing left to spend it on."

Nora had moved into Theo's room to make space, after negotiations Theo had written down. In Nora's room the bed was made up with new sheets, under the plastic stars.

Everything was ready except Anna's father.

Walt had been in the nursing home in Kettering, the next town over from Dayton, since his fall nearly three years before, and he and Ruth had not been in a room together since that night. Twice Dolores had driven him to the District in his wheelchair and pushed him up to the visitors' window, and they had talked through the glass for the twenty minutes allowed. The second time, Ruth had told him his collar was wrong. If she went to Finland, he would never see her again, even through glass.

Walt knew it. On Sunday's call, with the aide holding the tablet for him, he had said it before Anna could.

"You get her out of there, Annie. Don't you worry about me. I've got the television." He thought about it. "And the food's terrible. That'll keep me going."

He had more news. The pastor at Ruth's church had gone to his son's in Kentucky, like half the street, and the church was shut. The girl who said "Okay, Mr. D." was doing two shifts back to back, because the other one had gone too. He told it the way he told the weather.

Nora came through the kitchen with Alma behind her. Since New Year she had been answering one question in the house, whenever anybody asked it and mostly when nobody had.

"Eight," she told her sister, and held up eight fingers.

Alma was three. She counted them, got there on the second try, and was delighted.

"Eight days," Nora said, in Finnish, and then again in English for her mother, out of politeness. "Then they're home."

For a moment Anna heard it wrong, and thought she meant her grandmother. Nora meant the Shango, the Europa Mission's ship, and its eight astronauts, who had left for Jupiter on her fifth birthday and were due home the winter she was ten.

---

The ice came to Dayton on Thursday night.

It came as rain, the kind that freezes on whatever it touches, and by morning every tree in the south of the county was cased in it, and the branches were coming down on the lines. In any other winter, crews from three states away would have driven in to put the lines back up. This winter nobody's trucks were crossing a state line.

Carol rang from Toronto on Friday, because Dolores had told her. She had tried to buy a ticket back across at Detroit, for a week, to help.

"They're letting Americans out," she said. "They're not letting this American back in. The man looked at my name and then at my face and said he'd keep my passport if I tried it again." She was quiet a moment. "Tell your mother I've still got the hymn. Five thirty-four. I sing it Wednesdays at a church on Bloor Street and the Canadians think I'm showing off."

The home rang on Saturday afternoon. Anna's suitcase was already packed, standing in the hall by the door.

It was the nurse on duty, who sounded as if she had been on duty since Thursday. Since his good leg had gone, Walt's chest had gone with it, slowly, and he slept with a machine that breathed for him at night. The power had gone at two on Friday morning. The generator ran the hall lights and the kitchen and nothing else, and there was diesel for it until about Sunday. Walt's machine had run on its battery until Friday afternoon. Now he had pneumonia. They had moved everybody into the dining room, toward its one big window, and put coats on them in bed.

"I'd say a day or two," said the nurse. "I've been wrong both ways."

"A hospital—"

"He signed a paper, honey, after he broke his hip. No more hospitals, whatever happens. It's in his file in his own writing." She didn't say the rest unkindly. "And nobody's answering for an ambulance, not in this. I've had to shut the doors. We had folks walking in off the street for the warm. I can't let in family, I'm sorry. If you've got anybody with a nurse's license, I'll let her in and thank God for her." She breathed out. "Do you want to talk to him? He's awake."

The aide held the phone for him. Anna heard him breathe before she heard him speak. Every breath had a catch at the top, like a door that sticks.

"Annie."

"I'm here, Dad."

"You put your mother on that bus." He had to stop. "I'm fine."

He had said it on the porch the summer they left, so that she could go. He had said it about the button on a cord that stayed in its drawer the night he fell. Three years ago, in a dark kitchen in Helsinki, Anna had stopped letting it stand, and she didn't let it stand now.

"You're not fine, Dad."

There was a long breath, with the catch in it.

"Annie," he said again, as if that answered it. Then the aide said she was sorry, he needed to rest, and the line went.

---

Anna stood in the hall beside the suitcase.

Ben had heard. He had Alma on his hip, and he didn't say anything, which she was glad of.

Her mother would be packing her one bag this morning, if she hadn't packed it days ago. At six she would be at the gate in her good coat. Nobody could ring her. The District didn't give its residents phones, and nobody had been able to ring Ruth Dorsey in nearly three years. She knew nothing.

And she was well. That was the part Anna couldn't get past. Her mother had been well for most of three years, teaching children on a locked piano on Tuesdays and Thursdays, and in a few minutes Anna was going to decide her life for her without asking, from the other side of an ocean, as if she weren't. The paper in the piano bench had never given her that.

There was one Dolores. She could be at the gate at six, or at Walt's bed. The bus would leave at nine with Ruth or without her, and nobody at the station would say whether there would be another.

Anna had promised her father once, at the field where the Wright brothers learned to fly, that she wouldn't let them put her mother in one of those places. She had broken it three years ago, to get her mother onto her shot behind that fence. On Monday at ten she could keep it after all. He had just asked her to.

And he had been five once, in a polio ward, with his mother on the other side of the glass.

"He watched for my car," said Ben.

She looked at him.

"The day I went to Toledo. When I came back, he was on the porch, watching for my car." Ben shifted Alma to his other hip. "Nobody ever did that before. I'm only telling you. I don't know what's right."

Anna rang Dolores. Dolores answered on the first ring, and Anna could hear a zip, as if she had been packing a bag.

Anna told her. Dolores listened to all of it without a word.

"I can sit with him," she said, "or I can take your mother. You know I can't do both."

"I know."

"Then you tell me which."

The kitchen clock went on. Through the wall Anna could hear Theo explaining something to Alma, at length.

"Go to Dad," she said.

"All right," said Dolores.

"Will you tell Mom? Before six? She'll be at the gate."

"I'll go by first."

"Tell her it was me," said Anna. "Not you. Not him. Tell her I'm the one who said."

There was a pause from Dayton.

"I'll tell her," said Dolores.

Nora was in the kitchen doorway in her socks. She had been there for some time.

"Is Grandma still coming?"

Anna could have told her it was nothing. She knew how to say that now, in two languages.

"Not on Monday," she said. "Grandpa is very sick, Nora. The doctors think he's going to die, maybe tomorrow. So Dolores is going to stay with him, and Grandma can't come yet."

Nora looked at the suitcase.

"When can she?"

"I don't know."

Nora took that, and went and sat on the floor of the hall beside the suitcase, with her back against the wall, and didn't say anything else for a while.

That evening a message came from Dolores.

*I told her at the gate. She had her bag. She said, Tell him I'm eating. He'll ask.*

The bus left Dayton without them in the middle of the night. Anna was awake to know it.

---

At six on Sunday evening she propped the tablet against the fruit bowl, where it always went for the call, and a minute later it rang.

Dolores's face came first, very close, grey in a poor light, with her coat on. "I've got him," she said, and turned the phone round.

It was a long room with one big window, and every bed in the home had been pushed toward it. There were old people in the beds with their coats on, and wool hats, and the aide in a parka going from bed to bed. In the bed nearest the window was Anna's father, under three blankets, in his cap from the plant. On the table by his head was the machine that had breathed for him at night, with no light on it.

Somebody had combed his hair.

"Hi, Dad," said Anna.

His eyes opened. It took him a while to find the screen.

"There's my girl." His voice was a thread. Then, before anything else: "How's she eating?"

"She told me to tell you she's eating," said Dolores, behind the phone. "She said you'd ask."

Something went across Walt's face that might have been a laugh, if he'd had the breath for it.

"Eating," he said, slowly, to keep it.

The children had come round the table. Theo leaned in first, because he had something to report.

"It's landing in the sea, Grandpa. The Shango. In the actual sea."

"Is that right," said Walt.

Nora leaned in beside her mother and spoke in the careful English she kept for him. "They're coming home on Wednesday, Grandpa. It's the winter I'm ten."

It took his eyes a moment to find her.

"Wednesday," he said.

Then he looked past the screen, up at the woman holding it, and gave her the message he had always given anybody going Anna's way.

"Tell Annie everything's fine."

Dolores had heard that word down a telephone line from the other side of the world for nineteen years, from her own mother, and she knew exactly what it was. She didn't pass it on. She didn't have to. Anna had heard.

She could have said *you're not, Dad*. She had said it yesterday, and it had been true, and it had sent Dolores to him. Today it was the last thing he had to give anybody, and he was giving it to her.

"I know, Dad," said Anna. "I know it is. Everything's fine here too."

He closed his eyes.

---

Dolores held the phone.

She held it for a long time. Walt slept, and his breath went in and out under the blankets, catching at the top, and the aide came and went. Anna talked to him. She hadn't decided to. It was something for her voice to do. She told him that Theo had a loose tooth and wanted paying for it in two currencies. She told him the snow was up to the balcony railing and he'd have hated it. Then she told him that she was going to grow tomatoes this summer, in pots on the balcony, and that he'd have to tell her how, because she had never once paid attention.

After a while the picture began to shake, and Dolores changed arms. She was seventy-four, and she had been up all night on a chair beside him. Later the other arm tired too, and the picture tipped back, and for a few seconds Anna was looking at a ceiling in Kettering: square white tiles, a brown stain in one corner, and a light that wasn't on.

"Sorry," said Dolores, and her father's face came back.

Ben was standing behind Anna's chair. He heard Dolores say sorry, and put his hand on the back of Anna's neck and left it there.

The breathing changed. Dolores could hear it, there in the room, and Anna could see it without being sure of what she saw. The breaths came further apart, and each one seemed to cost more.

Nora had gone to brush her teeth and come back, because she couldn't think of a reason to be anywhere else. She stood at her mother's shoulder in her pyjamas and looked at her grandfather's face in the white light from the window. Then she said something to him in Finnish, which was her own language and not his.

"*Hyvää matkaa*, Grandpa."

Nobody put it into English.

A little after that, there was a long space in Walt's breathing, and then there wasn't any more of it.

Dolores saw it first. She said, "Anna," and that was all. Holding the phone in one hand, she reached down with the other, out of the picture, and Anna saw it come to rest on her father's hand, on top of the blankets, and stay there.

Anna found that she was thinking about his hair: that somebody had combed it for the call, and that he would have wanted to know.

---

Dolores stayed on long enough to say the practical things. The aide would stay with him. The home on the next road had its own generator, and the nurse was ringing them about the others. Dolores would try the funeral homes, and one of them would pick up in the end.

"Will you tell my mother?" said Anna. "I don't want her to hear it from the county."

"Tomorrow, at the window. First thing."

"Tell her the first thing he asked was how she was eating." Anna looked at the cap on the screen. "And will you take her his cap? She'll want something of his that isn't a form."

"I'll take it," said Dolores.

Her battery was going, and she said so, and the call ended. Anna went on holding the tablet up in both hands, at the height she had been holding it for nearly two hours, with nothing on it now but her own face. Ben took it out of her hands.

Theo was in the doorway. He asked his sister something in Finnish, and Nora answered him with one word. Anna understood them both.

Late that night a message came to Ben's phone from Declan, in Belfast.

*Sorry for your trouble, Ben. It's what we say. Tell Anna.*

A minute later: *I'm up, if you're up.*

Ben didn't ring him. He found it helped to know he could.

---

At five o'clock on Monday afternoon, ten in the morning in Washington, the appointment came round on the clock on the oven, and Anna unpacked her suitcase.

She did it carefully, one thing at a time, because it seemed wrong to be doing anything else while it went by. A good coat for an embassy. Her passport, dark red, with the lion. A second toothbrush, still in its packet, which she had bought for her mother. As far as anybody at the embassy would ever know, Ruth Dorsey had not turned up.

At half past five a message came from Dolores, written at the visitors' window, with every comma in it.

*I told your mother first thing. I told her the first thing he asked was how she was eating. She asked me whether he said everything was fine. I said he did. She said, "He would." I gave her the cap. She put it on, over her hair, and said she had children tomorrow, and went back in.*

And under that: *The bus station has a sign in the window. Nothing east until further notice.*

Anna read it twice. Then she put the letters from Finland in the drawer of the hall table, under her passport, and shut the drawer.

---

The Shango had been gone for nearly six years. In that time it had been farther than anything with people in it had ever been, and turned round, and come back on the day it said it would.

It came down on Wednesday in the Pacific, off California, where the plan had always put it. Nobody had to decide which of America's two Presidents it was landing under, because the sea belonged to neither of them, and the ship that went out to fetch it belonged to the company that had built the capsule. Within the hour each President welcomed the crew home on behalf of the United States. Both thanked the many countries that had built the Shango and sent it. Neither mentioned the other.

In Helsinki it was evening, and the whole family was in front of the television. Nora was on the floor, as close to the screen as she was allowed. Theo was beside her, because it was landing in the sea, and the sea was his. Ben had Alma on the sofa. Anna stood in the doorway, where she could leave if she needed to.

A capsule hung under its parachutes over a grey sea, very small, and then not so small, and then it hit the water and sat there rocking, scorched down one side, while boats came at it from three directions. Theo made a noise he would have been embarrassed by if anybody had mentioned it.

A crane on the ship lifted the capsule out of the water and set it on the deck, and people in white suits with masks over their faces went round it with hoses. Then the hatch opened.

They came out one at a time, and none of them walked. Each one was lifted from the hatch by two men and set in a chair, and the chairs were carried down the deck past the cameras, toward a long white box on the far side of the ship with a window in its side.

"Why are they carrying them?" said Theo.

"Their legs forgot," said Nora, who had read about it. "In space nothing's heavy. They have to learn it again."

The crew waved from the chairs. Some of them were crying. One man was laughing so hard that the men carrying him had to stop. Nora counted them under her breath in Finnish. Eight had gone, and eight came home.

The fourth was Renée Picard. Her hair was short now, and she was much thinner than the young Frenchwoman who had waved on her way to the rocket on Nora's fifth birthday. As her chair went past, somebody held out a microphone, and she took it.

"We're home," she said. "You can all go to bed now."

Anna laughed out loud, because her father would have. It came out of her before she knew it was there. Walt had missed the polio vaccine by three years, and been on time for the next one. He had missed this by three days. She put her hand over her mouth and went back into the kitchen.

---

She was still in the kitchen when the rest of it came. Nora brought it, with the remote still in her hand.

"They have to stay in the white box," she said. "For weeks. In case."

"In case of what?"

"Of what they brought." Nora was breathing fast. "They brought something, Mom. Not from Europa." She sounded slightly offended about that. "From Io. It's another moon. It's yellow, and it's all volcanoes. Nobody was even looking there. They brought it home in a box, and it's alive."

In the whole history of the world, nobody had ever found anything alive anywhere but here.

Anna's hand had gone to the phone in her pocket. She had it halfway out before she knew who she had been going to ring, and who would have read every word about it and half believed it, and then she held it a while and put it back.

On the television the Finnish commentator said it, and then said it again in other words, and then a third time. Even in Finnish, Anna could hear her checking. On Ben's phone people in a dozen countries were already frightened of it, and other people were already in love with it, and some were already saying whose it was.

Theo had turned round on the floor.

"Can it live in water?"

"Io hasn't got any water," said Nora. "It's the driest place there is."

Theo thought about that. "Then it doesn't know about the sea," he said. "Somebody should show it." He seemed to feel that this was urgent.

Later they showed Renée again, in her chair behind the window of the white box, talking to a reporter on the deck through a microphone. The reporter asked her what it was.

"We don't know yet," said Renée. "It's very small, and it's alive. The first time we shone a light on it, it moved toward the light. By the tenth time, it didn't bother." She looked at the reporter through the glass. "Until somebody shows me it isn't someone, I'd like us to be careful with it."

---

When the others had gone to bed, Nora came back into the kitchen with a sheet of paper and her pencils, and sat down across the table from her mother.

She drew the white box first, with its window, and eight faces in the window, round, because there were eight. Then, on a table in front of the box, she drew a jar. In the jar she made one mark, very small, and left the rest of it empty.

Then she sat with the pencil over the space under the jar and didn't write anything.

"What's it going to say?" said Anna.

"I don't know which word." Nora frowned at it. "In Finnish you have to pick. *Jokin* is something. *Joku* is somebody. You can't say it without deciding."

Anna looked at the jar for a while.

"Then leave it," she said. "Till somebody knows."

Nora thought that was fair. She left it.

"Grandpa's pictures are still on his fridge," she said. "In Dayton. All of them."

"They are."

"Who's going to look at them?"

"Nobody, for now," said Anna. "One day we'll go and get them."

Nora considered the drawing. "This one's for Grandma, then," she said, and pushed it across the table, and went to bed.

Anna took a photograph of it and sent it to Dolores, to take to the visitors' window in the morning. It was a long way, through more than one country's wires, to a phone in Dayton. A small wheel turned in the middle of the screen, and turned.

Then it went through.
