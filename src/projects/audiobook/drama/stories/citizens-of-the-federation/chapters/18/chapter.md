# Chapter 18: The Sea

Four summers after the war, Theo Novak was still bringing up black mud from the bottom of the gulf.

He brought it up every week from May to October, from the same point on the chart, twenty minutes out from the station past the last of the islands. Henrik ran the winch. The grab went down on its wire, bit, and came up streaming, and when Theo opened it over the tray the smell came out first: rotten eggs, so thick it seemed to have a colour. Then the mud, black as tar. Nothing moved in it. Nothing had moved in it, out there, for as long as Theo had been coming.

Henrik had been coming longer. He was the station's oldest technician, from a village down the coast, and he spoke Swedish to the boat and Finnish to everybody else, as people there had for centuries. He looked at the tray and said what he said every week.

"Still dead."

Theo wrote it down. That was the job.

He knew why it was dead, the way a doctor knows why a man's lungs are bad and has to look at them anyway. The Baltic is nearly shut. The ocean's salt water gets in only round Denmark, only when a great storm shoves it, and it lies on the bottom of the deep places for years with nothing to stir it. For a century the farms and towns round the sea had fed it what washed off their fields, and every summer the top of it bloomed green, and the green died and sank and rotted, and used up the air down there. Where the air ran out, the mud went black and let go of everything it had been holding, and next summer's green fed on that. The sea had learned to make itself sick.

The war had added its own. Somewhere under this boat there were mines, and some of them were Theo's. He had laid them in the last summer of the war, from the stern of a navy ship, in the dark, with his helmet strap cutting into his chin. He did not think about it every week. He thought about it most weeks, when the grab went down, and wondered whether it would come up with something in its teeth that he had put there.

---

The thing from Io lived at the station, in a cold room behind two locked doors.

It lived in glass tanks of the same black mud, under Baltic water, and you could see it only when there was a great deal of it: a faint yellow haze, low down, the colour of Io in the pictures. Io was the most volcanic place anybody knew of, its ground yellow with sulphur, and the thing that Renée Picard had found there lived on sulphur. That was what was in the black mud. That was the smell.

Theo had been seven when she brought it home. He remembered the astronauts carried out of their capsule in chairs on the deck of a ship, and Renée afterward behind the glass of the quarantine, very thin, saying that it moved toward a light the first time and by the tenth time didn't bother. He remembered asking whether it could live in water, and his sister telling him that Io had none.

For twenty-seven years it had been studied behind glass. In Los Angeles a team that everybody called Ricardo's, after the man who ran it, had spent ten of those years learning to grow it by the tonne and keep it alive in water. They had sent their strain to three stations round the Baltic. Theo ran the Finnish trial.

In tank after tank, the yellow haze went down into the black mud and ate the poison out of it. In a week the smell was gone. In a month the mud was brown, the colour of ordinary mud, and had stopped letting go of what it held, and the water over it kept its air. The first time, Theo had stood over the tank with his nose nearly in it, like a dog, and then gone outside and stood on the jetty with his hands in his pockets.

Then it died. The yellow went grey and settled on the brown mud like dust on a shelf, and the next week there was nothing to show it had been there but the mud.

It lived about three weeks in cold salt water. It ate until there was nothing left that it could eat, and after that nothing in an Earth sea could keep it alive. To heal the gulf you would grow it in tanks by the tonne and pour it in, and pour it in again, every summer, for years. If it was someone, it was someone by the trillion, and every one of them would die of the work.

Henrik had done Renée's experiment the first week, because everybody who worked with it did, the way you'd say hello. He shone a light on one side of a dish, and the haze drifted toward it. He shone it again, and again. By the tenth time the haze stayed where it was. Then he left it in the dark for two days, and when he shone the light it went toward it, as if it had forgotten. Last, he tried a red light instead of the white one. It went to the red. It knew a new light from one it had learned to ignore.

"There was a paper before the war," said Henrik. "A slime mould. Yellow stuff that grows on logs. It learned to stop minding a bitter taste, and it could tell one bitter taste from another."

"Did anybody ask whether the slime mould was someone?"

"Not that I heard," said Henrik. "Nobody asks a slime mould."

That was nearly all anybody knew. There was one thing more, and nobody knew what to do with it.

Eleven years before, in a laboratory in Toulouse, somebody had put two dishes of it side by side, joined by a channel of water too narrow for any of it to cross. One dish was shown a light ten times, until it stopped bothering. Then the other dish, which had never seen a light in its life, was shown it once. It didn't bother either.

No slime mould had ever done that. Nobody had managed to make it happen again, in Toulouse or anywhere else, though a great many people had tried. Most of them thought now that it had been a mistake in the dishes.

"Or it only does it when it feels like it," said Henrik, the evening they read the paper again, and went back to his crossword.

---

The countries round the sea had a commission in Helsinki. It would put the organism into the gulf in September, over the dead floor, if all three trials advised it. The woman in Gdańsk had signed in July. The man in Kiel signed in the middle of August. If the Baltic said yes, Ricardo's team meant to take what was learned there to the oceans, and after the oceans, if it could be done, to the air, which over half the world had not been clean since the cities burned in the war. If one trial said no, nothing would happen that year, or the next.

Nothing else would do it in time. The Swedes had once brought one fjord back to life with pumps, before the war; a sea would take the power of a country, and no country had any to spare.

Theo had until Thursday.

Somebody had painted Renée Picard's words in white on the side of the station's boathouse, in English, where the boats could read them coming in. UNTIL SOMEBODY SHOWS ME IT ISN'T SOMEONE. A week later somebody else had added a line under it, smaller. SHE BROUGHT IT HOME, DIDN'T SHE.

Henrik had left them both. He said they were both true.

---

On Sunday Theo took the train into Helsinki, which ran twice a day now, when the power held, and climbed the sixty-four stairs to lunch.

His mother was sixty-eight and his father was seventy, and they still lived in the flat where Theo had grown up, because it had a shelter under it and a piano in it, and because neither of them could imagine leaving the stairs. Lunch was potatoes and herring from the north of the sea, where the water was still alive.

Alma had set the table by feel. The clouding had come the winter after the shelter, as the doctor had said it might, slowly, like breath on a window. She could tell light from dark now, and not much else. She was on a list for the operation that would give her new lenses, which before the war a hospital did a dozen of in a morning. The hospitals did a few a week now. She was a long way down. She still taught the piano at their grandmother's bench, and she still ran at five on the shore path, more slowly, by the feel of it under her feet.

Nora came last, from across the city, with her coat open. She was thirty-eight, a physicist at the university, and for years she had worked on the shape of empty space. Lately a man in Montana had been writing to her about it. He thought space could be bent enough to go somewhere. Nora half believed him.

She sat down across from Theo and looked at him.

"You're going to sign," she said.

"I haven't decided."

"You decided when you were seven."

Anna looked up from the potatoes, decided not to say anything, and passed them.

"Toulouse," said Nora. "You've read it more times than I have."

"Nobody's repeated it."

"Nobody's repeated it in eleven years of trying, with a few grams at a time, in labs that can't get the power on." She put her fork down. "Listen to me. If it can tell another one something across a channel it can't cross, that's not a slime mould. That might be the beginning of a language. And you want to make it a mop."

"I want the gulf to breathe."

"I know you do." Nora leaned forward. "Here's what I'm afraid of, Teo. Once it's the thing that cleans the sea, nobody will ever want to find out. Who's going to pay to learn that the mop minds? Ricardo's lot will breed it for eating fast and dying fast, and in ten years whatever did that in Toulouse will be bred out of it or buried under it, and we'll never know what we had. Wait five years. Let somebody do Toulouse properly."

Theo had known this argument was coming since July. He had had it with her in his head all week, and he had lost it most nights, lying in the station's narrow bed listening to the water against the jetty.

"In five years the eastern cod are a line in a book," he said. "They spawn in one deep, off Bornholm, and three summers out of four it's been dead at the bottom. I've got the count." He didn't raise his voice. He never had; it was Nora who did that. "And it isn't only mine. There are children in the south who've breathed brown since they were born. Five years is a lot of breathing."

"I know."

"Then what do I do with the five years?"

Nora looked at the window, and the strip of pale sky over the roofs.

"If there's ever anybody out there," she said, more quietly, "and one day they come, they'll want to know what we did with the first one. That's all. I'd like us to have an answer we're not ashamed of."

"So would I," said Theo. "I'd also like there to be a sea to show them."

She opened her mouth, and shut it. She had given him his first word, on a ferry, pointing, when he was a year old. She had been explaining the sky to him ever since, and he had been dragging her down to the shore to look at the water, and neither of them had ever got the other one to stop.

"What happens to it?" said Anna. "After the three weeks."

"It dies," said Theo.

His mother nodded. She looked at her hands on the table for a while.

"I've signed for my mother," she said, "and for your sister, when they couldn't." She got up and took the plates. "It doesn't get any lighter for being right."

Alma had been quiet. Now she got up too, and went to the piano, and lifted the seat of the bench. She felt under their grandmother's music, past the card on its shoelace, and came back with a sheet of paper gone soft at the folds, and laid it on the table in front of Theo.

It was a child's drawing. A white box with a window, and eight round faces in the window, and on a table in front of the box, a jar. In the jar there was one small mark. Under the jar, where a label would go, there was nothing.

"Nora gave it to Grandma the week she came from Ohio," said Alma. "She kept it in here."

Nora looked at it. She had been ten. She remembered the pencil held over the space, and the two Finnish words that wouldn't let you write it down without deciding: *jokin*, something, and *joku*, somebody. Nobody had written anything there since.

"When I went up the stairs," said Alma, "Jari said I was built for it. Same stairs either way. The difference was that somebody asked me." She turned her face toward her brother's voice. "You can't ask it."

"No."

"Then whatever you do, don't tell yourself it said yes."

---

Ben walked him down to the street, because he always did, one step at a time with his hand on the rail.

At the bottom he stopped.

"You'll never find out," he said. "Whether you were right. Nobody tells you that part."

Theo looked at him. "Did you? Find out? About whatever it was?"

Ben thought about it, and didn't say what it had been.

"No," he said, and went back up.

---

On Tuesday night, when the station was empty, Theo went into the cold room and did what he had said he would do when he was seven.

He took a little of the haze from a tank and put it in a dish with a long channel down the middle, under the microscope, so that the screen beside it showed a yellow cloud of specks. At one end of the channel he put a drop of water from the floor of the gulf, black, from the place where nothing moved. At the other he put a drop from the top of the sea, from the jetty: clean, cold, with the living green in it. The sea that had made him laugh from a ferry window before he had a single word.

Then he sat and watched.

The haze moved. It moved slowly, all together, like a flock, toward the black drop. It didn't bother with the other end at all.

Of course it did. The black water was full of what it ate. A moth goes to a lamp. It meant exactly what it would have meant if he had put a drop of soup at one end, and Theo knew it, and sat there anyway with his chin on his fist until the cloud reached the black drop and went into it.

He did not tell himself it had said yes.

He did make himself look at the other thing, which he had been walking round all week. Out past the islands, on that black floor, lay round steel things he had rolled off the back of a ship in the dark, and he wanted, more than he had wanted anything since the war, to put something into that water that wasn't a mine. He didn't know how much of what he had said at lunch was the cod and the children, and how much was that. He suspected he would never know. He sat with it, and it didn't get any smaller.

"That's the sea," he told the haze on the screen. "That's the bad part. The rest's better."

He felt foolish, and said it anyway.

---

On Thursday he took the morning train into Helsinki and sat at a table in the commission's office, by a window over the harbour.

The form was four pages. The first three were easy to answer: whether it worked in Baltic water, which it did; whether it could survive and spread beyond where it was put, which it couldn't; whether its dead harmed anything, which they didn't seem to.

The fourth page had been added in the year it came home, and had sat at the back of every form about it since. It asked whether there was evidence that the organism had interests that would be harmed by its use.

He could have written *No evidence*. Most people who filled in that page wrote it. He thought of two dishes in Toulouse, and of his sister saying *a language*, and of a worm that had not come up in his grab for as long as he had worked there.

He wrote: *Unknown. It cannot be asked.*

He had thought that at the end he would feel sure, one way or the other. He didn't. His hand was cold, and he was afraid, the plain way you are afraid before you jump into the sea in May, knowing exactly how cold it will be. He ticked the box that advised release, and signed under it, and sat for a while afterward looking at the harbour before he could make himself get up.

The same morning, in the paper, there was a letter from a hundred and forty scientists asking the commission to wait. Nora's name was the third from the top. Theo read it on the train back to the coast, and rang her from the platform.

"I said I would," said Nora.

"I know you did."

There was a long silence, with the platform announcements going on over Theo's head.

"When do you go out?" she said.

---

They went out on a Tuesday in the middle of September, on a navy ship, because nothing else had the deck or the rails.

It was the ship Theo had served on in the war. At its stern two steel rails ran down to the water, built so that heavy round things could be rolled along them and off the back into the sea. Four summers ago those things had been mines, and Theo had been one of the young men in helmets who rolled them. Now there were tanks bolted along the rails, with hoses going down from them over the stern, and in every tank the yellow haze lay low down, waiting.

Nora stood at the rail with her coat buttoned to the chin. She had caught the five o'clock bus from Helsinki to be there.

Over the point on the chart, the ship slowed and stopped, and lay rocking on the grey water. A woman from the commission read something from a sheet into the wind, and nobody heard it. Then the captain looked at Theo, and Theo opened the first valve.

It went down into the sea in a long yellow ribbon, and spread, and faded, and sank toward the black mud on the bottom, where it would eat for three weeks and die.

"*Hyvää matkaa*," said Theo, under his breath.

Nora heard. She took his arm and held on to it, and they watched the ribbon go until there was nothing to see but water.

"I still think you're wrong," she said.

"I know."

"I'm glad it was you that did it, though." She didn't look at him. "Not somebody who was sure."

---

In June, Theo went out with Henrik to the point on the chart, and the grab went down on its wire, and bit, and came up streaming.

Before he opened it he looked at the sensor on the side of the frame, which had read nothing at the bottom of the gulf every week of his working life. It said there was air down there. Not much. Some.

He opened the grab over the tray. The mud was brown. It smelled of mud. In the middle of it a thin red worm, as long as his finger, was trying to find a way back into it.

Henrik looked at the tray until the worm had nearly made it.

"Well," he said, in Swedish, and then in Finnish, in case it hadn't counted the first time.

Theo wrote it down: the air at the bottom, the colour of the mud, the worm. Then, under it, he wrote the other number, the one he wrote every week now without being asked: how many of the guests had died that week, at the end of their three weeks' work.

At the station they called it that. The Finnish word for a guest is *vieras*, and it is also the word for a stranger, and for a thing that has come from somewhere else. Henrik had started it, the first winter, and nobody had found a better word.

Theo looked at the two numbers, one under the other, the air and the dead, for longer than it took to read them. Then he closed the log, and they turned the boat for home.
