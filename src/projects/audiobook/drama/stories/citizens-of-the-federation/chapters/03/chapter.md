# Chapter 3: Leaving

Walt Dorsey put the tomatoes in on a Saturday in the middle of May, two weeks before his father would ever have dared, and Nora helped.

Nora had just turned two. Her help was to carry each plant from the tray to its hole in both hands, at a run, and then stand on it. Walt sat on an upturned bucket at the edge of the bed, with the leg in its steel brace stuck out straight in front of him, and each time he fished the plant out from under her shoe and set it in the ground as if nothing had happened.

"My dad put them in at the end of May," he told her. "Never a day before. Said the frost would get them." He pressed the earth round a stem with his thumbs. "The frost's not what it was."

Nora stood on the next one.

Inside, a pupil was stumbling through a minuet in the front room, and Ruth was saying "Again," in the voice of a woman who had said it to every pupil she ever had and meant it every time. The pupils came to the house now, in person, because everybody who could be vaccinated had been. Across the street, Gene Kessler, who hadn't been and wouldn't be, was mowing his lawn in straight lines, and lifted a hand to Walt at the end of every one.

From the porch, Anna watched her father and her daughter, and thought that they had come for two weeks, more than a year ago, and that nobody in the house had said so in months.

---

At the beginning of July, she sat on the edge of the bath in the house she grew up in, holding a test with two lines on it. She had been nearly seven weeks along before she thought to wonder. She had been busy.

She told her parents that evening, out by the tomato bed, where her father was tying the plants to their stakes with strips of an old shirt.

Walt held the strip of shirt for a while without tying it.

"Well," he said. "I'll have the crib down."

"Don't you dare go up in that attic," said Ruth, from the porch, and then laughed, because she had heard who she sounded like.

Nobody asked where the baby would be born. It would be born where they were. That was how it went with the house, that year: nothing was decided, and everything was settled.

Brynner's offices, in the meantime, kept almost opening. In the summer the company said it would have its people back in the autumn. In the autumn a new variant of the virus came through, and it said January. In January there was another, and it said it would let everyone know. Ben worked in the spare room at the back and paid for a storage unit in San Francisco every month, full of a life they weren't living, and that September Nora had started at the preschool in the basement of her grandmother's church, two mornings a week, in a coat with ducks on it.

---

Theo was born on a Friday in February, in the hospital downtown. The hospital allowed one visitor, because of the virus, so Walt and Ruth met their grandson on the Sunday, in the front hall, with snow melting off Ben's boots onto the mat.

Walt held out his hands. He had held out his hands like that on the front path nearly two years before, for Nora, and Anna had had to decide in a second whether to let him. This time she didn't have to decide anything. She gave him the baby.

Walt looked down at Theo and didn't say anything. Theo looked at the ceiling, which was the most interesting thing he had seen so far.

"He's got the Dorsey chin," said Walt.

"He's got no chin at all," said Ruth. "Give him here."

She held him with her left hand under his head. It shook a little, as it always did now. She looked at it, and then at the baby.

"He doesn't mind," she said.

Then she gave him back and went to the piano and played him "Happy Birthday", with every one of the extra notes, because he hadn't had it yet.

Six days later, Russia invaded Ukraine. On Ben's screen the next morning, the Finns on his team were quieter than usual. Mikko, who had been on Ben's screen nearly every morning for three years, mentioned that his grandfather had fought the Russians in the snow in 1939, and then talked about the player's buffering for an hour, as if he had said nothing at all.

---

On the second Tuesday in April, Brynner sent its email. From the first of September, everyone would work from an office again: their own, or, at the company's expense, another office where their team had people. Ben's team had people in two offices. One was San Francisco. The other was Helsinki, where the player had come from.

"Belfast?" said Anna.

"Belfast has one desk on the player team," said Ben. "Declan's sitting at it."

They gave San Francisco one evening. It had three times the rent, and the same country round it, and it was a day's flying from this house. It was everything that was wrong with leaving her parents, and very little of what might be worth it. If she was going to leave them at all, Anna said, it wouldn't be for that.

She gave Toronto an evening too, because Joy was there. Brynner had no office in Toronto, and Canada, when she read its pages, drew the names of people who could bring their parents out of a pool, like a raffle.

"So it's Helsinki," said Ben. "Or I find something here."

"Here," said Anna.

"I'll look," said Ben, and he did.

---

That night, after Ben had gone up, Anna did what she always did with something coming: she found the forms.

She found the page on the website of the Finnish immigration service at one in the morning, with Theo asleep on her shoulder. She had typed in *parents*. The page was in English, and it was very clear.

Only a Finnish citizen could ask for a parent to come. That was four years away at the very least, and an exam in a language in which she could at present say nothing. And when she could ask, there would be two ways to do it. The first was for a parent who had moved at the same time as her child, so that their life together was never broken off. Anna couldn't ask for four years, so that one was shut before she started. The second had no date on it at all. A parent could come if she was *fully dependent* on her child, *both physically and mentally*. Needing money wasn't enough, the page said. Being ill wasn't enough, if there was anybody at home to treat it.

She read it three times, in case it said something else. It didn't. It was written by sensible people for sensible reasons, and what it came to was this: she could have her parents in Finland on the day they couldn't manage without her. For as long as they were fine, the answer was no.

It would take something terrible, said a small voice that she didn't like. She would have to sit in Helsinki and wait for something terrible.

She shut the laptop.

---

In May, Ben found something here. A company out past the mall, which did the billing for hospitals, wanted somebody to look after the program that sent the bills. It paid a little more than half of what Brynner did. It had health insurance, and Theo was three months old. Ben told her about it on the porch, carefully, with all the numbers.

"Is it what you want?" said Anna.

"No," said Ben. "I'll take it, though. If you say stay, I'll take it, and that'll be that."

She knew that he would. He had had a map of Helsinki open on his laptop since April, with the tram lines on it, and while she watched he closed it. He would drive out past the mall every morning for twenty years and send sick people their bills, and he would never once say Helsinki again. It would have been easier if she hadn't known it.

She made a spreadsheet: two columns, the case for Dayton and the case for Helsinki. Her parents went at the top of Dayton's, and for a day there didn't seem to be any need for a second line. Mikko told Ben that Finland had had two shootings at schools, years ago, and had changed its gun laws after. She put that in Helsinki's column. In the middle of May, Finland asked to join NATO, the alliance of countries that defend each other, because of what Russia had done next door. She put that in Dayton's. She was trying very hard to be fair.

---

On the Tuesday after that, something happened at an elementary school in a town in Texas called Uvalde. The television in the front room went off that evening and stayed off for the rest of the week, and Walt, who had watched the news every night of his adult life, didn't turn it back on.

On the Thursday, Nora came home from preschool with a note in her backpack. It had been written the week before, and the teachers had sent it home anyway. It said that the children had practised their lockdown drill, and that they had done wonderfully.

At supper Nora showed them. She got down off her chair and crawled under the table and sat among their feet with her knees pulled up and one finger pressed hard against her lips.

"It's the quiet game," she said, from under the table, not very quietly. "We go in the cubby corner. And Miss Jackie turns the light off. And somebody knocks on the door, and we don't say anything." She was enormously proud of it. "Not even if it's Mommy."

Nobody at the table said anything.

Walt put his fork down. Ruth looked at the window. Ben looked at Anna, and Anna got down on the kitchen floor in her good jeans, so that her face was level with her daughter's.

"Why do we play the quiet game?" said Nora.

Anna had promised herself, in the year a county woman came every Thursday to look in her fridge and nobody told her why, that she would tell her own children the truth about things. She had not expected to begin with this, or so soon.

"Because once in a long while," she said, "a person comes to a school who wants to hurt people. It almost never happens. But if it did, the quiet game is how Miss Jackie keeps you safe. That's why you're so good at it."

Nora thought about it.

"I'm the best at it," she said, and came out from under the table for her ice cream.

That night Anna lay awake under the plastic stars on her old ceiling.

"I'm not raising them here," she said.

"Then we go," said Ben, into the dark.

Anna opened her mouth to say yes. Down the hall, her father coughed in his sleep, and she heard the bed creak as her mother turned over to see that he was all right.

"I didn't say that," said Anna.

---

In the second week of June the billing company wrote to say that the job was his, and that they would need an answer by the fifth of July. Ben printed the letter and put it on the kitchen table beside Brynner's form, and didn't say anything about either of them.

On the last Friday in June, the Supreme Court took away the country's right to an abortion and left it to each state. That evening Ohio's ban came into force: nothing after about six weeks, before many women know they are pregnant at all.

Anna read it on her phone at midnight, in the kitchen, with Theo feeding. She had been nearly seven weeks along before she knew she was carrying him. She had wanted him with her whole heart. But it had been hers to want.

She thought about Nora at fifteen. In Finland, nothing had changed that night. In the house she had grown up in, it had changed by bedtime. She didn't put it in either column. It wasn't that kind of thing.

---

She asked them on the Sunday before the Fourth of July, on the porch, after church.

Every porch on the street had its flag out for the country's birthday, Walt's included. Walt was in his chair with his cap from the plant on, and Ruth sat beside him in her church dress, with her organ shoes still on, because she had played that morning. Ben stood in the doorway with Theo. Nora was on the steps with a box of chalk, drawing a sun with a great many legs.

Anna told them about Brynner and the first of September, and about the letter from the billing company. Then she told them about the page on Finland's website, all of it, just as she had read it: the four years, and the exam, and the two ways in, and the words *fully dependent*.

"So I can't bring you now," she said. "I know that. I've read it every night since April, and I can't. But I could get the exam. I could be a citizen in four years, and then I could ask, and I'd find a way to make them say yes. I'm good at forms." She made herself look at her mother. "So I'm asking now. When I can bring you, will you come?"

She knew what she was asking. Her mother had fought Dolores for a year before she let her near her arm, and trusted her ever since, and had sent a stranger off this porch rather than let him touch it. Anna was asking her to start again, at seventy, in a country where she couldn't have read the label on her own medicine. Anna asked anyway. She wanted them with her more than she could make sensible, and she had never asked her parents for anything this size.

Walt looked out at the tomato bed. The plants were up past the top of their stakes, heavy with fruit, all of it still green.

"Who'd do the tomatoes?" he said.

"Dad."

"I mean it. They're coming on good this year." He shifted the brace on the boards. "Annie. I'm seventy-five, and I've got the one leg. I'm not learning Finnish with it."

Ruth had not taken her eyes off her daughter.

"Fully dependent," said Ruth. She said it as she read out other people's mistakes. "I have been that. I was that the year you were eleven, and you stood on the stairs and watched. I am not going to fill in a form to be it again, on you. You've done your years." She smoothed her dress over her knee. "And it took me a year to let Dolores near that arm. I haven't got another year to spend on a stranger."

"Then I'll stay," said Anna.

It was out before she had put it in order. In the doorway, she felt Ben not move.

"Ben's got the job. It's a good job. I'll ring the school board about kindergarten, and we'll get a place near here, or we won't, we'll just stay, and I'll do the shopping, and I'll be here. If you need me here, I'll stay." She could hear how fast she was talking. "All you have to do is say you need me."

Nobody said anything. Two houses down, somebody was dragging a grill across a patio.

"I won't tell you stay and I won't tell you go," said Ruth. "It's my arm. It's your life."

Then she got up and went inside, and the screen door banged behind her, and through the open window the piano started: a scale, up the whole keyboard and down again, fast and very clean. Anna had been hearing that scale since before she could reach the keys. It was what Ruth played when she didn't want to say anything else.

Nora put down her chalk and listened, with her head on one side.

That left her father. Anna looked at him, and waited for him to say it. It was three words. She would have unpacked by supper.

"You call us Sundays," said Walt. "Eleven o'clock, after church. Wherever you end up. Your mother'll play you something."

"Dad."

"We'll be fine, Annie," said Walt. "We're fine."

He had been saying it since he was five, the way other people said hello. It was the first time he had ever said it knowing exactly what it cost and choosing to say it anyway. He watched it work. He watched his daughter's shoulders come down.

---

Walt had decided on the Thursday of the note, at supper, with his fork in his hand and his granddaughter under the table among his feet. He had decided before Anna ever asked.

He knew something about Annie that she didn't seem to know herself. She would stay for the smallest reason he could give her. He wouldn't have to ask. He would only have to let her see him once on the stairs, really see him, the way he went up them now when there was nobody in the hall. She would tell everyone it was what she wanted. She would give up the other place for her children, the one without the quiet game, and Ben would go out past the mall to send people their bills, and neither of them would ever complain of it. And if he said yes to the other thing, in four years, it would be Annie getting a man with one good leg down the steps of some building in the ice, every day, until he died. He had exactly one thing big enough to get her out of this house, and it was a word. What she did with it was hers to decide.

He had also worked out, on the back of an envelope, how old he would be by the time Theo was old enough to remember him. Then he had torn the envelope up and put it in the trash under the potato peelings, where Ruth wouldn't find it.

---

That night, in bed, Ben said, "Well. He says they're fine."

Anna turned her head on the pillow and looked at him.

He saw his mistake about a second after he had made it, the way you see a glass going off the edge of a table. What he had felt on the porch was relief. He had taken her father at his word. He could see that Anna was in pain, the way you can see that somebody is cold. He couldn't feel it.

"We'll come back every summer," he said.

"And every summer they'll be a year older," said Anna.

He didn't have anything to say to that, so he didn't say anything. He did the one thing he could think of, which was to stay awake with her. Downstairs, a little after midnight, the piano started again, so softly it was hardly there: something slow that Anna didn't know. She lay and listened, and for once she didn't listen for how her mother was. She didn't want to know.

---

In the morning she sat at the kitchen table with the two pieces of paper and her pen. On Brynner's form there was a box for her husband's new office. On the billing company's letter there was a line for his name.

She could still sign for Dayton. If she stayed, she would be ten minutes away when her father fell. She would drive her mother to church and be in the room when the doctors talked. She would be the daughter whose name was on the form in the piano bench, in the town where the form was. Her mother had sat in the front room and watched people with their bags going through a gate in Columbus, and said, *Now we know where they'll put me when your father dies.* From this table, Anna could stop that. From Helsinki she would hear about it on a Sunday.

And Helsinki itself was no promised land. She had read about the winters, when the sun went down in the middle of the afternoon. She would be a woman who couldn't read the label on a jar of baby food, in a language with fifteen ways to end a noun, in a city where no dentist would want a receptionist who couldn't talk to the patients. She would be a stranger for years, and maybe always.

It was also the place where her children would grow up without the quiet game.

She had never in her life been fooled by her father's *fine*. She had been hearing it all her life. She knew what was under it. That was the trouble with what he had done. He hadn't fooled her, and her shoulders had come down anyway. He had handed it to her, and she could hand it back, or she could take it. If she took it, she would have to act from now on as if she believed it, and she would know every Sunday that she didn't.

She held the pen until her hand ached. She thought of her mother watching the gate. She thought of her daughter under this table, with one finger pressed against her lips, waiting for a knock.

She wrote *Helsinki*. Then she put her hand flat over the word, as if somebody might come in and see it, and sat like that until Theo woke.

That afternoon she took an empty folder out of the drawer under the phone and printed the page from Finland, all of it, and put it in. She wrote *Mom and Dad* on the front. They had said no. She had heard them. She put down *four years* underneath, and the month.

---

Finland had opened a fast lane that summer for people like Ben, whom it wanted: a decision in two weeks, for him and his family, as long as his family applied at the same time. Anna read the words, *at the same time*, and thought of the other page. Then she got on with the forms, because the forms were what she could do.

Ruth went through every one of them at the kitchen table with her red pen. She found a comma missing in the Finnish government's own English, and marked it. She checked Theo's date of birth against his birth certificate twice, and Nora's three times. She did it all without a word about why there were forms, and Anna, watching her mother's pen go down the page, helping her leave, had to go out and stand in the yard.

The permits came in thirteen days.

Mikko sent a video from his phone: the daycare round the corner from the apartment Brynner had found, low and yellow, with a sandpit and a fence, and past the fence the sea. It was grey and wide and very calm, with a little boat going across it.

"Boat," said Nora, and put her finger on it.

"There's a place for her," said Mikko, off the screen. "They're very excited. They have never had an American."

Walt watched videos on Ben's laptop about how to say *thank you* in Finnish, and practised it on Nora, who said it back to him better. He asked Ben whether they grew tomatoes in Finland. Ben said they did, in greenhouses.

"Greenhouses," said Walt, as if it were cheating.

In August, Dolores came and gave Ruth her shot in the front hall. Afterward she stood on the porch with her bag and looked at the boxes stacked by the stairs, and at Anna.

"I left my mother when I was twenty-five," she said. "In the Philippines. For nineteen years she told me on the telephone that she was fine."

Anna didn't know what to say to that.

"So I'll tell you," said Dolores. "Not your father. Me. If she isn't."

---

They left on a Saturday in August, when Theo was six months old.

The night before, Anna stood on her childhood bed in her socks and took the plastic stars down off the ceiling, one at a time, and put them in an envelope for Nora's new room. They came away easily. They had only ever been stuck on.

In the morning Gene brought his car round, the big one, because Walt couldn't manage the airport and Gene had offered to drive them before anybody could ask. Ben carried the cases down the steps. Barb stood at her own front door across the street in her housecoat and waved, already, though nobody had gone yet.

Ruth said goodbye in the front hall. She wouldn't do it on the porch, where the street could see. She held Theo, with her left hand under his head, and gave him back. Then she took Anna by both arms.

"You're still the one," she said. "On my form. In the bench. I'm not changing it."

"Mom. I'll be in Finland."

"They have telephones in Finland," said Ruth. "I looked it up." She let go. "Go on, now. Go on."

Out on the porch, Walt had come down two steps, and stopped there, with his hand on the rail. Anna sent Nora out to say goodbye to her grandfather.

Nora didn't. She ran back in past her mother, through the front room, and climbed up onto the back of the sofa under the front window, where she had waved at the street since she could stand, and put both her hands flat on the glass.

Walt looked round, and saw her.

She was singing to him. He could see it. Her mouth was going, and her head went from side to side in time, and her hands came off the glass for the parts with actions in them. Through the window he couldn't hear a word of it. It was the song they sang at preschool every day, at the end of the morning, when it was time to go home.

He thought of his mother at the window of the polio ward, and of what she had told him, years later, that she was doing there. Only singing.

He knew better now. He stood on the step and watched his granddaughter sing to him through the window, every verse, with all the actions.

Then Anna came and lifted her down, and carried her out to the car, and buckled her in beside her brother with the knitted rabbit in her lap, and got in between them.

Gene pulled away from the curb slowly, because he could see Walt's face. Walt waved. Nora waved back through the car window, and Ben, in the front, turned round in his seat and looked at his wife, and put his hand back between the seats without a word. Anna held on to it. Behind them, through the open front door, the piano began, and they heard it all the way down the street, until the corner.

---

On the porch, Walt waited until there was nothing left to wave at. Then he came back up the two steps, one at a time, good leg, brace, good leg, and went in.

Ruth was at the piano with her hands in her lap. She had stopped when the car turned the corner, and she was sitting the way she sat at the end of a hymn, very straight, waiting for the next one.

"They'll call Sunday," said Walt. "We'll be fine."

"Don't you start that with me, Walter," said Ruth.

He sat down on the end of the bench, where there was just room for him, and she moved over, and neither of them said anything at all for the rest of the morning.
