# Frogmino

Brain Wall (the Japanese game show) meets Tetris, with Frogger-style hopping. The player is a frog shaped like a tetromino, and shaped vehicles come at it down a road: side by side, leaving a gap it must match (staggered vehicles to weave between are tabled: see Traffic). It is real-time, seen from behind the frog in real 3D with Three.js (through `@react-three/fiber`). Faked 2D depth was considered and rejected: it is harder to get right than real 3D.

The owner and a friend designed it. What follows is decided unless it sits under Open questions.

The obstacles are rows of traffic (see Traffic). The rules still call a row a wall, from the prototype: a row's vehicles are its solid cells and everything else on the face is its opening, so wherever the sections below speak of walls and openings, they mean a row as the rules see it.

## Code map

- `types.ts`: cells on the wall face, tetromino kinds, the frog, openings, spans of lanes, and the solids of a row as the rules see them: a vehicle, the cells it fills and its length, and a gate's post, the lane line it stands on and its length.
- `logic.ts`: pure piece rules, no React and no Three. The tetromino definitions, rotation, piece sizes, whether a piece lies inside a span of lanes, turning about the piece's middle with Tetris-style kicks, the cells a frog covers, and the pass test.
- `run.ts`: pure run rules. The rule state of one playthrough (a team of frogs, one per player, solo a team of one, sharing one depth; the road's width in lanes, the course's; traffic; clock), where each frog starts, the players' actions and held keys, the player inputs and the input log, `step` for moving time on a tick, `playTo` and `replay` for playing a run on, or again from its start, with its log, the overpass start and the drop, judging rows as they reach the team, keeping each frog out of the vehicles still overlapping it and out of its partners, riding, the bonk, jumping back among and through rows already passed, taking a gate's piece either way, recycling the traffic out of view as the stream's next rows, and the finish.
- `traffic.ts`: a row of traffic (which vehicles, in which lanes) and the opening it leaves, refusing a row whose vehicles share a lane or leave the road, or fill a gate; a gate's lanes and the lane lines its posts stand on; the row as the rules see it, each vehicle's cells and length and each gate post's line, as long as the row; the one test of a frog's move against a row's solids, vehicles and posts alike; and where a row's back is.
- `composer.ts`: the answer-first row composer, for one piece or several at once and refusing others, with or without a gate, the difficulty rules, and the poses a frog can take and which of them pass an opening, through a normal opening or a gate, never across a gate's post.
- `course.ts`: the course format, rows as plain data (vehicles in lanes, gates with their pieces, the gap before each row, whether it needs a gate, its difficulty), and the demo seed. It is the seed of the shared course format (see Courses).
- `courses.ts`: what a session plays: the road's width, each player's starting piece, and the rows, with their vehicles to draw and their solids for the rules. Solo's course is the row stream; local co-op's is the designed co-op course (see Co-op), and `designedCourse` plays any course designed by hand, going round its last rows once they are all met. A course spec names one of them as plain data, for a replay.
- `coop-course.ts`: local co-op's course as plain data, designed by hand: the road's width, the pieces, and each row's vehicles, gap, lesson and designed answer.
- `stream.ts`: the row stream, every row the frog will meet in order, from the seed alone: the designed ramp and the waves after it, the pieces the frog could hold at each row, the builder rules that keep them few, and how far a needed gate can be from the row that needs it.
- `ticks.ts`: the rules' clock: the tick rate, the tuning's times in whole ticks (the one place seconds become ticks), and how many ticks a frame runs, with the clamp for a backgrounded tab.
- `tuning.ts`: the feel knobs (wall speed and spacing, course length, how far behind the frog a row is recycled and how far ahead it reappears, hop airtime, ease duration, the drop's distance and duration, the finish leap's duration, corridor size, jump distance, held-key repeat interval, bonk knock-back, the camera's height, follow distance and look-ahead), in one constants object. ## The wall face

A wall is a grid of cells: columns across the corridor, rows up from the floor. An opening is a set of those cells. The frog is a tetromino with a lateral column (its leftmost cell), a rotation of quarter turns clockwise as the player sees it from behind, and a hop height of 0 or 1. A rotated piece always rests on the floor, or one cell above it when hopping.

## The motion model

What the rules decide is kept apart from what the player sees.

- **The course runs from an overpass to a finish gantry** on a road that never ends either way. Rows are placed along it and all move toward the start at the course's wall speed. The frog does not run on its own: it advances only by jumping forward, with no limit on how far ahead it may race.
- **The frog starts on the overpass,** standing on its deck above the start, out of play: the traffic drives on beneath it and no row judges it. It can slide and turn there to line itself up, but not hop or jump back. Its first jump forward (W, a drag up on a touch screen, or up on a controller) drops it onto the road the drop distance ahead, into play, in a big leap over the traffic: the rows it flies over go by beneath it unjudged, a drop that would land it in a vehicle is refused, like a jump back, and a row's face within the leap's last jump of the landing is judged like any jump forward. A held key repeats once the leap lands. There is no way back up. The drop distance (a knob in `tuning.ts`) puts the camera past the overpass's deck when the frog lands, and still after a bonk as it lands, so the deck never hides the frog or the road; the first row starts a reading room beyond the landing. The leap is drawn rising well above the deck and falling to the road, and the camera glides forward over the deck with it, rising a little to keep the frog in view; a test holds the camera's path clear of the overpass and its view of the frog unblocked.
- **There is no safe start zone.** A jump back past the start is fine, and so is a knock-back: the road goes on behind the overpass, and the traffic follows the frog down it.
- **Traffic is a stream, and never runs out.** The run lines up the row stream's rows in order (see Levels), each its own gap beyond the back of the one before, until the traffic reaches the traffic horizon. A row whose back is the recycling distance behind the frog, behind the camera and out of its view, reappears ahead as the stream's next row: its gap beyond the frontmost row's back, and never nearer the frog than the traffic horizon, beyond the world's fog. So a row never appears or vanishes on screen, however far ahead or behind the start the frog is, and the frog meets the stream's rows in their order, however fast or slow it goes. The recycling distance is also further than a frog knocked back by a needs-gate row can ever be from the gate it needs, so that gate is always still there to go back for (see Gates). Both distances are knobs in `tuning.ts`.
- **Rows are spaced back to front.** Every vehicle's front in a row lines up; each reaches back its own length from the fleet (two or three), so the row's back is wherever its longest vehicle ends. The spacing is the gap from one row's back to the next row's front, so the time to read the next row doesn't depend on how long the vehicles are.
- **Crossing the finish line ends the run.** A jump forward that reaches the finish line, under the finish gantry, crosses it: the rules stop there, so no row can judge or bonk the frog again, every held key lets go and a hop lands. The frog springs up from the spring pad onto the gantry's deck in a celebratory leap, spinning once on the way, and stands there while the traffic keeps driving underneath. The done screen comes once it lands.
- **The rules play a team of frogs,** one per player, and solo is a team of one: wherever this section says the frog, each frog of the team is meant (see Co-op for what the team shares). Each frog has its own piece, rotation, column and hop; the team has one depth, one held jump, and a held slide for each player.
- **The frog's rule state is discrete** (column, rotation, depth and whether it is up from a hop) and changes the instant a key is pressed: the input applies at once, at the rules' current tick, with no wait for the next. One press is one action, and the operating system's key repeat is ignored; only a held jump or slide key repeats (see Controls). Its depth changes a jump or a bonk at a time.
- **The drawn frog follows its rule state,** and the rules never see the in-between. A jump or a slide is drawn as a move: a small hop that glides from where the frog was drawn to its new place, setting off and settling gently. A move takes the held-key repeat interval, so held moves chain, each landing as the next takes off, and a held jump flows on as a steady run of hops. A turn, and anything else, eases there over the ease duration. While a wall overlaps the drawn frog, it is drawn exactly at its rule state across the face and in height, with no move's hop: an eased turn swings cells through places neither pose covers, and a move made just before a wall arrives may not have finished, so either would draw the frog inside the wall. The one exception is a drawn frog still riding when the rules land it (see Riding). Its depth goes on gliding, since every opening is the same along the row, unless the frog in the pose it is drawn in would be inside a vehicle at the depth it is drawn at (one it has just jumped clear of and slid into the lane of), when it is drawn at its rule depth at once. The camera follows the drawn depth, so that is the only time it jumps.
- **The rules run on a fixed tick,** a hundred a second (`ticks.ts`). Their clock is a whole number of ticks, and every time they keep (a hop's airtime, a held key's next repeat, the drop, the finish leap, a bonk) is a tick. The times in `tuning.ts` stay in seconds, for reading, and become ticks in one place, rounded to the nearest; at a hundred a second every one of them is a whole number of ticks, so the clock changes none of them, and a tick is shorter than a frame at any refresh rate. A wall's depth falls by the wall speed each tick, worked out from where and when it was placed, so no rounding builds up. The frame loop runs as many ticks as real time has passed, carrying the rest of the frame into the next, and clamps a long frame, so returning to a backgrounded tab doesn't lurch the course forward.
- **Inputs are a log.** Every player input (an action, a jump or slide key pressed or let go, every key let go as the window loses focus) is stamped with the tick it applies at and the player's slot, from 0 (0 in solo; the rules throw for a slot with no frog), and kept in the run's input log, in tick order, inputs at one tick in the order they came. A local input applies at once, at the current tick, and goes into the log there. The log is plain data, so a session can insert an input that reaches it late in its place and replay from there.
- **A run is a pure function of its course, its tuning and its log.** Replaying the log from the start reproduces the run bitwise, however the ticks were chunked into frames: a tick per frame, a long catch-up or anything between. That determinism is what co-op's timed inputs stand on. A test plays a real stream by inputs alone, through bonks, riding, a gate, the drop and the finish, and replays its log in chunks of every size to the same state. A replay runs a tick at a time, and a whole run's takes a fraction of a second; rollback will replay from a recent state rather than the start.
- **Drawing runs between ticks.** Everything is drawn on the drawing's clock, the rules' time plus how far real time has got into the next tick, so nothing visibly steps at the tick rate: the traffic is drawn where the wall speed has it at that moment, and the drawn frog's glides, arcs and knock-back, and the camera following it, run on the same clock. The rules judge a row at the first tick after it truly reaches the frog, so a row can be drawn up to a tick's travel (a fortieth of a cell) into the frog for a moment before it bonks it.
- **Every wall the frog hasn't passed is ahead of it, and every wall it has passed is behind it.** Each rule keeps that true, and it is what makes each arrival judged exactly once.
- **The frog and every vehicle have depth,** as drawn: the frog one cube, each vehicle its own length. A vehicle overlaps the frog while their depth ranges along the course intersect: from the instant the row's front face reaches the frog's until the vehicle's own back face goes by the frog's.
- **A wall is judged when it reaches the frog,** once, at the tick its front has reached the frog's and the overlap begins, against all its vehicles, using the frog's rule column, rotation and hop at that tick. The rules move a tick at a time however long the frame, so a long frame can't carry a wall past the frog unjudged, and the hop is read at the wall's arrival rather than at the frame's end. Within a tick, things happen in one order: the walls move; a wall reaching the frog is judged, before a hop due to land that tick lands, since the wall arrived within the tick, at or before its end; then a hop lands, or a ride ends; then the held keys repeat, in the order pressed; then the inputs stamped with the tick apply. A bonk knocks the frog back from the wall's face where it is at that tick, at most a tick's travel past the frog's own. A jump forward that reaches a wall's face is judged at once in the same way.
- **The frog is solid against the vehicles that overlap it,** each for as long as it overlaps. Having passed, its cells are all inside the opening, and they stay clear of every vehicle until it has gone by: a move, a turn (wall kick included) or a hop that would put a cell into an overlapping vehicle's cells is refused, just as a move off the corridor edge is ignored, while moves that stay clear are fine. A gate's posts are as solid (see Gates). Once a shorter vehicle has gone by, its lanes are free, while a longer neighbour still overlaps. So the opening can only grow after the row is judged, never shrink. A jump forward only takes the frog away from a wall it has passed.
- **The bonk.** A wall the frog fits passes around it. A wall it doesn't fit bonks it: the frog is knocked back from the wall's face by the bonk knock-back, a few jumps' distance, however far behind the start that takes it. The wall stays solid and keeps coming, so it bonks the frog again when it arrives unless the frog fits by then or has got clear. A jump forward into a wall the frog doesn't fit is a bonk too. Walls never let through a frog that doesn't fit, and never carry it along: the obstacles are becoming vehicles, and being carried along by one looks wrong. The rows are spaced, back to front, so that a knock-back always lands the frog clear of every row, and never reaches one it has passed, however long its vehicles; the rules refuse tuning that would allow it. That spacing also means no two walls overlap the frog at once.
- **Feedback is for bonks only.** A bonked frog is drawn knocked back along a low arc, from wherever it is drawn: a bonk that interrupts a move (a jump forward into a row's face, or a row arriving mid-glide) finds the drawn frog short of the face, and it never skips forward to the face first, nor does the camera following it. A held jump that repeats mid-knock-back glides on from where the arc has it. The frog itself flattens against the row and is left dazed (see The frog). A pass has no feedback on the frog. The pink bonk flash of the cube frog is gone: the dazed look reads on both players' frogs, where pink suited only the green one.
- **A jump back may go back into a passed row, and through it, wherever the frog fits.** It is refused only if it would put one of the frog's cells into a vehicle of a row the frog has passed, or the frog across one of its gate's posts, anywhere along the ground the jump covers, each vehicle its own length; then it doesn't happen, just as a move off the corridor edge is ignored, with no bonk. Among a passed row's vehicles the frog is solid against them as usual. A jump back that takes the frog's front back across a row's front makes that row ahead of it again, to be judged afresh when it arrives, so the frog meets it again in its order; through a gate, it takes the gate's piece (see Gates). Jumping back is faster than the traffic, so a frog can always catch a row it has passed.
- **A hop lifts the frog one cell for its airtime,** then it lands. A hop pressed while airborne is ignored. It is drawn as a smooth arc that rises quickly to a full cell and falls once the frog lands.
- **Riding.** A frog that is up while a row overlaps it rides the row: it stays up, gliding across the vehicles' low parts, until the last of the row's vehicles has gone by, then lands, however long ago its airtime ran out. This is a mechanic, not a safeguard. It makes the hop's timing forgiving: a hop pressed early, whose airtime would end while the row goes by, still carries the frog across. And it looks like the frog gliding across a car's hood. The riding frog can still slide within the opening, and can jump forward, in the same pose and height, which stays inside the opening, so it clears the vehicles and lands sooner. A held jump or slide keeps repeating while it rides. A jump back follows the usual rule: refused only where it would meet a vehicle. Riding has no visual of its own; the hop arc simply stays up. The drawn frog rides too: its glide trails the rules, so when a jump forward carries the rule frog clear and it lands, the drawn frog may still be among the row's vehicles. It stays up there, gliding on, while that pose keeps clear of them, and falls along the arc once the row has gone by it too, so it never drops into a vehicle or skips ahead to avoid one.
- **Turning** is about the piece's middle, with Tetris-style kicks. A turn tries the piece centred where it turned, then kicked a lane sideways, then two lanes, and takes the first spot that stays on the road, clear of the vehicles overlapping the frog, of a gate's posts (never swept across one) and of its partner; if none is clear, the turn fails. A clockwise turn tries a lane right before a lane left, a counter-clockwise turn left before right, so a turn and its mirror image kick mirror-image ways; turning and turning back in open space returns the piece to its lane. Kicks are sideways only: a frog's height is its hop's, timed by the airtime and kept up by riding, so a kick that lifted a frog would be a free hop and one that dropped it would cut its hop short, and the face is only four cells tall. Two lanes is the most any piece needs at the road's edge (the I, between four lanes wide and one). Solo turns kick the same way, so a turn that once failed against a vehicle, a post, or the I's two-lane edge can now succeed; no turn that succeeded before lands anywhere else. A move off the corridor edge is ignored.
- The rules are pure functions of the run state and its inputs, a tick at a time, run from the render loop; the drawing updates Three objects directly, so React does not re-render per frame.

## The camera

The camera follows the frog from behind and above, over the middle of the road, higher than the tallest wall, so a wall passes beneath it and the camera never goes through one. It is pitched down at the floor a little ahead of the frog: steep enough to keep the frog in view, shallow enough to read the incoming wall's opening and judge how far away it is. A camera too high flattens depth. Its height, follow distance and look-ahead are knobs in `tuning.ts`. It rises and falls with the ground under the frog: the overpass's deck at the start, the road, and the finish gantry's deck at the end.

## The fit outline

The frog's cells, at its current column, rotation and hop height, are always projected onto the face of the next wall it will meet, as an outline only: no fit colouring and no toggle. It follows the rule state instantly, so the player reads the fit from where the outline sits against the opening.

## The pass test

The frog passes a wall if every cell it covers is inside an opening: a subset test, checked when the wall reaches the frog. Any covered cell that meets the wall is a bonk, which knocks the frog back, and so is a frog standing across a gate's post (see Gates). Openings are usually larger than the piece; how tight they are is the difficulty dial.

## Controls

- **Move** (slide) left and right across the corridor. Holding the key repeats exactly as a held jump does (below).
- **Rotate** the piece, with Tetris-style kicks off the road's edge, the vehicles, a gate's posts and the partner (see Turning in The motion model).
- **Hop**: vertical, exactly one cell, for raised openings. The pass test decides a hop like anything else: a needless hop is fine as long as the piece still clears the opening, and it bonks only when there is no room above. A special rule punishing unneeded hops would be confusing.
- **Jump** forward or back along the course a fixed distance at a time. Jumping forward is the only way the frog advances; jumping back buys reading time. Holding a jump key keeps jumping at a steady cadence, so the player needn't mash it: the first jump comes on the press, then another every held-key repeat interval, timed by the rules' own clock rather than the operating system's key repeat. A bonk doesn't stop the repeat: the next held jump comes a full repeat interval after the bonk, never at once, and a refused jump back doesn't stop it either. Releasing the key, or the window losing focus, lets go.
- **Held slides follow the same rules,** on the same repeat interval: a refused slide (the corridor edge, or a vehicle overlapping the frog) doesn't stop the repeat, a bonk puts the next slide a full interval after it, and it works on the overpass and while riding. A jump and a slide can be held at once, each on its own cadence, so W and D together climb diagonally. A newer key on the same line replaces the held one: pressing D while A is held slides right from then on, and releasing D then stops sliding rather than going back to A, as with W and S.

Hop and jump forward are different actions. There is no action for changing piece: a gate changes it as the frog goes through (see Gates).

**Input sources.** Every device reaches the rules through an input source, which turns the device's presses into player inputs (an action, a jump or slide pressed or let go, everything let go) for that device; the seating says which player slot a device drives, and the session applies the input to that slot's frog. In solo every device drives player 0. In local co-op each device drives the slot it joined (see Lobby). Online, every device drives this player's own slot, Sprout for the host and Splash for its partner. The sources never know which player they are, and the rules never know which device, or whether the player is on this computer or online.

**The keyboard,** by physical key, so the WASD block stays put on other keyboard layouts:

- **Solo:** A and D or ← and → slide, Q and E rotate, Space hops, W and S or ↑ and ↓ jump. R restarts and M mutes.
- **Local co-op** splits the keyboard into two devices, one hand block each, so no key is both players': the left half is WASD to slide and jump, Q and E to rotate and Space to hop; the right half is the arrows to slide and jump, , and . to rotate and / to hop. Each half has every action. R restarts and M mutes, for either player. The legend in the game's corner shows each player's keys side by side. Two players on one keyboard can hit a cheap keyboard's limit on keys held at once (ghosting), which shows as a press going missing.

**Controllers,** on the shared gamepad library (see the root `CLAUDE.md`), standard (Xbox) layout only; a pad whose layout isn't standard drives nothing, since its buttons are known only by number. Each controller is its own device, in solo and local co-op alike.

- **The left stick or the D-pad** slides (left, right) and jumps (up forward, down back), held as a key is, so the rules repeat a held direction on the same interval as a held key, and a diagonal holds a slide and a jump at once, like W and D. The stick has the library's radial deadzone (0.2), then holds a way once it tilts past half way along that axis and lets go once it comes back inside 0.35: two thresholds, so a stick resting near one never chatters. The D-pad wins over the stick on its axis. Flicking across from one way to the other lets go of the first and holds the second.
- **A** hops; **X** or **LB** rotates anticlockwise and **B** or **RB** clockwise. **Menu** restarts. The right stick, Y, the triggers and View do nothing.
- A pad that disconnects lets go of everything it held. A pad's buttons are read once per animation frame, so a press reaches the rules at most a frame late.

**Touch,** in solo and online co-op, where each player has a device of their own; local co-op shares one screen, so touch doesn't join it. Every finger on the play area is its own gesture, so one thumb can drag while the other taps. The screen is split into thirds, left, centre and right, by where a finger comes down. A finger that stays within the tap slop (12 CSS pixels) is a tap; one that leaves it is a drag, locked to the axis it left the slop along (sideways if it went more sideways than up or down, otherwise up and down), and it never becomes anything else.

- **Drag** sideways, anywhere, to slide one lane per 32 CSS pixels of finger travel, and up or down to jump forward or back one jump per 32 CSS pixels, like mobile Tetris: a fast drag makes several steps at once, and dragging back takes them back, past where it started too. Each step fires the moment the finger gets there, mid-gesture. At 360px wide the corridor fits within one thumb's sweep, and a sweep up the screen makes a dozen jumps.
- **The axis lock** keeps a sideways drag's drift up or down from jumping the frog, and a jump drag's drift from sliding it: a thumb never drags dead straight, and an unmeant jump forward can bonk the team. Tracking both axes at once (a diagonal drag sliding and jumping, like W and D) was weighed and not built; a second finger can drag the other axis at the same time.
- **Tap** the left or right third to rotate anticlockwise or clockwise, and the **centre** to hop. A tap can only be told from a drag once the finger lifts, so a tap acts on the lift: the hop comes a tap's length (typically a tenth of a second) after the finger lands. Firing the hop as the finger lands would remove that wait, but every drag up or down starting in the centre would hop too.
- There is no press-and-hold: a long still touch is a tap like any other. A drag up covers jumping, a dozen jumps per sweep.

Mouse pointers are left to the keyboard. The play area takes no browser gestures: no scrolling, zooming, pull-to-refresh, text selection or long-press callout. The mute button and the done screen sit above the touch layer and keep their own taps; on touch screens the done screen has a Restart button, since there is no R key.

**The touch hint** shows on touch screens only (a coarse pointer), over the play area: the three zones with their taps, and the two drags beneath. It goes once the player has made three gestures, and the keyboard legend, with a line for controllers, shows only for fine pointers.

## Pieces

The frog's piece changes only at gates. There is no Tetris-style hold and no dealt-piece queue; a gate coming down the road, with its piece on it, is the next-piece preview, in the world. The frog's piece is part of its rule state, and every rule works for all seven pieces: passing, the bonk, solidity, riding, turning with its kick, the fit outline and the drawn frog.

## Gates

Pieces change with the traffic, not at fixed places on the road. Rows reach the frog in a fixed order, since it can't skip one, so a piece change that travels in a row comes at the same point in the run for a fast player and a slow one. (Pull-offs beside the road were tried and dropped: a fixed place on the road meets the rows at whatever pace the player goes, so the piece a row needs could be ahead of the frog or behind it, which softlocked fast and slow players alike, and an optional-looking pull-off turned out to be secretly required.)

- **A gate is part of a row:** a gap two lanes wide and the face's full height, beside the row's vehicles and its normal openings, framed by a thin post on the lane line either side and a lintel across the top. No vehicle takes its lanes, and the posts take up none: the road keeps its full width. A row has at most one.
- **The frame is solid.** A post fills no cell, but no frog may stand across it, with cells on both sides, or move across it, for as long as the row overlaps the frog, by the same rules as a vehicle: a frog across a post is bonked when the row reaches it or when it jumps forward into the row's face; a slide or a turn (wall kick included) that would put it across a post, or carry it through one, is refused while the row overlaps it; and a jump back that would put it across a post is refused, like one into a passed row's vehicle. So every pass is unambiguous: wholly through the gate or wholly beside it. In the rules a post is a solid of the row, alongside its vehicles, as long as the row's longest vehicle, so every rule that tests the vehicles tests the posts too.
- **Going through sets the frog's piece.** The frog takes the gate's piece when it crosses the row's front with every one of its cells in the gate's two lanes: forward, as the row arrives or as the frog jumps into it, and backward, as a jump back takes it out through the row's front. A frog wholly beside the gate, through a normal opening, keeps its piece.
- **The new piece's pose.** It takes the frog's rotation and column if that pose fits inside the gate, at the frog's height; otherwise the first rotation, then column, that does. If none does at that height (only an I, one cell up, whose upright pose is four cells tall) the frog lands and takes the first that fits on the road. One always fits: every piece has an upright pose two lanes wide or narrower, and the gap is the face's full height. The rules fail loudly if it ever doesn't. Depth is kept.
- **A gate sets the piece; it doesn't swap.** It keeps its piece, so going through it twice, or back and forth, is harmless.
- **Gates are optional.** The player can go through a normal opening and keep their piece, or through the gate and change. Sometimes the rows ahead need the gate's piece, sometimes they don't: reading the road ahead is the puzzle. Two lanes wide, a gate may need the frog to turn upright to fit.
- **Needs-gate rows** let through only the latest gate's piece (see Levels), and come at most two rows after it. A player who skipped a needed gate is bonked by the row they can't pass, and must go back for the gate: the intended learning moment, and it costs time.
- **No softlock.** A frog bonked by a needs-gate row is knocked back clear of every row it has passed, and that row keeps knocking it back while it stays there, so the needed gate's row is never further behind it than the rows and gaps between the two, at most two gaps and a row at their longest (the gate reach in `stream.ts`). The traffic is only recycled further behind the frog than that, so the gate's row is always still there. Jumping back is faster than the traffic, and each row between was built for every piece the frog could hold, its own included, so the frog can line up with each, jump back through it, then line up upright in the gate and jump back through that, taking the piece. The gate's row then comes at it again and passes around it, still in the gate, and the rows after it, built for the gate's piece too, let it on through the row that bonked it. A simulation test plays exactly that on several seeds' streams, skipping every gate it can.
- **The look.** The frame travels with the row, banded in two violets, as solid-looking as the vehicles since it bonks like them. Each post is a thin fin on its lane line, from the road to just above the tallest vehicle, running the row's whole length, as the rules hold it solid; it reaches into the lanes beside it only as far as a vehicle's details may stand proud of its side, so it buries them rather than lying about a cell. The lintel joins the posts at the row's front, just above the tallest vehicle and under the overpass's and the finish gantry's decks, and a cream sign on its face shows the gate's piece as a little frog, the player's own green, with eyes on its top row, like a next-piece preview coming down the road. The rows drive under those decks, which leave room above the tallest vehicle for the lintel and no more, so the icon is small. Below the lintel nothing of it stands on the road but the posts, and all of it passes under the decks and between their pillars; tests hold it to that, and its faces clear of the vehicles beside it for the depth buffer. A faint lilac shimmer drifts across the gap between the posts, not solid and not hiding the fit. The colours are tokens in `globals.css`; the art is in `components/gate.tsx`.
- **The sound.** A transformation plays the gate sound once, whichever way the frog went through (see Sound).

**In co-op** the rules give a gate's piece to whichever frog crosses the row's front with every cell in its lanes, and the gate stays open for the partner. The new piece's pose must also keep clear of the partner; a lone frog in a gate always has one (the partner is wholly outside the gate's lanes, or it would be across a post). Two frogs can be in a gate together only by filling its two lanes exactly (two upright Is, or two pieces interlocked with one up), and then neither new piece has room beside the other, so both keep their pieces. Still to build: rows with two gates letting partners pick, and the stream tracking pairs of pieces, one per partner, rejecting, failing loudly, any course that isn't completable for every pair the partners could hold; it should prefer two-gate rows, which keep the pairs few.

## Courses and medals

**Courses** come mainly from Claude, designing them on purpose; courses made by people are a bonus for later. The plan (one shared course format, Claude's tools for building on it, and later an editor) is `ideas/course-authoring.md`. `course.ts` holds the format's rows, which the seeded row stream already produces.

**The road's width is a level design tool,** not a fixed setting: a course says how many lanes it has, the way it says its rows. Solo plays on 7 today, and local co-op on 10 (see Co-op for why). 7, 9 and 10 lanes all read well in the `?world` preview, solo and as the co-op pair, on desktop and on a phone, so the choice is about play, not looks. A course has one width throughout: the world, the camera's fit and the rules all take it once, at the start.

A level is a course with a finish line, run against the clock: gold, silver and bronze times, in the manner of time-trial marble games. There is no endless mode and there are no lives. A bonk costs time: the ground it knocks the frog back is the penalty.

## Lobby

The page opens on the lobby, built on the shared `GameLobby`. It offers four ways in:

- **Play solo** starts the solo game at once.
- **Local co-op** opens the join screen, for two players on this computer.
- **Host co-op** opens a peer room and lists it on the open-games list.
- **Join** is a click on a game in that list, which joins its room by code under the hood. There is no typing of codes: every hosted game is public, which suits a small site played among friends.

The open-games list is the shared room list (see Multiplayer in the root `CLAUDE.md`), updating live as games are hosted, fill, start or close. A game is listed only while its host is connected and waiting for a partner: co-op is two frogs, so a game drops off the list once it has a partner or starts, and when its host leaves or closes the tab. It comes back if the partner leaves before the round.

**The waiting room** lists the players by their profile names, with the room's connection status (connecting, connected, reconnecting). The host has a Start button, enabled once a partner is in. A guest whose host leaves is told so, a guest who clicks a game whose host has gone is told it has closed (the peer module's `not-found`), and a guest who arrives after the seat was taken is turned away with a note. Leaving, or closing the tab, takes the room down through the peer module.

**The local co-op join screen** shows the two player slots, P1 Sprout and P2 Splash. Each device on this computer joins by pressing its join button, and takes the lowest free slot: the keyboard's left half with Space, its right half with /, a controller with A. A device holds at most one slot, a third device is turned away while both are taken, and a device leaves with its jump-back key (S, ↓) or B, freeing its slot for the next to join while the other player keeps theirs; a controller that disconnects leaves too. Each slot shows the device in it. Start (the button, Enter, or Menu on a controller) is enabled once both slots are taken, and starts local co-op on the co-op course (see Co-op). Back returns to the lobby. Touch doesn't join: it plays solo only.

**Start plays online co-op** on the co-op course: the host is Sprout and its partner Splash, each on their own device, with every device on it (the keyboard as solo's, controllers, touch) driving that one frog. A corner says which frog you are, and Leave goes back to the lobby. A partner who leaves mid-round, or closes the tab, ends it: the host is told who left, back in its waiting room, where its game is listed again; a guest whose host leaves is told so, as in the waiting room. Either player's restart (R, Menu or the done screen's button) starts the round over for both: the guest's asks the host, which decides.

**The protocol** (`coop.ts`) follows the root `CLAUDE.md`: guests send intents, the host decides and sends results. Every message is checked as it comes off the wire, and one that isn't a message of the protocol fails loudly. Host to guest:

- `start`, carrying the course, as a course spec. Both devices build the course from it.
- `full`: the guest is turned away, because the game already has its partner or has started.
- During play (see Online co-op): `input`, an input in the host's final order, the guest's own carrying the number the guest gave it; `confirm`, a tick before which every input is final, with the run's fingerprint there; `pong`, the reply to a ping, with the host's tick; and `restart`.

Guest to host, during play: `input`, one of the guest's own, numbered and stamped with the guest's tick; `restart`, asking for one; `ping`, for the clock; and `desync`, saying the confirmed runs disagree. The guest's `input` and `restart` carry the round (how many restarts there have been), so the host drops one meant for a round it has already restarted: an expected race, not a bug. A message from anyone but the seated partner fails loudly; play messages arriving after the round stopped are dropped, for the same reason.

**The URL switches.** `view.ts` resolves the URL to one screen. `?play=solo` skips the lobby into solo play, so loading the page straight into the game still works, and `?play=local` opens local co-op's join screen; any other `play` value fails loudly. The dev views replace the game and win over `?play`: `?garage` (the fleet), `?world` (the world preview), `?sounds` (the sound audition), `?music` (the music ideas), `?frog` (the frog preview) and `?replay=<name>` (a replay), in that order of precedence. `?replay` with no name fails loudly, and so does a name no replay has, listing those there are.

## Co-op

Solo is fully playable; co-op is optional. It runs over PeerJS through the repo's shared PeerJS wrapper (see Multiplayer in the root `CLAUDE.md`).

**The philosophy:** co-op is meaningful only if the players can't each do their own thing; they must coordinate to succeed. The inspiration is Brain Wall's pairs, two people forming together a shape neither could form alone.

The rules for the team are built (`run.ts`), and local co-op plays them: two players on one computer, each on a half of the keyboard or a controller (see Controls and Lobby), with both frogs drawn and the camera following the team over the middle of the road, and online co-op plays them over a peer room (see Online co-op). Co-op's rule tests build their rows by hand from real traffic.

**Sessions.** A session owns a run: its course, its input log and its stepping. Solo is a local session with one player; local co-op is a local session with two, every input applied at once at the run's current tick and logged there. The game sends each input to the session by player slot and draws whatever run the session holds, so online co-op's sessions, owning the log the host orders, sit behind the same interface with no change to the rules, the drawing, the sound or the input sources. A test plays two players' random inputs through a local co-op session with uneven frames and replays its log to the identical run.

**The co-op course** (`coop-course.ts`). The row stream builds rows for one frog, so local co-op plays a course designed by hand for Sprout's L and Splash's J, with no gates. It is a teaching ramp: one idea a row, then the ideas combined, so the coordination is the puzzle and the shapes stay readable. The course, not the rules, says how many lanes the road has (see Courses and medals); the rules' lane count comes from the course through the run, as the world's and the camera's do.

- **Ten lanes,** two even halves of five. The frogs start as mirror images of each other and the L and the J are mirror images too, so a row mirrored about the middle gives each frog the same task mirrored: the early rows' main way of reading at a glance. Nine lanes start the frogs off-centre, so no row is symmetric for both; seven leave no room for an opening each with vehicles between. Wider would add lanes to read without adding ideas.
- **Side by side, each its own opening:** jumping together (the jump is the team's), hopping together, and each standing up into its own slot.
- **One opening for both, side by side:** noses together in a gap between two low tunnels; making room, where one frog must move aside before its partner can reach the space (each way); and a bridge, the two pieces' legs either side of a snowplough.
- **One up, one down:** one frog stands up on a low vehicle's end with its foot on its partner's foot, Sprout and then Splash, then one lies across a low vehicle onto its partner's foot. A partner underneath holds up a frog whose hop runs out, so a hop pressed early is forgiven: the perch in its simplest form.
- **Combined:** one up, one down, away from where the frogs stand, so both must cross the road; both up at once, in their own notches; and the finale, both up back to back between two carts' feet, its only way through. Both-up rows leave nothing under either frog until the row arrives, so the two hops must be timed together.
- **Readable first:** an opening's shape shows its answer, the vehicles outlining where each frog goes. Each row names the answer it was designed around; others may pass too, and the analysis counts them (see Proving courses), from hundreds in the first rows to one at the finale.
- **Reading room** shrinks as the course goes: the early rows' gaps are longer, the last ones the stream's own. The finish stays the tuning's, and comes after every row, even for a team jumping flat out. A slow team meets more rows than the course has, so once all have been met the last eight go round again.
- **Tests** hold every row's answer to passing it, the frogs clear of each other, and the whole course to being proved in its answers to the finish.

- **The frogs move along the road as one.** The team has one depth: every frog's front face is there, so they are always side by side. Either player's jump forward or back moves the whole team, so the pace is the team's to agree. The held jump is the team's too: a newer jump press, either player's, replaces the held one, as held keys do in solo, so when both hold, the newest press wins. A player lets go only of their own key: letting go of a jump the partner's newer press replaced changes nothing, and losing focus lets go of only that player's keys. Hops, slides and turns are each frog's own, and each player has their own held slide, on its own cadence; the held keys repeat in the order they were pressed, whoever pressed them.
- **The frogs are solid to each other,** like vehicles, on the road, on the overpass and in the air: no slide, turn (kicks included) or hop may put one frog's cells into the other's, and they never pass through each other. A frog is solid to its partner's cells as they are now, a hop's raised cells included: a grounded frog may slide in under a partner that is up, wherever the cells under it are free. A frog that is up can't land into its partner, so once its airtime is over it stays up, standing on its partner, and lands the moment its partner has moved out from under it. The jumps move both frogs together, so they never meet each other; a jump back is refused if it would put either frog's cell into a vehicle of a row the team has passed, or either frog across a gate's post.
- **Turns follow Tetris rules.** A turn that collides with the corridor edge, an overlapping vehicle, a gate's post or the partner tries Tetris-style kicks before failing (see Turning in The motion model), so a player can twist into a notch the partner forms and lock the two pieces together, as in Tetris. The kicks are the same for solo.
- **One judgement for the team.** A row reaches both frogs at the same instant, since they share a depth, and passes only if every frog fits; if any doesn't, the whole team is bonked back together, the row counting one pass or one bonk for the team. A bonk lands no hop: a frog in the air when the team is knocked back stays up for the rest of its airtime. Riding and the fit outline stay per frog: a frog that is up while a row overlaps the team rides it, while its partner may stand on the road between the same row's vehicles.
- **Shared openings are the puzzle.** A row may give each frog its own opening, or one opening that both must fit through at once, interlocked; since the frogs can't overlap, an opening shaped as two pieces fitted together lets the team through only in that interlock. Hops may be needed by both frogs, or by one. The `?world` preview's co-op rows are such openings, and a rule test plays the team through each of them.
- **Each frog has its own piece.** Partners start with different pieces, and a gate changes whichever frog goes through it (see Gates). The stream will track the pieces the team could hold, as pairs, and build rows for every pair (the course-format step).
- **Start and finish together.** Each frog starts on the overpass in the middle of its own share of the road (the road split evenly between the frogs), in its piece's first rotation no wider than that share, so the frogs start side by side, never overlapping; solo's share is the whole road. Either player's first jump forward drops the team off the overpass together, refused if any frog would land in a vehicle. The team crosses the finish together, when the shared depth reaches the line: every held key lets go and every hop lands, but a frog standing on its partner's raised cells stays up. The run's done state, its passes, bonks and time are the team's.
- **Every input feels immediate,** for both players, in local co-op on one device and online alike: neither player ever waits on the network to see their own frog act. The host is the authority. One system serves both: the rules are a pure function of the seed and the players' timed inputs, each device runs them at once on its own inputs, and the host's ordering of inputs is final. A device that learns of an input late re-runs the rules from that input's time and corrects to the host's result. A test plays two players' interleaved inputs through bonks, riding, gates, the drop and the finish, and replays the log in chunks of every size to the same state.
- The rules are written for a list of frogs, and solo is a list of one: one frog, the rules as they always were. The pass test, frog-to-frog solidity, the kicks and the shared depth all work on that list, and row generation will, so co-op is never retrofitted onto solo-only systems. The rules take inputs for players 0 to n-1 of an n-frog run and throw for any other.

**Online co-op** (`online.ts`, `rollback.ts`) is two sessions, the host's and the guest's, behind the session interface, with the shared peer module carrying their messages (see Lobby for the protocol). The rules, the drawing, the sound and the input sources don't know it is online.

- **The clock.** Online, the clock is real time on both devices: a frame is never clamped, as solo's is, so a tab back from the background catches up with its partner, who played on. The host's run starts at tick 0 as it sends `start`, the guest's as `start` arrives. The guest keeps its clock with the host's from round trips: it pings on the start and at every confirmation, the host answers with its tick, and the guest takes the host's tick now to be that tick plus half the round trip, measured on its own clock in ticks. A drift of a tick is left alone, a few are pulled in a tick a frame (running one tick more or fewer), and more than ten at once, holding still to wait for the host when ahead. Each device's game starts as its scene loads, so the first round trip mostly corrects that. The pings ride on the confirmations, events both sides already have; there is no timer.
- **The input order.** Each player's inputs apply at once on their own device, stamped with its tick, as in a local session. The host's order is final: its own go into the log at once, and the guest's at the tick the guest stamped, as they reach it, after every input stamped then or earlier, the host's run rolling back to put them there. An input stamped before the host's latest confirmation is moved to the host's tick now instead, never dropped. The host sends every input on in that order, the guest's own too, and the guest builds the host's log from them.
- **The rewind window.** The host confirms the run every half second (`CONFIRM_TICKS`), half a second behind its own tick (`REWIND_WINDOW`): every input before the confirmed tick is final, so the guest's inputs are taken at their own tick for at least half a second, longer than any working connection's one-way trip. The guest, until the host's word, runs the log it expects: the host's inputs, and its own still on their way after the host's at each tick, since they reach the host after them. When the host sends one back at another tick or in another order, the guest re-runs from there; one of its own the host has confirmed past leaves its run until the host sends it back at the host's tick.
- **Rollback.** Each device keeps the run every tenth of a second (`SNAPSHOT_TICKS`) since its last confirmed run, each before its tick's inputs, so a late input re-runs from the nearest snapshot before it, never from the start. A confirmation drops the older ones. A rule test inserts random inputs at random past ticks and holds the run to a straight replay of the whole log every time.
- **Corrections, drawn.** The drawn frog already glides toward its rule state, so a late input of the partner's is drawn as the partner's move, a moment late, and the camera follows the drawn team as ever. The rules make a bonk afresh as they re-run, so the drawing tells a bonk by its tick and depth, not by the object holding it, or every correction would replay the last bonk. Sounds are cued from state changes, so a correction that adds or moves a bonk or a pass plays its sound (see Open questions).
- **Out of sync.** With every confirmation the host sends a fingerprint of the confirmed run (FNV-1a over its rule state). The guest checks the run its copy of the host's log gives at that tick against it. The rules are deterministic, so a difference is a bug, and it fails loudly: each device logs its log as a replay (the course spec, the log and the tick) in the browser's console, for the replay tools to play, the guest tells the host, and the round stops for both, back in the waiting room with a note.
- **Messages in order.** Each side numbers its messages from 0 and handles them in that order, whatever order they come in. PeerJS's reliable channel is ordered already; the numbers keep the protocol from depending on it. A number arriving twice fails loudly.
- **Tests.** A simulated network joins a host and a guest session in simulated time, a tick at a time, each message delayed by its own seeded amount, so messages overtake each other. Over a LAN, the internet and a phone hotspot, on several seeds, with both players' random inputs, both devices end with the identical log, and runs a straight replay of the host's log gives. Others hold the guest's clock to the host's, an input taken at the guest's tick by rolling the host back, an input later than the window moved to the host's tick, restarts from either side with the old round's inputs still on the wire, a partner vanishing mid-game, and a desync failing loudly on both devices. End-to-end tests host and join in two browsers: each player's key moves their own frog on both screens, a restart from the guest restarts both, and either player leaving is told to the other.

## Proving courses

"Seeing is believing." A run is a pure function of its course and its input log, so a proof that a course can be beaten is an input log, and replaying it in the real rules is the verification. The tools are in `proof/`; they are the foundation of Claude's toolbox (`ideas/course-authoring.md`).

- **The solver** works row by row in the real engine. Once the last row has gone by and every hop is down, the planner searches the team's poses breadth first (slides, turns with their kicks, hops, waits, and a frog standing on its partner) for the fewest slots to a pose that passes the next row, on the real piece rules, between rows, where only the road's edges and the partners are in the way. Time runs in slots of one action by one player (14 ticks, so a hop's airtime is five), and the row arrives half a slot after the plan's last action, so every hop the pose needs is still up. The solver jumps forward a held key's repeat apart for as long as the plan still has room before the row, plays the plan's actions a slot apart, and checks after each that the engine's team is the plan's: a difference, or a bonk in a pose the plan passes, is a disagreement between the planner and the rules, and fails loudly. At a row with a gate it tries keeping the pieces first, then each frog through it, and backs up to the last gate when a later row has no route, so a needs-gate row is met holding the gate's piece. It goes on to the finish, or a set number of rows, and replays the whole log from the start to confirm it.
- **It is bounded.** A row's search can only visit the team's finitely many poses, and throws past a cap; the whole search stops after a cap on row searches. A row with no route is reported by its index with every way tried, never hung on.
- **Designed answers.** Given a designed course's answers (the prove CLI gives the co-op course's), each row's plan must end in the row's answer rather than any passing pose, so the proof plays the course as designed and shows its answers can be reached in time.
- **How hard a row is** (`analyse.ts`): the number of team poses that pass it, out of every pose the team can reach between rows, counted once per shape. One is a single answer; hundreds, a row with room to spare. The CLI prints it per row.
- **Its limits.** It plans only between rows, so a route needing a move while a row overlaps the team (sliding along a passed row's opening, riding into the next) isn't found. It never jumps back, so it proves a course can be beaten, not how a player recovers from a bonk. It plays at one fixed pace, not the fastest, so its times can't set medals yet. A slot is one action by one player, where two players act at once, so a row with far to travel takes it longer than a team, and its fewest-slot route may be one no player would choose: a turn kicked two lanes through the partner, swapping the frogs' sides, which the rules allow.
- **Readable routes.** Each row's route is printed as steps in words ("Sprout hops; Splash slides left"), with the row's face drawn in ASCII at each stage (`#` a vehicle, `.` open, `:` a gate's lanes, `1` and `2` the frogs, `x` a frog's cell where a vehicle will be) and the inputs that play it.
- **The CLI:** `npm run frogmino:prove -- coop` (or `solo`, or `all`), with `--rows N` to stop after N rows, `--length U` for a longer stretch of the stream, `--brief` for a line per row, and `--save [NAME]` to keep the proof as a replay (`proof-<course>` by default) in `proof/saved-replays.ts`. It exits non-zero when a course has a row with no route.
- **Replays.** `?replay=<name>` plays a named replay (a course spec, an input log and the tick it ends at, as plain data) in the real game scene through a replay session: the game sees only a session, as if the players pressed those keys at those ticks. It plays at real speed, a half or a quarter, or paused (P), and restarts (R). A test replays every named replay in the rules, so a rule change that breaks one fails loudly; the proofs are then re-saved with the CLI.
- **The perch** (`?replay=perch`) is the co-op course's proof up to the row where Sprout goes up, played with two jumps fewer before that row: the row comes later than the plan's hop allows for, so Sprout's hop runs out with Splash's foot under its own, and Sprout stands on Splash until the row arrives. It is made from the saved proof, so it follows when the proof is saved again, and tests hold it to standing on Splash and passing with no bonk.

## Traffic

Obstacles are shaped vehicles on a road, not walls. "Wall" is prototype vocabulary and will go away.

- **For now, vehicles arrive lined up in rows at one speed.** Side by side as one row, they leave a gap the frog must match to pass: the Brain Wall moment.
- **A row is vehicles from the 19, side by side, one per lane.** Each vehicle (see The fleet) takes the lanes its silhouette spans, no two share a lane, and lanes may stay empty. There is no stacking yet. A row's solid cells are its vehicles' cells; everything else within the four rows is the opening. So the pass test, the bonk, a row's solidity while it overlaps the frog, riding and the stream of traffic all work on a row exactly as on a wall.
- **No filler vehicles.** Smaller one-, two- and three-cell fillers were tried in analysis: they added clutter and didn't close the gaps they were meant to.
- **Rows are composed answer-first.** The composer picks the intended answer (a rotation, a column, and whether it hops) for each piece the row is for, fills lanes one vehicle at a time with vehicles that keep the answers' cells open, then counts how many distinct poses of each piece the finished row lets through. It keeps the row only if each count suits the difficulty, and no piece it must refuse passes at all, and tries again otherwise. A row with a gate keeps the gate's lanes clear, its answers wholly beside the gate, and counts only the ways through that keep the piece, wholly outside the gate: a pose entirely in the gate is a transformation, not a fit, and one across a gate's post is a bonk, not a way through. Easy rows let 4 to 14 poses through, medium 2 to 5, hard 1 or 2, and a hard row lets through none of the placements that passed the row before it, so the frog must change something. The harder the row, the more it prefers vehicles that close the open cells beside the answer. A raised answer must need its hop: a row built for several pieces raises all their answers or none, and a raised row is kept only if no grounded pose of any of them passes. Every row is passable by each of its pieces, because its answers are.
- **Known gap: rows rarely force the frog to lie flat.** A pose held down by a ceiling needs an overhang reaching over it from a neighbouring lane, and one vehicle per lane seldom gives one. If that is missed in play, the idea to try is vehicles carrying vehicles (stacking): analysis showed it raises the poses a row can force from 129 to 202 of 210.
- **Mixed speeds and staggered vehicles are tabled.** Staggered vehicles the frog weaves between, as in Frogger, and vehicles travelling at different speeds were part of the direction. The design problem that tabled them: getting vehicles at different speeds to arrive lined up into a matching row at the right moment is hard. Two v1 rules are proposed for when they are revisited: within a lane, a vehicle nearer the frog is never slower than one behind it; and a vehicle keeps a constant silhouette along its length, with solid sides. The earlier idea that a hop onto a vehicle stands on it is adopted, as riding (see The motion model).
- **Vehicle shapes are silhouettes chosen for gameplay,** and the art serves them: see The fleet.

## The fleet

The traffic is exactly the 19 fixed tetrominoes: every rotation of the seven pieces, with rotations that look the same counted once (I 2, O 1, S 2, Z 2, T 4, J 4, L 4). Each is its own vehicle, with an id made of its piece and the first rotation that gives its shape, such as `J2`. A vehicle drives head-on at the frog and never rotates. Its front silhouette, as the player sees it from behind the frog, is exactly its piece's four cells on the lane × row grid, resting on the road. Funky shapes, such as a long bar on top of a single wheeled cell, are intended.

The art serves the shape: fun, quirky vehicles that never hide which four cells they fill.

- **The silhouette is sacred.** Seen head-on, a vehicle's solid volume fills exactly its four cells. Nothing reaches into an empty cell of its bounding box or a neighbouring lane: not wheels, mirrors, antennas, exhausts, cargo, flags or smoke, because an intrusion would lie to the player about the opening. The ground clearance under a vehicle's lowest cells holds its wheels, which reach down to the road, and nothing else; nothing hangs in the clearance under an empty lane. Details stay inside the cells' volume, standing proud of an outer face by no more than the surface-detail tolerance in `vehicles/parts.ts`, a few hundredths of a cell. A unit test checks every detail's front-projected bounds against its vehicle's cells.
- **A vehicle never crosses its lane lines.** Its two outer sides, on the lane lines either side of it, stand two paint layers inside the lines (`SIDE_INSET` in `vehicles/parts.ts`), and nothing on them, wheels included, stands prouder, so everything it draws stays within its own lanes. Two vehicles side by side then share no space, and no face of one can meet a face of the other however each rumbles, since the rumble never moves a vehicle across the lanes; the same holds for the frog and a gate's post beside it. Details that crossed the line were what flickered between neighbours: a side band running the whole length ends in the neighbour's front and back faces, and hubs and hatches poke through the neighbour's faces as the two bounce out of step. Unit tests hold every vehicle's body and parts within its lanes, and scan every pair of vehicles the road has room for side by side, fronts lined up, at rest and rumbling on their own.
- **Cells stay countable.** Every body cell carries the darker inset border the walls and the frog have: all round on its front and back, and along the seams on its sides and top, so the border never stretches with the length. Details sit inset from the cell edges so the seams show, and colour blocking by cell (a deck, an awning, a load) helps the count.
- **The silhouette is constant along the length.** Each body cell is one solid box the vehicle's whole length (its outer side a hair inside the lane line, as above), two or three cells to suit its archetype, so the opening is the same wherever along the vehicle the frog is. The lengths are the fleet's, in `fleet.ts`, and the rules keep each vehicle solid for exactly its length (see The motion model).
- **No Tetris colours.** The paints are the fleet's own design tokens in `globals.css`, and none belongs to a piece: not the standard seven-colours-per-piece mapping, and never one main colour for every rotation of a piece (a test checks this). Vehicles are told apart by archetype, silhouette and decoration, not colour alone. This is deliberate distance from Tetris's protected look; see the legal note in `ideas/tetris-worlds-look.md`.
- **Procedural, in the game's look.** Vehicles are boxes and discs built in code and merged per paint, in the plain lit materials and lights of Look. There are no models, textures or fonts, so there is nothing to credit.
- **No depth fighting.** Paint lies on a face in layers of one thickness (`LAYER` in `vehicles/parts.ts`): a detail on a decal sits a layer higher and inset from its edges, and stripes of two paints lie side by side in one layer. A layer is more than the depth buffer resolves where the traffic is farthest, and two fit within the surface-detail tolerance. A unit test runs the world's face scanner (`world/coplanar.ts`) over every vehicle and every pose of the frog for faces of different paints closer than that.
- **It rumbles as it drives.** Each end of a vehicle settles a little on its springs and springs back, quickly, with its own rates and phases seeded per vehicle (by row and lane in the game), so the traffic never bounces in step; the two ends bouncing apart tip it a hair along the road. It is a pure function of the frame clock. The whole vehicle moves, wheels and all, and only ever down, by at most the rumble's drop in `vehicles/rumble.ts`: its wheels press that little into the road and never lift off, and it never moves across the lanes. The drop is within the surface-detail tolerance, and a unit test holds every vehicle's body and parts at the rumble's extremes to its cells. The wheels don't spin: a plain drum with a centred hub looks the same turned. The garage's turntable and the world preview's traffic rumble too; the garage's head-on view stands still, so it stays exactly the silhouette.

## The frog

The player is a frog shaped like its piece. It has to read as the same frog character in all 28 poses (seven pieces, four rotations), change piece at a gate without becoming someone else, and never lie about its cells. The fleet's art rules all apply: the art serves the shape, the silhouette is sacred, cells stay countable, and it is boxes, balls and discs built in code in the plain lit materials of Look, with the shared cell border and no outlines or toon shading, so there is nothing to credit.

- **The features stay upright; the head is the top row.** Whatever the piece and rotation, the head is the piece's top row, with two eyes on top of it, centred on the row's middle half a cell apart, and the legs are under its bottom row, on the road. The eyes never rotate with the piece. A frog whose eyes turned with it would be upside down or on its side in half its poses, with its eyes underneath or on its far side, hidden from the camera, and the eyes would jump round the shape on every turn. Upright, the eyes are always at the top of the shape, where the player is already looking to read the next row, and the frog always sits the right way up, like a real frog, whose eyes are its highest point. A turn still swings the whole body; the features are already seated for the new pose.
- **Seen from behind, the eyes bulge up out of the head, never above it.** The camera sees the frog's back and top, and a frog from behind is two eye bumps on its head: that is the look to keep. Eyes above the head would reach into the empty cell above it. The head's cells are shaped along their depth instead, which the silhouette doesn't see: a thin full-height snout ridge across the front keeps each head cell a full square head-on, the back of the head sits lower, and the eyes bulge up out of that dip, just behind the snout, their highest part (the pupil, anywhere on its dazed circle) at exactly the cells' top.
- **The pupils face the camera; the frog faces down the road.** The frog faces the oncoming traffic, and the player sees it from behind and above, so the eyes sit high on the head, as a tree frog's do: each eye is a skin bump with its eyeball bulging out of the bump's top, toward the outside and a touch back, and its pupil in the middle of that bulge, looking up and out. From the gameplay camera each pupil shows plainly, on the far, outer side of its eyeball, which reads as a frog seen from behind looking ahead and out; the turntable shows it from the front looking up. Unit tests hold each pupil turned to the gameplay camera's direction, on the far side of its eye in the camera's picture, and in its view past the bump and the head, open and dazed. The bar pupil lies across the road, so seen from above it is a bar across the eye.
- **No eye part ever runs alongside another paint.** Balls of different paints that meet at a shallow angle flicker through each other, as a white barely poking out of its lid did. So each eye's white stands well out of its bump and its pupil out of its white, each crossing the surface beneath it steeply, open and dazed. A blink sinks the eyeball into the bump until it is hidden, and the pupil, which stands proudest, shrinks away ahead of the white so it never shows on the lid; the dazed lids droop only as far as the white's rim stays steep. Unit tests sample the eyes' surfaces in every distinct placement and hold them to that.
- **The legs stand in the ground clearance.** Everything drawn in the cell grid is lifted by the ground clearance (see Look), so feet and wheels touch the road while the rules stay unchanged; the frog takes it as a parameter, the shared clearance by default. The frog's legs live only in that gap, only under its bottom row, and face down the road with the frog: two small front feet, and two hind legs at the row's ends, each folded under the body as a sitting frog's is, the haunch along the body's underside and the long webbed foot on the road beneath it, from the heel at the back to the toes fanned out ahead and to the side. The body hides all but the back of the gap from the gameplay camera, so the camera sees each haunch with its heel below it, never toes; feet whose toes reached back into that strip, toward the camera, read as a frog facing the wrong way. With no clearance it has no legs. The hind legs kick out on takeoff, stretching back along the road and lifting off it, never across or up, so seen head-on they only ever shrink within the gap; then the legs tuck away into the body for the rest of the hop, so a frog riding a vehicle sits on its roof rather than standing in it, and on landing they come down still kicked out and fold back. A move's small hop kicks them out a little and folds them back as it lands. The drop and the finish leap are hops, so they kick and fold too.
- **Its hind legs can't sit on its sides.** A sitting frog's haunches bulge from its sides, but seen head-on the frog may fill only its cells and the gap under its bottom row, and along the road only its own depth, which the rules hold solid. So a haunch on the side could only be carved into the back corner of an end cell, which splits that cell's back into two bordered faces, so it would count as two cells. The legs stay folded in the gap instead. A carved corner that keeps one border round the cell's back would need a notched-cell shape of its own, with its own border mapping: a renderer change, the owner's call.
- **Cells stay countable.** Every cell carries the cell border, and every cell's back, which always faces the camera, carries the same marks, as do exposed tops away from the head: count the spots, count the cells. The mouth, a wide dark line across the front of the head, and the throat under it are on the front, seen on the turntable.
- **Distinct from Tetris.** It is plainly a frog, with eyes, spotted skin and webbed feet, and its colours are its own tokens in `globals.css`, the same for every piece and far from any Tetris piece colour (a test checks this); see the legal note in `ideas/tetris-worlds-look.md`.
- **Life, kept subtle.** It breathes, blinks now and then (sometimes twice), and puffs its throat, each on a deterministic schedule seeded per frog, so two frogs are never in step. A hop narrows it as it stretches up, kicks its hind legs out and tucks its legs, a landing folds them back and squashes it, each move's small hop narrows it a touch and draws its legs a little up and kicks them a little out, with a touch of a squash as the last of a run of moves lands, and a bonk flattens it against the row along the road, then leaves it dazed for a moment, its pupils circling and its lids drooping. None of it can reach into an empty cell: every cell only ever shrinks toward a point in itself, never grows, and the throat grows only as far as its cell has room for. Unit tests hold every part of every pose, with the throat fully puffed and the pupils anywhere on their circle, to the frog's cells and the clearance under its bottom row, and every motion to its limits.
- **The co-op pair.** The two players are a matched pair of real frogs, not a recolour: P1, Sprout, a leaf-green tree frog with a sunny belly, orange feet, one big round spot per cell and round pupils; P2, Splash, an azure dart frog with a pale belly, pink feet, two freckles per cell and bar pupils. Green and orange beside blue and pink complement each other, and both stand out on the slate road. Their skins differ in lightness as well as hue, and their markings in shape, so they tell apart at a glance even for a colour-blind player, and even as the same piece side by side.
**The frog preview** is `?frog` on Frogmino's URL. It shows every piece in every rotation, from the gameplay camera's direction over the lane × row grid on the frog's front face (lifted by the clearance), or flat from straight behind, where the outline is exactly the silhouette on the grid; a turntable of the selected pose; the two players side by side; and the co-op pair together, as the same piece and as different pieces. Its buttons play a hop (up a cell and down again, as the game's arc does), a move forward, back, left or right (three in a row, as when the key is held, with the game's own glide and small hop, from as far away as that takes the frog to its resting place), a bonk, idle, the drop and the finish leap on every frog at once, and switch which frog the grid shows. The drop and the finish leap run the game's own paths, with the frog's resting place as the road: the drop starts a deck's height up and the drop distance back and lands at rest, and the leap lands a deck's height up, holds a moment and goes back to rest. While any of these is cued, the views pull back to fit its whole path.

**In the game.** The gameplay scene draws every frog of the team with `FrogBody`, each as its player's variant: P1 Sprout, so solo's frog is Sprout, and P2 Splash. Each has its own drawn-frog state (its glides, hop, riding, bonk, drop, finish leap and gate changes), its own silhouette and its own fit outline, and its own life schedule, so the two never blink in step. The camera follows the team's drawn depth, which is the same for both frogs as their rule depth is, and stays over the middle of the road. It is placed by the frog's pivot (`frogPivot`, the middle of its cells), so it turns about the piece's middle, and drawn at the rule rotation at once, easing only the leftover swing, since the features are seated for the rule pose. It plays a hop when a hop starts (and as it drops from the overpass or leaps onto the gantry), a landing when the drawn frog comes down, after riding too, a bonk on a bonk, and a move's small hop on each jump and slide. A gate's new piece is drawn at once in its rule pose, without easing or swinging, and only the piece changes: the depth, the camera, and a hop, bonk, drop or leap in progress carry on as they were. React draws the frog's model a frame after the rules change its piece or rotation, so the drawn frog holds its pose until the model has caught up.

## Levels

v1 levels are generated from a fixed seed, with rows from the answer-first composer (see Traffic). A floor row may let through fits that hop alongside a grounded one; that is good level design, not a flaw. Raised rows come at the rate the composer finds natural, rarely when easy and more often as the difficulty climbs.

**The row stream** (`stream.ts`) is every row the frog will meet, in order, however many that is: row n is a function of the seed and n alone, never of what the player does, so co-op partners and replays see the same traffic. It is composed a few rows at a time, each with its own seeded randomness, and fails loudly if it can't compose a row. Each row's gap from the one before is the spacing, shifted by the seed by up to the jitter.

- **The demo ramp** is its first fifteen rows, spaced for a new player to read each one: five easy, five medium, five hard. Its gates come at rows 3, 9 and 12 (counting from 0). The first is optional: nothing after it needs its piece. Row 10 needs the second gate's piece, the very next row, and row 14 needs the third's, two rows on.
- **The waves** carry on after the ramp, five rows each: a medium row, then a gate in another medium row, two hard rows and an easy one. The seed picks whether the wave's gate is needed by the next row, by the one after, or not at all, a third of the time each; a frog that could already hold either of two pieces meets a gate needed by the very next row. So one row in five has a gate, and about three gates in four are needed.

**The pieces the frog could hold** are tracked along the stream: the start piece, then each gate adds its piece, since the frog may or may not go through it. Every row lets every one of them through a normal opening, keeping its piece, except a needs-gate row, which lets through only the latest gate's piece and so brings the pieces back down to that one. Builder rules keep the pieces few: a gate only offers a piece the frog can't already hold, a gate met holding either of two pieces is needed by the very next row, and a needs-gate row comes at most two rows after its gate. So the frog could hold at most three pieces, only ever at a needs-gate row, and no row is built to let through more than two. A gate's piece is the first, in an order the seed shuffles, whose rows the vehicles can build. Shutting pieces out takes a tight row, so needs-gate rows are hard.

## Look

Fun, quirky and colourful, in keeping with Novelty World: bright, flat and cartoony. Inspiration: see `ideas/tetris-worlds-look.md`.

- **Cells touch,** in walls and the frog, with no gaps between cubes. Every cell face has a darker inset border so the grid still reads.
- **Plain lit materials,** with no cel shading and no outline around shapes: toon bands and an inverted-hull outline were tried and dropped. The cell borders are what keep shapes and cells readable.
- **Lighting is simple:** one ambient light and one directional light. Richer lighting (postprocessing, ambient occlusion) is deferred.
- Walls are solid and opaque at all times.
- **The ground clearance.** Everything drawn in the cell grid stands a quarter of a cell above the ground under it: the vehicles, the frog and the fit outline. It leaves room for a vehicle's wheels and the frog's legs to reach down to the road. It is purely visual, one constant (`GROUND_CLEARANCE` in `clearance.ts`) that the vehicles, the frog and the structures' headroom all read; the rules count rows from the top of it.
- **The frog's silhouette.** Wherever a vehicle, the overpass or the finish gantry hides the frog from the camera, its hidden parts show as a flat silhouette in its own token colour, a light tint of each player's skin (Sprout's green, Splash's azure): the frog drawn a second time, in one unlit material that draws only where something is in front of it, after the traffic and the world and before the frog itself. Vehicles stay opaque.

## Theme

The obstacles are oncoming cars and trucks, as in Frogger (see Traffic). Cells blocked above an empty floor cell are fine: they are odd-looking vehicles with overhanging parts, like trailers and cabs. That is why the traffic moves toward the frog rather than the frog running at it. The frog is a frog: see The frog.

## World

The world around the road frames the gameplay and never competes with it: the same rules as the fleet's art. Its theme is a mountain road at golden hour: the mountainside rising on the left with more rocks and trees, the valley side on the right with more guard rails. It is procedural, built from boxes in code in the fleet's plain lit materials, with a fainter version of the cell border on its blocks, and no models, textures or fonts, so there is nothing to credit.

- **The road never ends,** either way. A bonk can knock the frog back past the start, and traffic comes from far up the road, so the road, its roadside and the land are laid out in tiles around the camera and rebuilt as it moves onto a new one. Each tile is laid out from the course seed and its own index alone, so it comes back the same every time. The sky, the clouds and the ground follow the camera. Ahead, the road reaches past the fog's far end, so it fades into the horizon haze rather than ending.
- **The road:** a slate surface over the traffic lanes, with faint dashed lines between the lanes, a step lighter than the road so they help judge lanes without looking like the cell grid or competing with the fit outline. Solid edge lines run along both edges. Beyond each edge are a gravel shoulder and a low kerb, unbroken.
- **Lay-bys** are scenery only: patches of packed gravel beyond the kerb where a driver might stop for the view, with a bench or two. A guard rail runs between each and the road along its whole length, with no gap, and the kerb runs on unbroken, so nothing suggests the frog could go there; the frog can't, since it never leaves the traffic lanes. The world places them from the seed like the rest of the roadside, about one road tile in three, more often on the valley side.
- **The overpass** crosses the road at the course's start: the frog starts on its deck and jumps down to the road, and the traffic drives on under it and away. **The finish gantry** spans the road at the course's end, a chequered beam on chequered towers with pink flags, with a deck the frog can stand on after finishing. Beneath its near face a chequered finish line is painted across the road, and just short of it a pink spring pad with a chevron per lane, which is decoration until the frog leaps from it. Both decks clear the tallest vehicle, lifted by the ground clearance, with headroom, and their pillars stand clear of the lanes and verges. The frog starts on the one and finishes on the other (see The motion model).
- **Readability rules,** which unit tests hold the layout to. Nothing stands on the road, or within a clearance around it, nor on a lay-by's gravel but its benches. Near the road everything stays under the near-height limit, well under a cell, so it never hides a lane or the frog from the camera; anything taller, such as trees and hills, keeps its whole footprint beyond the tall offset from the road's edge. The world's paints are its own tokens in `globals.css`, soft and muted, and none is a vehicle's saturated body paint, so nothing beside the road reads as traffic.
- **Depth.** The land beyond the roadside comes in layers of stepped, terraced hills: grassy knolls, then ridges, then snowy peaks, with two landmarks along the course (a boulder with a frog's eyes, and a mesa shaped like a T). The further a layer, the paler and bluer its paints, and a fog in the horizon's colour carries that on, starting beyond the rows the player reads, so the background recedes and the road and traffic stand out. The camera travels along the road, so each layer's distance sets how slowly it drifts by: that is the parallax.
- **The sky** is a gradient dome from warm haze at the horizon to blue overhead, with a blocky low sun and chunky clouds drifting slowly across. At the gameplay camera's pitch, a wide screen's view ends on the ground well short of the horizon, so the sky and the far peaks show on narrow portrait screens, and the knolls, trees and nearer ridges frame the road on wide ones.
- **Cheap on older phones.** Every list of boxes is one instanced mesh, so the whole world is a handful of draw calls, and the tiles are rebuilt only when the camera crosses into a new one.
- **No depth fighting.** No two faces of the world that look the same way share a plane, since the depth buffer can't tell which is in front and they flicker. Paint lies on a surface in thin layers, paint on paint a layer higher; the chequers on a deck's faces are set into the deck rather than laid on it; and parts that touch meet face to face or only along an edge. A unit test scans the whole layout, over several seeds, for faces sharing a plane (`world/coplanar.ts`). The depth buffer's precision depends mostly on the camera's near plane, which sits as far out as the camera allows (`CAMERA_NEAR` in `components/camera-fit.ts`).

`FrogminoWorld` takes the course length, the course seed and the road's width in lanes: the game passes its course's (solo's is `corridorCols` in `tuning.ts`, the width the row stream is built for), and the whole layout follows whatever width it is given, the road, its markings and verges, the roadside's clearances, the lay-bys, the land and both structures, with the readability and depth-fighting tests run at each width the preview shows. The width is the course's, so it is passed in rather than read from tuning. It draws in the game scene's axes, adds the fog, and relies on the scene's lights. The gameplay scene mounts it in place of its own floor. The scene's old per-jump cross-lines are gone: the world's lane dashes and the steady rhythm of the kerb blocks and reflector posts already give distance, and lines across the lanes read as the cell grid.

**The world preview** is `?world` on Frogmino's URL: the world from the gameplay camera, with a few rows of traffic coming at the player's frog (`FrogBody`, P1 Sprout as an L), which takes a pose passing the next row. It can also show the co-op pair, Sprout as an L and Splash (P2) as a J, side by side at one depth, each in its own lanes. Either drives a road 7, 9 or 10 lanes wide, with the gameplay camera's field of view fitted to it, for judging which width reads right for co-op, phones included: solo stays at its own width, and local co-op plays its own course (see Co-op). Each width and team has its own hand-made rows (`world/preview-rows.ts`); the pair's alternate an opening for each frog with one opening they pass only interlocked, one frog hopping over the other, and tests hold every row to letting its frogs through together, clear of each other. The frogs wait on the overpass, then leap down together along the game's drop and drive up the road, past the finish gantry and on. The buttons pick the road's width and solo or the pair (each starts the drive over), drive the camera up or back down the road (past the overpass, to check the road goes on), fast or paused, restart it, and switch the world off to compare with the bare road. A collapsible camera panel has a slider for each camera knob (height, follow distance, look-ahead), with the pitch and fitted field of view they give, applied live through the gameplay camera's own maths; it resets to `tuning.ts` and copies the values in that file's shape, to paste in. The game itself reads only `tuning.ts`.

## Sound

Sound complements the gameplay and never gets in its way, like the art. It adds a little polish and fun, never overwhelms the player, and must not get repetitive or annoying, so only moments worth marking have a sound. It is made with ZzFX, the MIT micro-library that generates retro sounds in code from arrays of parameters: no audio files, and nothing to credit, since code libraries aren't credited. The palette is soft retro: sine and triangle waves, mostly through a low-pass filter, never harsh square beeps.

**The set.**

- **Hop:** a light springy boing as a hop starts. It can come at most once per airtime, so it is short and quiet.
- **Bonk:** a comedic thud, a falling boink over a low bump, when a row knocks the frog back.
- **Row passed:** a small pop as the frog fits through a row. Each clean pass in a row climbs one note of the major pentatonic scale, up to the octave, and a bonk or a restart starts it over: the reward for a streak, heard rather than shown.
- **Drop onto the road:** a short falling whistle and plop, as the frog drops from the start overpass.
- **Gate:** a soft rising whoosh with a sparkle of quick high notes climbing over it, as the frog goes through a gate and becomes its piece. It plays once per transformation, whichever way the frog went through, and not when a gate gives the frog the piece it already has.
- **Finish:** a short, soft four-note fanfare as the frog reaches the end.

**Slides, turns and jumps are silent.** They are the game's constant input: a player lines up a fit with a burst of slides and turns before every row, and holds a jump key for most of a run, repeating several times a second. A sound on each would be by far the most repeated sound in the game, a drone under everything else, however quiet and varied. What they do is already shown at once by the frog and the fit outline, and forward progress is heard in the pass pops. Refused moves, landings and restarts are silent too.

**Anti-annoyance rules,** applied to every play:

- A small random stray in pitch and volume, from a range per sound, so a repeated sound doesn't sound like a recording. Musical sounds stray least: the pass pop barely, so its scale stays in tune, and the fanfare not at all.
- A minimum gap per sound, measured on the audio clock: a play sooner than that after the last play of the same sound is dropped, so no sound can machine-gun.
- Frequent sounds are the quietest and shortest.

**Playback.** One AudioContext, ZzFX's own. The sounds load as the game opens, before any input: ZzFX is imported, which creates the context, and every sound is rendered once to an AudioBuffer and replayed from it, which is cheap on older phones. They must not wait for the first input: creating a browser session's first AudioContext blocks the page for a tenth of a second or more, and the first input is usually the drop off the overpass, where that showed as a skipped frame. Browsers keep audio off until the player interacts with the page, so a context created before then starts suspended, and the first key press, click or tap resumes it, inside that input event; that first press is heard, the drop included. ZzFX's own per-play randomness is off, since it would be baked into the one rendering; the variation comes at playback instead. A sound triggered before that first input is dropped, not queued.

**Mute.** A small button in the game's top corner, or the M key, mutes all sound. Mute and the master volume are remembered in local storage; if storage refuses, the setting still holds for the visit.

**The sound audition** is `?sounds` on Frogmino's URL. It lists each sound with its trigger, its variation and minimum gap, and its ZzFX parameters, which paste into the ZzFX designer. Each has a play button and a ten-in-a-row button, which plays it ten times at about the busiest rate the game can trigger it (passes climbing their streak), to judge how it wears. It has the master volume and the mute toggle. The designs are tuned by editing `audio/sounds.ts`.

**Triggers.** The rules know nothing about sound. The game view watches the store and turns each state change into sounds (`soundCues` in `audio/cues.ts`): a pass when the run counts a new pass, once per row each time the traffic brings it round (a row going by beneath the frog on the overpass or the gantry is no pass), a bonk when the run records a new bonk, a hop when a new hop starts, the drop when the frog leaves the overpass, the gate when the frog's piece changes (only a gate changes it), and the finish the moment the frog crosses the finish line, before its leap. A restart is silent. An event that shows in the run's state belongs there, as a new cue with a test. An event the state can't show, such as a moment in an animation, calls the trigger API, `playSound` in `audio/sound-board.ts`, with the sound's id, when it happens.

**Music** is at the ideas stage: nothing plays in the game. The candidates are a handful of short themes on the `?music` page, for the owner to listen to and pick from: looping themes for the lobby, the run, the hard waves and co-op, and two jingles, for a gate and the finish. They are made with ZzFXM, Keith Clark's MIT music renderer for ZzFX: songs are data (instruments as ZzFX parameters, patterns of notes, and the order the patterns play in), rendered in code to an audio buffer, so there are no audio files, and nothing to credit but a folk tune a theme arranges (below). ZzFXM has no npm package, so it is vendored as a TypeScript port with its licence header (`audio/music/zzfxm.ts`), handed ZzFX's own sample builder. The instruments keep the sound effects' soft retro palette, a little brighter.

- **Original tunes, borrowed style.** The themes draw on the style of the arcade Frogger music (bouncy major-key marches, oom-pah bass, staccato hops) and Game Boy Tetris (brisk 2/4 minor with a harmonic-minor folk colour, octave-pumping bass, speeding up with the danger), and each card on the page says which traits. They never quote, arrange or closely paraphrase a melody those games used, Tetris's Korobeiniki and the Japanese songs Frogger used included: the same distance from Tetris as the look (see the legal note in `ideas/tetris-worlds-look.md`).
- **Folk tunes are the exception.** A theme may arrange a traditional folk melody in the public domain, as Tetris did with Korobeiniki: Frog Polkka (`frog-polkka.ts`) is the Finnish Ievan Polkka, the main run loop's candidate. It takes the traditional tune only, never a later recording's arrangement or lyrics (Loituma's, or the songs built on it), and credits the tune (`credits.ts`), since a melody is a work the site plays.
- **The page** lists each theme with its use, mood, style traits, key, tempo and length, with play and stop, a loop switch (on for loops, off for jingles), and a copy of the song for the ZzFXM tracker, which plays it a little brighter since its ZzFX has no low-pass filter. It plays through ZzFX's AudioContext, the game's one context, at the sound settings' volume and mute. A theme renders the first time it plays, which takes a moment on a phone: the click only unlocks audio and shows it rendering, and the render starts once the page has painted that.
- **Composing.** A theme is a file of data: its notes are lines of note names, one per sixteenth, with dots to rest and dashes to release, compiled to ZzFXM's format; tests hold every bar to a full bar and every note to its instrument's range.

## Open questions

- Are the bonk knock-back (three jumps) and the held-key repeat interval (0.22 s) right? To be tuned in playtesting.
- **Which road widths make good play,** solo and co-op. A wider road gives a row room for more decoy vehicles and more openings to weigh; a narrower one is tighter and quicker to read. To be found by playing hand-made rows at each width, starting with local co-op.
- **Co-op feel, to playtest:** the newest press winning when partners press different jumps, and how the shared bonk looks with the frogs apart.
- **Local co-op, to playtest:** whether the split keyboard is comfortable (the right half's , . / beside the arrows) and whether the keyboard drops keys with two players on it; the controller mapping (A hop, X B and LB RB rotate, the stick's thresholds); whether two fit outlines in one colour read, or each wants its frog's colour; and the silhouettes' two tints.
- **Touch controls are on hold.** The owner played the drags in all four directions and the centre-tap hop as built; they work as asked but don't feel right yet, and are set aside to come back to. Open when revisited: the axis lock (or tracking both axes at once), whether the centre tap's hop, on the lift, comes soon enough, and whether the hold to keep jumping is missed on long runs.
- **Co-op's rule calls, to playtest:** a frog standing on its partner's raised cells until the partner moves (instead of, say, refusing the slide under it); a frog in the air staying up through the team's bonk; the kick order (centred, then the turn's own way, up to two lanes) and whether a two-lane kick for every piece, not just the I, feels like a teleport; and the start, each frog in the middle of its half of the road.
- **Online co-op over a real network, to playtest:** whether a correction to the partner's frog reads as the partner simply moving a moment late, or jars (a bonk undone or added, the team's depth jumping); whether a correction's bonk or pass sound playing twice is noticed; whether the rewind window (at least half a second) is long enough on a phone hotspot, or inputs get moved to the host's tick, which shows as the guest's own frog correcting; the latency itself on a phone hotspot and over the internet, and how the first second feels while the guest's clock settles; and a backgrounded tab catching up on return.
- **The co-op course, to playtest:** is coordinating fun, or does one player direct the other? Which rows read at a glance, and which only after a bonk? Do the one-up rows read as one frog going up, and is it clear which? Do players find the perch, or only stumble on it? Are the both-up rows' timed hops a fun climax or a chore? Is the reading room right, early and late, and is ten lanes too much road to read? Do players kick through each other, swapping sides, and does it confuse them? Is the course the right length before it goes round again?
- **Gates in the co-op course.** It has none, so far: each gate taken or skipped by either frog multiplies the pairs of pieces the team could hold, and every later row must let every such pair through. A hand-made course can be proven for every pair by the solver rather than by a builder rule, so gates need not wait for the stream's pair tracking. Undecided: whether some rows need one frog to have changed piece, whether to prefer two-gate rows (one per partner), and where gates come in the ramp.
- **Kicks through a partner.** A turn's kick tests only the spot the frog lands in, so a two-lane kick can carry a frog through its partner and swap their sides (the solver does this on a make-room row). That contradicts frogs never passing through each other. Undecided: refuse a kick whose path crosses the partner, or keep it as a move; refusing it means re-proving the co-op course.
- **Online connections through strict networks.** Game data goes peer to peer after PeerJS's signalling, with no relay server of our own, so a strict mobile-carrier or office network may fail to connect. Untested; a relay (TURN) service is the fix if it bites.
- **The solver's reach:** should it also plan moves while a row overlaps the team (sliding through a passed row, riding into the next), and should it race for the fastest route once medals need its times?
- The code rename from wall to vehicle terminology, once the model changes.
- **The gates' look.** The gate's piece shows on a sign on the lintel, which the decks keep to a thin strip above the tallest vehicle, so the icon is small: does it read from far enough away? The posts run the row's length as fins, to match the rules: do they read as the frame's posts? Its violet is close to the fleet's grape paint.
- **Frog poses that may not read as a frog:** the flat I (it looks like a log), poses with a single bottom cell under a wide top, and head-to-one-side J, L, S and Z poses.
- **The frog seen from behind.** Do the pupils read from the gameplay camera, and does the frog still read as facing down the road? Do the folded hind legs and their kick read as a frog's?
- **The moves' feel.** A move glides over the held-key repeat interval with a small hop (its height is `MOVE_HOP_HEIGHT` in `frog/moves.ts`). Is the drawn frog's lag behind the rules short enough to judge a row by, and is the hop subtle enough held? The camera follows the drawn frog, so a held jump moves it in gentle surges.
