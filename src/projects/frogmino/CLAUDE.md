# Frogmino

Brain Wall (the Japanese game show) meets Tetris, with Frogger-style hopping. The player is a frog shaped like a tetromino, and shaped vehicles come at it down a road: side by side, leaving a gap it must match (staggered vehicles to weave between are tabled: see Traffic). It is real-time, seen from behind the frog in real 3D with Three.js (through `@react-three/fiber`). Faked 2D depth was considered and rejected: it is harder to get right than real 3D.

The owner and a friend designed it. What follows is decided unless it sits under Open questions.

The obstacles are rows of traffic (see Traffic). The rules still call a row a wall, from the prototype: a row's vehicles are its solid cells and everything else, up into the open air above them, is its opening, so wherever the sections below speak of walls and openings, they mean a row as the rules see it.

## Code map

- `types.ts`: cells on the wall face, tetromino kinds, the frog, openings, spans of lanes, and the solids of a row as the rules see them: a vehicle, the cells it fills and its length.
- `logic.ts`: pure piece rules, no React and no Three. The tetromino definitions, rotation, piece sizes, whether a piece lies inside a span of lanes, turning about the piece's middle with Tetris-style kicks, and the cells a frog covers.
- `team.ts`: how the frogs of a team move among each other, pure: standing on a partner, support, the hop that lifts what stands on the hopper, slides that carry riders, turns, and falling to the highest support. The run plays these with the vehicles in the way, and the planner on the open road, so the two share every move.
- `run.ts`: pure run rules. The rule state of one playthrough (a team of frogs, one per player, sharing one depth, each with its height and its own hop; the road's width in lanes, the course's; traffic; clock), where each frog starts, the players' actions and held keys, the player inputs and the input log, `step` for moving time on a tick, `playTo` and `replay` for playing a run on, or again from its start, with its log, the helicopter start and the jump out (the drop), with the traffic held still until it, judging rows as they reach the team, keeping each frog out of the vehicles still overlapping it and out of its partners, riding, the pass test, the bonk, jumping back among and through rows already passed, recycling the traffic out of view as the course's next rows, and the finish; and the run's time, read off the drop and the finish (see Courses and medals); `sinceJump` is a run as the course counts it, every tick from the jump out.
- `medals.ts`: the medals, each course's medal times as a type and their check, the medal a time earns, a time as a stopwatch shows it, and the held-jump floor, the time a held jump takes from the drop to the finish with nothing in its way.
- `traffic.ts`: a row of traffic (which vehicles, in which lanes), refusing a row whose vehicles share a lane or leave the road; the row as the rules see it, each vehicle's cells and length; the test of a frog's cells against a row's vehicles; and where a row's back is.

## The wall face

A wall is a grid of cells: columns across the corridor, rows up from the floor, with no top: above the vehicles is open air, as high as anything goes. A row's vehicles fill some of its cells and the rest are open. The frog is a tetromino with a lateral column (its leftmost cell), a rotation of quarter turns clockwise as the player sees it from behind, and a height: how many whole cells above the road its lowest cells are, from 0. It is 0 on the road, and more on a hop, on its partner, or both.

## The motion model

What the rules decide is kept apart from what the player sees.

- **The course runs from a helicopter to a finish gantry** on a road that never ends either way. Rows are placed along it and all move toward the start at the course's wall speed. The frog does not run on its own: it advances only by jumping forward, with no limit on how far ahead it may race.
- **The frog starts aboard the helicopter,** the Lily Lifter, standing on its deck above the start, out of play, while it cruises down the road at the traffic's speed. It can slide and turn there to line itself up, but not hop or jump back. Its first jump forward (W, a drag up on a touch screen, or up on a controller) is the jump out: it leaps onto the road the drop distance ahead, into play, a big leap off the deck and down to the road, and the helicopter, lightened, pops up, brakes, swings round over the valley side and flies off up the road beside it, never over the lanes ahead of the camera. A held key repeats once the leap lands. There is no way back up. The leap is drawn rising well above the deck and falling to the road, and the camera glides forward with it, rising a little to keep the frog in view; tests hold the helicopter's flight off clear of the camera and of the lanes ahead.
- **The course counts from the jump out.** Until the jump the helicopter keeps pace with the traffic, so the rules hold the team and every row still relative to each other: the rows stand where the course lines them up and nothing moves, judges or is recycled. The jump sets the traffic going from where it stands, and the clock starts there. So however long a team waits aboard, it meets the same rows at the same places from the jump on, tick for tick: a rule test plays one team's log with the jump moved later by different waits, before or after lining up, and holds the runs equal from the jump on, as the course counts them (`sinceJump`), and an online test does the same for a guest who started long after the host. The drawing gives the cruise: the road and its scenery go by beneath the helicopter until the jump, and stop there, while the rows and the finish gantry stay in the course's frame (see World).
- **The road is empty between the helicopter and the first row.** No row is ever placed between the deck and the landing: the first row's front stands a reading room beyond where the frogs land (the tuning's first-row depth, which the rules require beyond the drop and the course checks require of every course's first gap), so nothing is flown over or skipped, and the players see the first row waiting ahead, its fit outline on its face, while they line up. A jump out with a row in between fails loudly, as a malformed course.
- **There is no safe start zone.** A jump back past the start is fine, and so is a knock-back: the road goes on behind it, with nothing standing over it there, and the traffic follows the frog down it.
- **Traffic is a stream, and never runs out.** The run lines up the course's rows in order (see Courses and medals), each its own gap beyond the back of the one before, until the traffic reaches the traffic horizon. A row whose back is the recycling distance behind the frog, behind the camera and out of its view, reappears ahead as the course's next row: its gap beyond the frontmost row's back, and never nearer the frog than the traffic horizon, beyond the world's fog. So a row never appears or vanishes on screen, however far ahead or behind the start the frog is, and the frog meets the course's rows in their order, however fast or slow it goes. Both distances are knobs in `tuning.ts`.
- **Rows are spaced back to front.** Every vehicle's front in a row lines up; each reaches back its own length from the fleet (two or three), so the row's back is wherever its longest vehicle ends. The spacing is the gap from one row's back to the next row's front, so the time to read the next row doesn't depend on how long the vehicles are.
- **Crossing the finish line ends the run.** A jump forward that reaches the finish line, under the finish gantry, crosses it: the rules stop there, so no row can judge or bonk the frog again, every held key lets go and every frog comes down onto its support, its partner or the road. The frog springs up from the spring pad onto the gantry's deck in a celebratory leap, spinning once on the way, and stands there while the traffic keeps driving underneath. The done screen comes once it lands.
- **The rules play a team of frogs,** one per player: wherever this section says the frog, each frog of the team is meant (see Co-op for what the team shares). Each frog has its own piece, rotation, column, height and hop; the team has one depth, one held jump, and a held slide for each player.
- **The frog's rule state is discrete** (column, rotation, depth, height, and when its own latest hop started) and changes the instant a key is pressed: the input applies at once, at the rules' current tick, with no wait for the next. One press is one action, and the operating system's key repeat is ignored; only a held jump or slide key repeats (see Controls). Its depth changes a jump or a bonk at a time.
- **The drawn frog follows its rule state,** and the rules never see the in-between. A jump or a slide is drawn as a move: a small hop that glides from where the frog was drawn to its new place, setting off and settling gently. A move takes the held-key repeat interval, so held moves chain, each landing as the next takes off, and a held jump flows on as a steady run of hops. A turn, and anything else, eases there over the ease duration. While a wall overlaps the drawn frog, it is drawn exactly at its rule state across the face and in height, with no move's hop: an eased turn swings cells through places neither pose covers, and a move made just before a wall arrives may not have finished, so either would draw the frog inside the wall. The one exception is a drawn frog still riding when the rules land it (see Riding). Its depth goes on gliding, since every opening is the same along the row, unless the frog in the pose it is drawn in would be inside a vehicle at the depth it is drawn at (one it has just jumped clear of and slid into the lane of), when it is drawn at its rule depth at once. The camera follows the drawn depth, so that is the only time it jumps.
- **The rules run on a fixed tick,** a hundred a second (`ticks.ts`). Their clock is a whole number of ticks, and every time they keep (a hop's airtime, a held key's next repeat, the drop, the finish leap, a bonk) is a tick. The times in `tuning.ts` stay in seconds, for reading, and become ticks in one place, rounded to the nearest; at a hundred a second every one of them is a whole number of ticks, so the clock changes none of them, and a tick is shorter than a frame at any refresh rate. A wall's depth falls by the wall speed each tick, worked out from where and when it was placed, so no rounding builds up. The frame loop runs as many ticks as real time has passed, carrying the rest of the frame into the next, and clamps a long frame, so returning to a backgrounded tab doesn't lurch the course forward.
- **Inputs are a log.** Every player input (an action, a jump or slide key pressed or let go, every key let go as the window loses focus) is stamped with the tick it applies at and the player's slot, from 0 (the rules throw for a slot with no frog), and kept in the run's input log, in tick order, inputs at one tick in the order they came. A local input applies at once, at the current tick, and goes into the log there. The log is plain data, so a session can insert an input that reaches it late in its place and replay from there.
- **A run is a pure function of its course, its tuning and its log.** Replaying the log from the start reproduces the run bitwise, however the ticks were chunked into frames: a tick per frame, a long catch-up or anything between. That determinism is what co-op's timed inputs stand on. A test plays a stream of rows by inputs alone, through bonks, riding, the drop and the finish, and replays its log in chunks of every size to the same state. A replay runs a tick at a time, and a whole run's takes a fraction of a second; rollback will replay from a recent state rather than the start.
- **Drawing runs between ticks.** Everything is drawn on the drawing's clock, the rules' time plus how far real time has got into the next tick, so nothing visibly steps at the tick rate: the traffic is drawn where the wall speed has it at that moment, and the drawn frog's glides, arcs and knock-back, and the camera following it, run on the same clock. The rules judge a row at the first tick after it truly reaches the frog, so a row can be drawn up to a tick's travel (a fortieth of a cell) into the frog for a moment before it bonks it.
- **Every wall the frog hasn't passed is ahead of it, and every wall it has passed is behind it.** Each rule keeps that true, and it is what makes each arrival judged exactly once.
- **The frog and every vehicle have depth,** as drawn: the frog one cube, each vehicle its own length. A vehicle overlaps the frog while their depth ranges along the course intersect: from the instant the row's front face reaches the frog's until the vehicle's own back face goes by the frog's.
- **A wall is judged when it reaches the frog,** once, at the tick its front has reached the frog's and the overlap begins, against all its vehicles, using the frog's rule column, rotation and height at that tick. The rules move a tick at a time however long the frame, so a long frame can't carry a wall past the frog unjudged, and the height is read at the wall's arrival rather than at the frame's end. Within a tick, things happen in one order: the walls move; a wall reaching the frog is judged, before a frog whose airtime ends that tick falls, since the wall arrived within the tick, at or before its end; then every frog out of its airtime with nothing under it falls, unless a row overlaps the team; then the held keys repeat, in the order pressed; then the inputs stamped with the tick apply. A bonk knocks the frog back from the wall's face where it is at that tick, at most a tick's travel past the frog's own. A jump forward that reaches a wall's face is judged at once in the same way.
- **The frog is solid against the vehicles that overlap it,** each for as long as it overlaps. Having passed, its cells are all inside the opening, and they stay clear of every vehicle until it has gone by: a move, a turn (wall kick included) or a hop that would put a cell into an overlapping vehicle's cells is refused, just as a move off the corridor edge is ignored, while moves that stay clear are fine. Once a shorter vehicle has gone by, its lanes are free, while a longer neighbour still overlaps. So the opening can only grow after the row is judged, never shrink. A jump forward only takes the frog away from a wall it has passed.
- **The bonk.** A wall the frog fits passes around it. A wall it doesn't fit bonks it: the frog is knocked back from the wall's face by the bonk knock-back, a few jumps' distance, however far behind the start that takes it. The wall stays solid and keeps coming, so it bonks the frog again when it arrives unless the frog fits by then or has got clear. A jump forward into a wall the frog doesn't fit is a bonk too. Walls never let through a frog that doesn't fit, and never carry it along: the obstacles are becoming vehicles, and being carried along by one looks wrong. The rows are spaced, back to front, so that a knock-back always lands the frog clear of every row, and never reaches one it has passed, however long its vehicles; the rules refuse tuning that would allow it. That spacing also means no two walls overlap the frog at once.
- **Feedback is for bonks only.** A bonked frog is drawn knocked back along a low arc, from wherever it is drawn: a bonk that interrupts a move (a jump forward into a row's face, or a row arriving mid-glide) finds the drawn frog short of the face, and it never skips forward to the face first, nor does the camera following it. A held jump that repeats mid-knock-back glides on from where the arc has it. The frog itself flattens against the row and is left dazed (see The frog). A pass has no feedback on the frog. The pink bonk flash of the cube frog is gone: the dazed look reads on both players' frogs, where pink suited only the green one.
- **A jump back may go back into a passed row, and through it, wherever the frog fits.** It is refused only if it would put one of the frog's cells into a vehicle of a row the frog has passed, anywhere along the ground the jump covers, each vehicle its own length; then it doesn't happen, just as a move off the corridor edge is ignored, with no bonk. Among a passed row's vehicles the frog is solid against them as usual. A jump back that takes the frog's front back across a row's front makes that row ahead of it again, to be judged afresh when it arrives, so the frog meets it again in its order. Jumping back is faster than the traffic, so a frog can always catch a row it has passed.
- **Height and support.** Each frog has a height, a whole number of cells above the road. A frog stands on its partner when one of its cells is directly on top of one of its partner's, and it is supported when it is at height 0 or stands on its partner. The inspiration is the co-op platformer Kalimba: stacking on your partner and timing jumps together (`team.ts`).
- **The hop.** A frog may hop when it is supported and not in its own airtime. It rises one cell and its airtime starts, and everything standing on it rises a cell with it, and whatever stands on that, and so on: carried, not hopping, so a carried frog's own airtime doesn't start. The hop is refused if the hopper or anything it lifts would rise into a vehicle overlapping the team or into a frog that stays put. Every hop is exactly one cell, and none is allowed aboard the helicopter. So the bottom frog hops alone and carries its partner up; the top frog hops alone off its partner, a cell above it; and the **double jump**: the bottom frog hops, and while it is still in the air the top frog, standing on it and so supported, and not in its own airtime, hops off it, ending two cells above where it stood. The window for the double jump is the bottom frog's airtime.
- **Falling.** A frog not in its own airtime and not supported falls at once to the highest support below it: its partner's top or the road. The lower frogs settle first, so a frog standing on its partner comes down with it when the partner lands. A bonk brings nobody down: a frog in the air when the team is knocked back stays up for the rest of its airtime. The drawn frog rises and falls smoothly along the hop arc between any heights, from wherever it is drawn, so a carried frog moves with its carrier, the double jump springs on from mid-rise, and coming down onto the partner reads as a landing, as coming down onto the road does; a longer fall takes longer.
- **Ride-along.** When a frog slides, every frog standing on it slides with it, if the riders' new places are clear (the road's edges, the vehicles overlapping the team, the other frogs). If not, the slide still happens without them, and they stay where they are, falling if that leaves them unsupported. A turn carries nothing: a frog standing on a turning partner stays put, and falls if the turn leaves it unsupported. The jumps forward and back move the whole team, as ever.
- **Riding.** A frog above its support while a row overlaps the team rides the row: it stays at its height, gliding across the vehicles' low parts, until the last of the row's vehicles has gone by, then falls to its support, however long ago its airtime ran out. That covers a frog whose partner slides out from under it while a row overlaps the team too. This is a mechanic, not a safeguard. It makes the hop's timing forgiving: a hop pressed early, whose airtime would end while the row goes by, still carries the frog across. And it looks like the frog gliding across a car's hood. The riding frog can still slide within the opening, and can jump forward, in the same pose and height, which stays inside the opening, so it clears the vehicles and lands sooner. A held jump or slide keeps repeating while it rides. A jump back follows the usual rule: refused only where it would meet a vehicle. Riding has no visual of its own; the drawn frog simply stays up. The drawn frog rides too: its glide trails the rules, so when a jump forward carries the rule frog clear and it falls, the drawn frog may still be among the row's vehicles. It stays at its height there, gliding on, while that pose keeps clear of them, and falls along the arc once the row has gone by it too, so it never drops into a vehicle or skips ahead to avoid one.
- **Turning** is about the piece's middle, with Tetris-style kicks. A turn tries the piece centred where it turned, then kicked a lane sideways, then two lanes, and takes the first spot that stays on the road, clear of the vehicles overlapping the frog and of its partner; if none is clear, the turn fails. A clockwise turn tries a lane right before a lane left, a counter-clockwise turn left before right, so a turn and its mirror image kick mirror-image ways; turning and turning back in open space returns the piece to its lane. Kicks are sideways only: a frog's height comes from its hops, what it stands on and riding, so a kick that lifted a frog would be a free hop and one that dropped it would cut its hop short. Two lanes is the most any piece needs at the road's edge (the I, between four lanes wide and one). A move off the corridor edge is ignored.
- The rules are pure functions of the run state and its inputs, a tick at a time, run from the render loop; the drawing updates Three objects directly, so React does not re-render per frame.

## The camera

The camera follows the frog from behind and above, over the middle of the road, higher than the tallest wall, so a wall passes beneath it and the camera never goes through one. It is pitched down at the floor a little ahead of the frog: steep enough to keep the frog in view, shallow enough to read the incoming wall's opening and judge how far away it is. A camera too high flattens depth. Its height, follow distance and look-ahead are knobs in `tuning.ts`. It rises and falls with the ground under the frog: the helicopter's deck at the start (at rest, not with the deck's bob, which only the frogs ride), the road, and the finish gantry's deck at the end. Behind the start it simply follows the frog down the road, stacked frogs included: there is no deck there to clip.

## The fit outline

The frog's cells, at its current column, rotation and height, are always projected onto the face of the next wall it will meet, as an outline only: no fit colouring and no toggle. It follows the rule state instantly, so the player reads the fit from where the outline sits against the opening.

## The pass test

The frog passes a wall if none of the cells it covers is in a vehicle's cells, at whatever height: checked when the wall reaches the frog. Any covered cell that meets a vehicle is a bonk, which knocks the frog back. There is no ceiling: an upright I hopping over a one-cell-high vehicle passes, its top cells in the air above the row. Openings are usually larger than the piece; how tight they are is the difficulty dial.

## Controls

- **Move** (slide) left and right across the corridor. Holding the key repeats exactly as a held jump does (below).
- **Rotate** the piece, with Tetris-style kicks off the road's edge, the vehicles and the partner (see Turning in The motion model).
- **Hop**: vertical, exactly one cell, for raised openings and for stacking: from the road or from the partner, never in mid-air, and lifting a partner standing on the hopper (see The hop in The motion model). The pass test decides a hop like anything else: a needless hop is fine as long as the piece still clears the opening. A special rule punishing unneeded hops would be confusing.
- **Jump** forward or back along the course a fixed distance at a time. Jumping forward is the only way the frog advances; jumping back buys reading time. Holding a jump key keeps jumping at a steady cadence, so the player needn't mash it: the first jump comes on the press, then another every held-key repeat interval, timed by the rules' own clock rather than the operating system's key repeat. A bonk doesn't stop the repeat: the next held jump comes a full repeat interval after the bonk, never at once, and a refused jump back doesn't stop it either. Releasing the key, or the window losing focus, lets go.
- **Held slides follow the same rules,** on the same repeat interval: a refused slide (the corridor edge, or a vehicle overlapping the frog) doesn't stop the repeat, a bonk puts the next slide a full interval after it, and it works aboard the helicopter and while riding. A jump and a slide can be held at once, each on its own cadence, so W and D together climb diagonally. A newer key on the same line replaces the held one: pressing D while A is held slides right from then on, and releasing D then stops sliding rather than going back to A, as with W and S.

Hop and jump forward are different actions. There is no action for changing piece: the course sets it (see Pieces).

**Input sources.** Every device reaches the rules through an input source, which turns the device's presses into player inputs (an action, a jump or slide pressed or let go, everything let go) for that device; the seating says which player slot a device drives, and the session applies the input to that slot's frog. In local co-op each device drives the slot it joined (see Lobby). Online, every device drives this player's own slot, Sprout for the host and Splash for its partner. The sources never know which player they are, and the rules never know which device, or whether the player is on this computer or online.

**The keyboard,** by physical key, so the WASD block stays put on other keyboard layouts:

- **Online:** the whole keyboard is the one player's: A and D or ← and → slide, Q and E rotate, Space hops, W and S or ↑ and ↓ jump. R restarts and M mutes.
- **Local co-op** splits the keyboard into two devices, one hand block each, so no key is both players': the left half is WASD to slide and jump, Q and E to rotate and Space to hop; the right half is the arrows to slide and jump, , and . to rotate and / to hop. Each half has every action. R restarts and M mutes, for either player. The legend in the game's corner shows each player's keys side by side. Two players on one keyboard can hit a cheap keyboard's limit on keys held at once (ghosting), which shows as a press going missing.

**Controllers,** on the shared gamepad library (see the root `CLAUDE.md`), standard (Xbox) layout only; a pad whose layout isn't standard drives nothing, since its buttons are known only by number. Each controller is its own device, in local co-op and online alike.

- **The left stick or the D-pad** slides (left, right) and jumps (up forward, down back), held as a key is, so the rules repeat a held direction on the same interval as a held key, and a diagonal holds a slide and a jump at once, like W and D. The stick has the library's radial deadzone (0.2), then holds a way once it tilts past half way along that axis and lets go once it comes back inside 0.35: two thresholds, so a stick resting near one never chatters. The D-pad wins over the stick on its axis. Flicking across from one way to the other lets go of the first and holds the second.
- **A** hops; **X** or **LB** rotates anticlockwise and **B** or **RB** clockwise. **Menu** restarts. **View** opens the game's menu (below). The right stick, Y and the triggers do nothing.
- A pad that disconnects lets go of everything it held. A pad's buttons are read once per animation frame, so a press reaches the rules at most a frame late.
- A button already down as the game first sees a pad (the A or Menu that started the game, or that closed its menu) waits for its next press, so the press made for the screen before is never also a hop or a restart. A direction already held moves the frog at once.

**Menus by controller.** Every screen outside play is a controller's too, on the shared pad navigation (see the root `CLAUDE.md`): the D-pad or the left stick moves a focus round the screen's buttons, by where they sit on the screen, a held way repeating like a held key; A presses the button focused; B goes back where a screen has a back (Back to Novelty World on the lobby, Leave in the waiting room, Back to lobby on the join screen); Menu starts where a screen has a Start. The focus is a ring in Frogmino's sunflower yellow, apart from the green of a course picked, and shows only while a controller was the last thing used: a key or a pointer press hides it, and the first D-pad or A press with it hidden only brings it into view. Each screen shows its controller buttons beneath, while a controller is connected. A screen opened by a controller puts the focus on its starting button at once: the course picked, on a screen with the picker.

- **In play the pads drive the frogs,** and the menus take none of their presses, except on the two overlays over the game. **The done screen** has Restart and the ways out (below), for every pointer and the controller alike; its focus waits for a first press, so a hop pressed as the team crossed the line doesn't restart, and Menu restarts at once, as in play. **View opens the game's menu** over a running game, for whoever holds a controller: Keep playing (focused, and B or View again), Restart, and the ways out. The game runs on under it, since online can't pause, and while it is open the pads drive no frog; a restart from anywhere closes it. In local co-op either pad opens it, and it stills both pads.
- **The ways out** never make a player join again or reconnect a controller to play another course. Local co-op has **Course select**, back to the join screen with both slots still held by the same devices and the course just played still picked, so LB or RB and Menu start the next at once (a controller unplugged meanwhile comes back without its slot), and Back to lobby. Online, **Course select** takes both players back to the waiting room with the partner still seated, for the host to pick and start the next course; the guest's asks the host, which decides (see Lobby). Leave the game leaves the room.
- **The local join screen** gives A and B to joining first (see Lobby).

**Touch,** in online co-op, where each player has a device of their own; local co-op shares one screen, so touch doesn't join it. Every finger on the play area is its own gesture, so one thumb can drag while the other taps. The screen is split into thirds, left, centre and right, by where a finger comes down. A finger that stays within the tap slop (12 CSS pixels) is a tap; one that leaves it is a drag, locked to the axis it left the slop along (sideways if it went more sideways than up or down, otherwise up and down), and it never becomes anything else.

- **Drag** sideways, anywhere, to slide one lane per 32 CSS pixels of finger travel, and up or down to jump forward or back one jump per 32 CSS pixels, like mobile Tetris: a fast drag makes several steps at once, and dragging back takes them back, past where it started too. Each step fires the moment the finger gets there, mid-gesture. At 360px wide the corridor fits within one thumb's sweep, and a sweep up the screen makes a dozen jumps.
- **The axis lock** keeps a sideways drag's drift up or down from jumping the frog, and a jump drag's drift from sliding it: a thumb never drags dead straight, and an unmeant jump forward can bonk the team. Tracking both axes at once (a diagonal drag sliding and jumping, like W and D) was weighed and not built; a second finger can drag the other axis at the same time.
- **Tap** the left or right third to rotate anticlockwise or clockwise, and the **centre** to hop. A tap can only be told from a drag once the finger lifts, so a tap acts on the lift: the hop comes a tap's length (typically a tenth of a second) after the finger lands. Firing the hop as the finger lands would remove that wait, but every drag up or down starting in the centre would hop too.
- There is no press-and-hold: a long still touch is a tap like any other. A drag up covers jumping, a dozen jumps per sweep.

Mouse pointers are left to the keyboard. The play area takes no browser gestures: no scrolling, zooming, pull-to-refresh, text selection or long-press callout. The mute button and the done screen sit above the touch layer and keep their own taps; the done screen has a Restart button, which touch screens need, having no R key.

**The touch hint** shows on touch screens only (a coarse pointer), over the play area: the three zones with their taps, and the two drags beneath. It goes once the player has made three gestures, and the keyboard legend, with a line for controllers, shows only for fine pointers.

## Pieces

The course sets each player's piece, and the frog keeps it for the whole course: there is no way to change piece, no Tetris-style hold and no dealt-piece queue. A co-op course's pair of pieces is its identity (see Designing co-op courses in `ideas/course-authoring.md`). The frog's piece is part of its rule state, and every rule works for all seven pieces: passing, the bonk, solidity, riding, turning with its kick, the fit outline and the drawn frog.

## Courses and medals

**Courses** come mainly from Claude, designing them on purpose; courses made by people are a bonus for later. The plan (one shared course format, Claude's tools for building on it, and later an editor) is `ideas/course-authoring.md`. The designed co-op courses are the format's first form.

**Designed co-op courses** (`coop-courses/`) are plain data, one file per course, gathered in a registry that is the picker's list. Each declares an id (lower-case words joined by hyphens, which names it in a course spec, a replay, the URL and the CLI), a name, a line describing it for the picker, the road's width, each player's starting piece (any two pieces, the same one twice included), its length, its medal times and its rows, each with its vehicles, gap, lesson and designed answer. There are no difficulty tiers: a course is named by its pair and its idea, and the registry lists the courses in the order the picker shows them, the first a course that teaches stacking, which the picker starts on. Every course is checked as the registry is built, so a broken one fails loudly, naming itself, before anyone plays it: a legal id, name and description; two frogs whose pieces each fit their half of the road; legal rows (as `traffic.ts` checks them), each with a gap and an answer for each frog that stays on the road, passes the row and keeps the frogs clear of each other; medal times in order, gold slower than a held jump down the course's own length; and every row met before the finish by a team jumping flat out. A test checks every designed answer is a pose the team can reach, and a slow test proves every course to its finish in its designed answers.

**The courses,** in the picker's order, each named by its pair and its idea:

- **Long and Short** (`long-and-short`, I and O, 9 lanes) teaches stacking, one move a row, each first where nothing simpler gets through: the O stands on the flat I (the perch), hops off it over a van, rides it up as it hops (the carry), and double jumps off it over a crane, while the I stands up in a slot of its own below; then the hop off and the double jump again, crossing to the other side and back.
- **Block Party** (`block-party`, O and O, 8 lanes): two Os can't stack (an O is two cells tall and a hop lifts it one), so it stays on the ground, the gentle one: hop together, shoulder to shoulder, make room, step up side by side.
- **Tea for Two** (`tea-for-two`, T and T, 8 lanes): a T pointing up is a staircase, its arm a step a cell up, its nose two up; the other climbs onto the arm, or is lifted onto the nose, and the rows swap who climbs.
- **Leg Up** (`leg-up`, L and J, 10 lanes): the mirror pair climbs onto each other's backs, hops off each other over a van, each standing in a slot shaped like it, then crosses over to slots on the other side, and back.
- **High Rise** (`high-rise`, I and I, 10 lanes): towers and double jumps: both stand upright in slots, then one lifts the other and the top one double jumps over a garage three cells tall, while the lifter stands up in its slot; who flies swaps, and the flyer crosses over and back.
- **Zig Zag Alley** (`zig-zag-alley`, S and Z, 10 lanes): the bridge and the lean, each climbing the other's step, hopping off over a van, then the crossing and back, the mirror pieces making the climb one way unlike the climb back.
- **Chain Reaction** (`chain-reaction`, T and L, 9 lanes), the last: everything together, leaning, climbing, lifting onto the nose, hopping off, crossing over and back, in a chain whose gaps shrink to a closing burst.

The set is a test bed for the stacking movement before a full set, eventually one course per pair of pieces, is committed to.

**A course's length is its own:** where its finish line is, in units down the road, so a short course ends where its rows do. The run takes the length from the course, as the world, the finish gantry, the finish leap and the held-jump floor do. **After the last row,** a slow team still short of the finish meets the course's rows again in order, from the first, or only its last few where the course says how many, so a short course works as well as a long one.

**The road's width is a level design tool,** not a fixed setting: a course says how many lanes it has, the way it says its rows. Each co-op course picks its own. 7, 9 and 10 lanes all read well in the `?world` preview as the co-op pair, on desktop and on a phone, so the choice is about play, not looks. A course has one width throughout: the world, the camera's fit and the rules all take it once, at the start.

A level is a course with a finish line, run against the clock: gold, silver and bronze times, in the manner of time-trial marble games. There is no endless mode and there are no lives. A bonk costs time: the ground it knocks the frog back is the penalty.

- **Medal times are course data.** Every playable course declares its gold, silver and bronze times, in seconds, beside its road width and pieces, in its own data. The course type requires them, so a course without them doesn't compile, and they are checked as the course is made: gold faster than silver, faster than bronze, each a positive time, or it fails loudly. A designed course's gold must also be slower than a held jump down its own length.
- **The clock is the rules'.** A run is timed in ticks from the team's jump out of the helicopter (the drop) to its crossing of the finish line, read off the rule state, so a replay, however its frames are chunked, and both devices online agree on a time to the tick. Lining up aboard is free, and changes nothing: the clock starts with the jump, the first moment the team's play costs it ground, the course counts from the same moment (see The motion model), and the jump is itself an input, so the time starts at a tick of the log. It stops at the crossing, not when the finish leap lands, since the leap is the same for everyone.
- **The medal earned** is the best one whose time the run is at or under, or none past bronze.
- **On screen,** a timer at the top in the middle shows the time in tenths, between the title and the mute button and above the touch hint's zones, with a medal icon in the colour of the best medal still in reach. The done screen shows the time in hundredths, the medal earned (or none) and the three times, the earned one picked out. Replays show the same. The medals' colours are design tokens of their own.
- **How the times are set.** Nothing yet finds a course's fastest run, so the times are provisional, set from two reference points the prove CLI prints for a course it proves to the finish. The solver's proof is a clean run at a careful pace: it waits for every row to go by before planning the next, and acts one player at a time, so a good team beats it. The held-jump floor, a held jump from the drop to the finish with nothing in its way, is faster than any run holding the key, since every row in the way slows it. Gold is about halfway between the floor and the proof, silver a little under the proof, and bronze a quarter over it, room for a few bonks; each rounded to five seconds. So the solver's own proof earns bronze.

## Lobby

The page opens on the lobby, built on the shared `GameLobby`. It offers three ways in:

- **Local co-op** opens the join screen, for two players on this computer.
- **Host co-op** opens a peer room and lists it on the open-games list.
- **Join** is a click on a game in that list, which joins its room by code under the hood. There is no typing of codes: every hosted game is public, which suits a small site played among friends.

The open-games list is the shared room list (see Multiplayer in the root `CLAUDE.md`), updating live as games are hosted, fill, start or close. A game is listed only while its host is connected and waiting for a partner: co-op is two frogs, so a game drops off the list once it has a partner or starts, and when its host leaves or closes the tab. It comes back if the partner leaves before the round.

**The waiting room** lists the players by their profile names, with the room's connection status (connecting, connected, reconnecting). The host has a Start button, enabled once a partner is in. A guest whose host leaves is told so, a guest who clicks a game whose host has gone is told it has closed (the peer module's `not-found`), and a guest who arrives after the seat was taken is turned away with a note. Leaving, or closing the tab, takes the room down through the peer module.

**The local co-op join screen** shows the two player slots, P1 Sprout and P2 Splash. Each device on this computer joins by pressing its join button, and takes the lowest free slot: the keyboard's left half with Space, its right half with /, a controller with A. A device holds at most one slot, a third device is turned away while both are taken, and a device leaves with its jump-back key (S, ↓) or B, freeing its slot for the next to join while the other player keeps theirs; a controller that disconnects leaves too. Each slot shows the device in it. Below the slots, the course picker lists every co-op course in the registry's order, each with its name and description, and as symbols its two pieces in the frogs' colours, its rows, its road's lanes and its medal times; the first starts picked. A click or tap picks a course, and so do either half's rotate keys (Q and , the previous, E and . the next, going round) and a controller's LB and RB; Enter on a course in the list picks it rather than starting, unless it is already picked. Start (the button, Enter, or Menu on a controller) is enabled once both slots are taken, and starts local co-op on the picked course. Back returns to the lobby. Touch doesn't join: it plays online only.

A controller's A and B join and leave before they move round the screen, so a join never also presses a button: A on a controller that isn't in joins it while a slot is free, and B on one that is in takes it out. A controller already in, or turned away with both slots taken, presses the focused button with A (a course, Back, Start), and B with no slot to leave goes back to the lobby. Menu always starts and LB and RB always step the course, whoever presses them. The keyboard's join keys never move the focus.

**In the waiting room the host picks the course,** on the same picker, and the guest sees the pick as a card, updated as the host changes it; until the host's word arrives it says the host is picking. **Start plays online co-op** on the picked course: the host is Sprout and its partner Splash, each on their own device, with every device on it (the whole keyboard, controllers, touch) driving that one frog. A corner says which frog you are, and Leave goes back to the lobby; a controller leaves from the game's menu or the done screen. The host's LB and RB step the course in the waiting room too, and its Menu starts. A partner who leaves mid-round, or closes the tab, ends it: the host is told who left, back in its waiting room, where its game is listed again; a guest whose host leaves is told so, as in the waiting room. Either player's restart (R, Menu or the done screen's button) starts the round over for both: the guest's asks the host, which decides.

**The protocol** (`coop.ts`) follows the root `CLAUDE.md`: guests send intents, the host decides and sends results. Every message is checked as it comes off the wire, and one that isn't a message of the protocol fails loudly. Host to guest:

- `course`, in the waiting room: the course the host has picked, as a course spec, sent as the partner is seated and whenever the host picks another.
- `start`, carrying the course, as a course spec. Both devices build the course from it. A co-op course is named by its id, and a spec naming an id this device has no course for fails loudly, naming those it has.
- `full`: the guest is turned away, because the game already has its partner or has started.
- `room`: the round ends and both go back to the waiting room, the partner still seated, so the game stays off the open-games list. The host sends it on its own Course select, or on the guest's asking.
- During play (see Online co-op): `input`, an input in the host's final order, the guest's own carrying the number the guest gave it; `confirm`, a tick before which every input is final, with the run's fingerprint there; `pong`, the reply to a ping, with the host's tick; and `restart`.

Guest to host, during play: `input`, one of the guest's own, numbered and stamped with the guest's tick; `restart`, asking for one; `ping`, for the clock; and `desync`, saying the confirmed runs disagree. Outside the round's numbered messages, the guest's `room` asks the host to end the round for the waiting room; the host answers with its own `room`, and drops one arriving once the round has stopped. The guest's `input` and `restart` carry the round (how many restarts there have been), so the host drops one meant for a round it has already restarted: an expected race, not a bug. A message from anyone but the seated partner fails loudly; play messages arriving after the round stopped are dropped, for the same reason.

**The URL switches.** `view.ts` resolves the URL to one screen. `?play=local` skips the lobby into local co-op's join screen; any other `play` value fails loudly. `?course=<id>` picks the co-op course the join screen and a host's waiting room start on, and fails loudly on an id no course has. The dev views replace the game and win over `?play`: `?garage` (the fleet), `?world` (the world preview), `?sounds` (the sound audition), `?music` (the music page), `?frog` (the frog preview), `?heli` (the helicopter on a turntable) and `?replay=<name>` (a replay), in that order of precedence. `?replay` with no name fails loudly, and so does a name no replay has, listing those there are.

## Co-op

Frogmino is co-op only: two players, on one computer or online. Online, it runs on the repo's shared relay rooms (see Multiplayer in the root `CLAUDE.md`).

**The philosophy:** co-op is meaningful only if the players can't each do their own thing; they must coordinate to succeed. The inspiration is Brain Wall's pairs, two people forming together a shape neither could form alone.

The rules for the team are built (`run.ts`), and local co-op plays them: two players on one computer, each on a half of the keyboard or a controller (see Controls and Lobby), with both frogs drawn and the camera following the team over the middle of the road, and online co-op plays them over a peer room (see Online co-op). Co-op's rule tests build their rows by hand from real traffic.

**Sessions.** A session owns a run: its course, its input log and its stepping. Local co-op is a local session with two players, every input applied at once at the run's current tick and logged there. The game sends each input to the session by player slot and draws whatever run the session holds, so online co-op's sessions, owning the log the host orders, sit behind the same interface with no change to the rules, the drawing, the sound or the input sources. A test plays two players' random inputs through a local co-op session with uneven frames and replays its log to the identical run.

**Designed courses.** Co-op plays courses designed by hand for the pair (`coop-courses/`, see Courses and medals): one idea a row, then the ideas combined, so the coordination is the puzzle and the shapes stay readable. Each row names the answer it was designed around, the vehicles outlining where each frog goes; others may pass too, and the analysis counts them (see Proving courses). How to design one is Designing co-op courses in `ideas/course-authoring.md`. The course, not the rules, says how many lanes the road has (see Courses and medals); the rules' lane count comes from the course through the run, as the world's and the camera's do. Tests hold every course to the course checks, and a slow test proves each in its answers to the finish.

- **The frogs move along the road as one.** The team has one depth: every frog's front face is there, so they are always side by side. Either player's jump forward or back moves the whole team, so the pace is the team's to agree. The held jump is the team's too: a newer jump press, either player's, replaces the held one, so when both hold, the newest press wins. A player lets go only of their own key: letting go of a jump the partner's newer press replaced changes nothing, and losing focus lets go of only that player's keys. Hops, slides and turns are each frog's own, and each player has their own held slide, on its own cadence; the held keys repeat in the order they were pressed, whoever pressed them.
- **The frogs are solid to each other,** like vehicles, on the road, aboard the helicopter and in the air: no slide, turn (kicks included) or hop may put one frog's cells into the other's, and they never pass through each other. A frog is solid to its partner's cells as they are now, at whatever height: a frog on the road may slide in under a partner that is up, wherever the cells under it are free. A frog may stand on its partner, and stays there for as long as the partner supports it; a partner's hop lifts it and a partner's slide carries it (see The motion model). The jumps move both frogs together, so they never meet each other; a jump back is refused if it would put either frog's cell into a vehicle of a row the team has passed.
- **Turns follow Tetris rules.** A turn that collides with the corridor edge, an overlapping vehicle or the partner tries Tetris-style kicks before failing (see Turning in The motion model), so a player can twist into a notch the partner forms and lock the two pieces together, as in Tetris.
- **One judgement for the team.** A row reaches both frogs at the same instant, since they share a depth, and passes only if every frog fits; if any doesn't, the whole team is bonked back together, the row counting one pass or one bonk for the team. A bonk lands no hop: a frog in the air when the team is knocked back stays up for the rest of its airtime. Riding and the fit outline stay per frog: a frog that is up while a row overlaps the team rides it, while its partner may stand on the road between the same row's vehicles.
- **Shared openings are the puzzle.** A row may give each frog its own opening, or one opening that both must fit through at once, interlocked; since the frogs can't overlap, an opening shaped as two pieces fitted together lets the team through only in that interlock. Hops may be needed by both frogs, or by one. The `?world` preview's co-op rows are such openings, and a rule test plays the team through each of them.
- **Each frog has its own piece,** set by the course and kept to the finish (see Pieces): any two pieces, the same one twice included.
- **Start and finish together.** Each frog starts aboard the helicopter in the middle of its own share of the road (the road split evenly between the frogs), in its piece's first rotation no wider than that share, so the frogs start side by side, never overlapping. Either player's first jump forward jumps the team out together. The team crosses the finish together, when the shared depth reaches the line: every held key lets go and every frog comes down onto its support, a frog standing on its partner staying on it. The run's done state, its passes, bonks and time are the team's.
- **Every input feels immediate,** for both players, in local co-op on one device and online alike: neither player ever waits on the network to see their own frog act. The host is the authority. One system serves both: the rules are a pure function of the seed and the players' timed inputs, each device runs them at once on its own inputs, and the host's ordering of inputs is final. A device that learns of an input late re-runs the rules from that input's time and corrects to the host's result. A test plays two players' interleaved inputs through bonks, riding, the drop and the finish, and replays the log in chunks of every size to the same state.
- The rules are written for a list of frogs: the pass test, frog-to-frog solidity, the kicks and the shared depth work on a list of any length, and a team of one is a rule test's simplest fixture. The rules take inputs for players 0 to n-1 of an n-frog run and throw for any other.

**Online co-op** (`online.ts`, `rollback.ts`) is two sessions, the host's and the guest's, behind the session interface, with the shared peer module carrying their messages (see Lobby for the protocol). The rules, the drawing, the sound and the input sources don't know it is online.

- **The clock.** Online, the clock is real time on both devices: a frame is never clamped, as a local session's is, so a tab back from the background catches up with its partner, who played on. The host's run starts at tick 0 as it sends `start`, the guest's as `start` arrives. The guest keeps its clock with the host's from round trips: it pings on the start and at every confirmation, the host answers with its tick, and the guest takes the host's tick now to be that tick plus half the round trip, measured on its own clock in ticks. A drift of a tick is left alone, a few are pulled in a tick a frame (running one tick more or fewer), and more than ten at once, holding still to wait for the host when ahead. Each device's game starts as its scene loads, so the first round trip mostly corrects that. A guest that starts later than the host changes nothing: the traffic holds still until the jump out, which is an input at a tick of the host's log like any other. The pings ride on the confirmations, events both sides already have; there is no timer.
- **The input order.** Each player's inputs apply at once on their own device, stamped with its tick, as in a local session. The host's order is final: its own go into the log at once, and the guest's at the tick the guest stamped, as they reach it, after every input stamped then or earlier, the host's run rolling back to put them there. An input stamped before the host's latest confirmation is moved to the host's tick now instead, never dropped. The host sends every input on in that order, the guest's own too, and the guest builds the host's log from them.
- **The rewind window.** The host confirms the run every half second (`CONFIRM_TICKS`), half a second behind its own tick (`REWIND_WINDOW`): every input before the confirmed tick is final, so the guest's inputs are taken at their own tick for at least half a second, longer than any working connection's one-way trip. The guest, until the host's word, runs the log it expects: the host's inputs, and its own still on their way after the host's at each tick, since they reach the host after them. When the host sends one back at another tick or in another order, the guest re-runs from there; one of its own the host has confirmed past leaves its run until the host sends it back at the host's tick.
- **Rollback.** Each device keeps the run every tenth of a second (`SNAPSHOT_TICKS`) since its last confirmed run, each before its tick's inputs, so a late input re-runs from the nearest snapshot before it, never from the start. A confirmation drops the older ones. A rule test inserts random inputs at random past ticks and holds the run to a straight replay of the whole log every time.
- **Corrections, drawn.** The drawn frog already glides toward its rule state, so a late input of the partner's is drawn as the partner's move, a moment late, and the camera follows the drawn team as ever. The rules make a bonk afresh as they re-run, so the drawing tells a bonk by its tick and depth, not by the object holding it, or every correction would replay the last bonk. Sounds are cued from state changes, so a correction that adds or moves a bonk or a pass plays its sound (see Open questions).
- **Out of sync.** With every confirmation the host sends a fingerprint of the confirmed run (FNV-1a over its rule state). The guest checks the run its copy of the host's log gives at that tick against it. The rules are deterministic, so a difference is a bug, and it fails loudly: each device logs its log as a replay (the course spec, the log and the tick) in the browser's console, for the replay tools to play, the guest tells the host, and the round stops for both, back in the waiting room with a note.
- **Messages in order.** Each side numbers its messages from 0 and handles them in that order, whatever order they come in. The relay's WebSocket is ordered already; the numbers keep the protocol from depending on it. A number arriving twice fails loudly.
- **Tests.** A simulated network joins a host and a guest session in simulated time, a tick at a time, each message delayed by its own seeded amount, so messages overtake each other. Over a LAN, the internet and a phone hotspot, on several seeds, with both players' random inputs, both devices end with the identical log, and runs a straight replay of the host's log gives. Others hold the guest's clock to the host's, an input taken at the guest's tick by rolling the host back, an input later than the window moved to the host's tick, restarts from either side with the old round's inputs still on the wire, a partner vanishing mid-game, and a desync failing loudly on both devices. End-to-end tests host and join in two browsers: each player's key moves their own frog on both screens, a restart from the guest restarts both, and either player leaving is told to the other.

## Proving courses

"Seeing is believing." A run is a pure function of its course and its input log, so a proof that a course can be beaten is an input log, and replaying it in the real rules is the verification. The tools are in `proof/`; they are the foundation of Claude's toolbox (`ideas/course-authoring.md`).

- **The solver** works row by row in the real engine. Once the last row has gone by and every hop is down, the planner searches the team's poses breadth first (slides with their riders, turns with their kicks, hops that lift a partner, double jumps, waits and falls, at any height) for the fewest slots to a pose that passes the next row, on the team's own moves the run plays too (`team.ts`), between rows, where only the road's edges and the partners are in the way. Time runs in slots of one action by one player (14 ticks, so a hop's airtime is five), and the row arrives half a slot after the plan's last action, so every hop the pose needs is still up. The solver jumps forward a held key's repeat apart for as long as the plan still has room before the row, plays the plan's actions a slot apart, and checks after each that the engine's team is the plan's: a difference, or a bonk in a pose the plan passes, is a disagreement between the planner and the rules, and fails loudly. It goes row after row, never back, on to the finish, or a set number of rows, and replays the whole log from the start to confirm it.
- **It is bounded.** A row's search can only visit the team's finitely many poses, and throws past a cap. A row with no route ends the search, reported by its index and why, never hung on.
- **Designed answers.** Given a designed course's answers (the prove CLI gives a co-op course's), each row's plan must end in the row's answer rather than any passing pose, so the proof plays the course as designed and shows its answers can be reached in time.
- **How hard a row is** (`analyse.ts`): the number of team poses that pass it, out of every pose the team can reach between rows, counted once per shape (by the cells each frog covers, so an O's turns count once). One is a single answer; hundreds, a row with room to spare. The CLI prints it per row.
- **The simplest way through** (`analyse.ts`): how much stacking the least demanding of a row's passes takes, and how many passes take that little: solo (each frog where its own hop reaches, standing on nobody), perch (a frog standing on its partner where its own hop left it), lift (a pose the team reaches only with a carry or a hop off) or double jump (only with a double jump). A row designed around a stacking move reads that move here, or the row has a simpler way through it doesn't mean to have. The passing-pose count alone no longer says how hard a row is: with no ceiling, the frogs flying high over the traffic pass most rows in hundreds of ways, each harder than the simplest.
- **Whether a row needs both frogs** (`analyse.ts`), for a co-op row. Alone, a frog reaches every shape on the road one hop high; together the team reaches those side by side and every stack besides, which no frog reaches alone, and what a row asks of the pair is how its ways through collide. The measures:
  - **Coupling,** the headline, from 0 to 1, read on the ways through side by side. A frog's solo passes are the poses it passes the row in alone, on the road or up a hop. At a lane line across the road, the free pairings are each of Sprout's solo passes wholly left of the line with each of Splash's wholly right of it: ways each can choose without looking at the other. The side by side passes are the team passes with neither frog standing on the other and each no higher than its own hop lifts it; the rest are **stacked**, a frog standing on its partner or higher than its own hop reaches, and are counted apart. Side by side passes are in order with Sprout's leftmost lane no further right than Splash's; the rest are crossed, the frogs swapped. A line's coupling is the share of the in-order side by side passes and free pairings together that are not free pairings passing as a team, and the row's is its best line's. 0 is parallel solo: a line splits the road, each frog picks any of its own ways, and nothing else passes side by side. 1: every way through side by side has the frogs sharing a lane, or only a stack passes. Stacked ways are left out because they share lanes and, whenever the pieces can stack, outnumber the side by side ones by tens to one, which read nearly every row fully coupled; what a row asks of a stack is read from the stacked count and the coordination kinds. The label reads it: parallel solo below 0.2 with no selfish decoys, fully coupled from 0.8, partly coupled between; the cut-offs are judgement, so read the number too.
  - **Selfish decoys,** per frog: the frog's solo passes with which the team has no way through, so its partner then fits nowhere.
  - **Coordination kinds** of the designed answer, from the team in the previous row's answer with every frog fallen to its support (the course's start, for the first row), and of the solver's route from where its plan started. Read off the pose: a perch (a frog up off the road standing on its partner one cell up, where a hop off the road leaves it), a stack (a frog standing on its partner higher than that), both up (every frog up and none on a partner, so the hops must be timed together), an interlock (the frogs' cells touching side to side or top to bottom), order (a frog can't reach its place while its partner stays where it started) and crossing (the frogs swap sides, by their leftmost and rightmost lanes). And the **stacking moves**: a carry (a hop that lifts a partner, or a slide that takes a rider along), a hop off (a frog hopping from a partner on its support) and a double jump (a frog hopping from a partner still in its own airtime). The answer needs one when the team can't reach its pose at all without it, which the analysis finds by searching the team's reach again with that move refused (`reachWithout`), so a pose two ways reach, such as a frog lifted on its partner (a carry, or a hop off then the partner's hop beneath it), shows its stack but needs neither. A route shows every stacking move its steps made, and a perch or a stack wherever one stood along the way. All nine are exact as defined; what they don't see is timing beyond the pose, such as whether a raised frog's hop actually runs out on its partner.
- **The course summary** (`report.ts`): per designed row its gap, passing poses (side by side, in order and crossed, and stacked), the simplest way through, coupling with its label and line, decoys and the answer's kinds, then the proof's time against the course's medal times and the held-jump floor.
- **Its limits.** It plans only between rows, so a route needing a move while a row overlaps the team (sliding along a passed row's opening, riding into the next) isn't found. It never jumps back, so it proves a course can be beaten, not how a player recovers from a bonk. It plays at one fixed pace, not the fastest, so its time is a reference point for medal times, not a target (see Courses and medals). A slot is one action by one player, where two players act at once, so a row with far to travel takes it longer than a team, and its fewest-slot route may be one no player would choose: a turn kicked two lanes through the partner, swapping the frogs' sides, which the rules allow.
- **Readable routes.** Each row's route is printed as steps in words ("Sprout hops, lifting Splash; Splash slides left; Sprout lands"), with the row's face drawn in ASCII at each stage (`#` a vehicle, `.` open, `1` and `2` the frogs, `x` a frog's cell where a vehicle will be) and the inputs that play it.
- **The CLI:** `npm run frogmino:prove -- <course id>` for one co-op course (or `all` for every one), with `--rows N` to stop after N rows, `--length U` to put the finish U units down the road, `--brief` for a line per row, and `--save [NAME]` to keep the proof as a replay (`proof-<course id>` by default) in `proof/saved-replays.ts`. A course is proved in its designed answers, and each row's line carries its measures and the route's coordination kinds; the course summary follows. For a proof to the finish of the course as the game plays it, it prints the proof's time, the medal it earns, the course's medal times and the held-jump floor down the course's length. It exits non-zero when a course has a row with no route.
- **Replays.** `?replay=<name>` plays a named replay (a course spec, an input log and the tick it ends at, as plain data) in the real game scene through a replay session: the game sees only a session, as if the players pressed those keys at those ticks. It plays at real speed, a half or a quarter, or paused (P), and restarts (R). A test replays every named replay in the rules, so a rule change that breaks one fails loudly; the proofs are then re-saved with the CLI.
- **The guard: a pair is never stuck for good** (`proof/never-stuck.slow.test.ts`). Stacking lets one frog climb over the other, so it must never let a pair into an arrangement it can't undo. For every pair of the seven pieces, the same piece twice included, on a 10-lane road with no traffic, the test explores every arrangement the two can reach from their starting places on the planner's own moves (slides with riders, turns with kicks, hops that lift, double jumps, waits and falls, airtime in the planner's slots), and asserts every one at rest leads back to the start. None of the 28 pairs can be stranded, and every pair but two Os can swap sides; under the rules before stacking an O and an S could cross over and never cross back. It also plays random walks of the planner's moves in the engine, slot by slot, for every pair, since the planner is what the solver holds to the engine. It is slow (half a minute) because it walks some eighty thousand arrangements per pair: a one-way arrangement only shows by searching them all, which no fast test does.
- **The perch** (`?replay=perch`) is Long and Short's proof up to the row where Splash steps onto the plank, played with two jumps fewer before that row: the row comes later than the plan's hop allows for, so Splash's hop runs out with Sprout's flat I under it, and Splash stands on Sprout until the row arrives. It is made from the saved proof, so it follows when the proof is saved again, and tests hold it to standing on Sprout and passing with no bonk.

## Adding a co-op course

What a course designer does, step by step. Read Designing co-op courses in `ideas/course-authoring.md` first, and the measures under Proving courses.

1. **The file.** Make `coop-courses/<id>.ts`, the id lower-case words joined by hyphens, exporting one `DesignedCourse` (`coop-courses/design.ts`), as the courses there do. Fill in every field:
   - `id` (the same as the file's name), `name` (its pair and its idea, never a difficulty) and `description`, one line for the picker.
   - `lanes`, the road's width, and `kinds`, Sprout's piece then Splash's: any two pieces, the same one twice included, each narrow enough in some rotation to fit half the road, which is where it starts, in the middle of its half.
   - `rows`, in order, each with its `lesson` (a few words), `vehicles` (each a fleet id at its leftmost lane, one per lane), `gap` (the reading room before it: the first row's is usually `TUNING.firstWallDepth`, and `READ_SLOW`, `READ` and `PACE` in `design.ts` are the usual later ones) and `answer` (the pose each frog is meant to pass in, `pose(col, rotation, height)`, the height in cells above the road).
   - `length`, where the finish is: just past where the rows end, so that a team jumping flat out still meets the last row first. The check says how far that is if it's too short.
   - `medals`, gold, silver and bronze in seconds, provisional: set them from the CLI's proof and held-jump floor as Courses and medals says.
   - `repeat`, only if a slow team should meet fewer than every row again after the last one; leave it out to go round them all.
2. **The registry line.** Import the course in `coop-courses/index.ts` and add it to the list there, where the list's order is the picker's.
3. **The checks.** From the worktree's root:
   - `npx vitest run src/projects/frogmino/coop-courses` runs the course checks on every registered course (each row legal, each answer on the road, passing its row with the frogs clear of each other and within the team's reach, the medal times, the length) in seconds. A broken course fails loudly, naming itself and the row.
   - `npx vitest run --config vitest.slow.config.ts src/projects/frogmino/coop-courses` proves every course to its finish in its designed answers.
   - `npm run typecheck` and `npm run lint`.
4. **The CLI to read.** `npm run frogmino:prove -- <id> --brief` proves the course in its designed answers and prints a line per row the team meets (its gap, passing poses, the simplest way through, coupling, selfish decoys and the answer's and the route's coordination kinds, with the route in words), then the course summary, a line per designed row, and the proof's time against the medal times and the held-jump floor. Drop `--brief` for each row's faces in ASCII. `npm run frogmino:prove -- all --brief` does every course. Read whether each row asks what it was designed to: its simplest way through should be its lesson (a double-jump row reads "double jump", a stacking row never "solo"), a row meant to need both frogs side by side should read partly or fully coupled, or have selfish decoys, and its answer should show the coordination kind or stacking move it teaches.
5. **Watch it, if wanted.** `npm run frogmino:prove -- <id> --save` keeps the proof as `?replay=proof-<id>`; `?play=local&course=<id>` opens the join screen on the course.

## Traffic

Obstacles are shaped vehicles on a road, not walls. "Wall" is prototype vocabulary and will go away.

- **For now, vehicles arrive lined up in rows at one speed.** Side by side as one row, they leave a gap the frog must match to pass: the Brain Wall moment.
- **A row is vehicles from the 19, side by side, one per lane.** Each vehicle (see The fleet) takes the lanes its silhouette spans, no two share a lane, and lanes may stay empty. There is no stacking yet. A row's solid cells are its vehicles' cells; everything else is the opening, up into the open air above the vehicles. So the pass test, the bonk, a row's solidity while it overlaps the frog, riding and the traffic's recycling all work on a row exactly as on a wall.
- **No filler vehicles.** Smaller one-, two- and three-cell fillers were tried in analysis: they added clutter and didn't close the gaps they were meant to.
- **Rows are designed answer-first.** A designer picks the pose each frog is meant to pass in, then the vehicles that outline it, and the prove CLI counts the other poses that pass too (see Designing co-op courses in `ideas/course-authoring.md`).
- **Known gap: rows rarely force the frog to lie flat.** A pose held down by a ceiling needs an overhang reaching over it from a neighbouring lane, and one vehicle per lane seldom gives one. If that is missed in play, the idea to try is vehicles carrying vehicles (stacking): analysis showed it raises the poses a row can force from 129 to 202 of 210.
- **Mixed speeds and staggered vehicles are tabled.** Staggered vehicles the frog weaves between, as in Frogger, and vehicles travelling at different speeds were part of the direction. The design problem that tabled them: getting vehicles at different speeds to arrive lined up into a matching row at the right moment is hard. Two v1 rules are proposed for when they are revisited: within a lane, a vehicle nearer the frog is never slower than one behind it; and a vehicle keeps a constant silhouette along its length, with solid sides. The earlier idea that a hop onto a vehicle stands on it is adopted, as riding (see The motion model).
- **Vehicle shapes are silhouettes chosen for gameplay,** and the art serves them: see The fleet.

## The fleet

The traffic is exactly the 19 fixed tetrominoes: every rotation of the seven pieces, with rotations that look the same counted once (I 2, O 1, S 2, Z 2, T 4, J 4, L 4). Each is its own vehicle, with an id made of its piece and the first rotation that gives its shape, such as `J2`. A vehicle drives head-on at the frog and never rotates. Its front silhouette, as the player sees it from behind the frog, is exactly its piece's four cells on the lane × row grid, resting on the road. Funky shapes, such as a long bar on top of a single wheeled cell, are intended.

The art serves the shape: fun, quirky vehicles that never hide which four cells they fill.

- **The silhouette is sacred.** Seen head-on, a vehicle's solid volume fills exactly its four cells. Nothing reaches into an empty cell of its bounding box or a neighbouring lane: not wheels, mirrors, antennas, exhausts, cargo, flags or smoke, because an intrusion would lie to the player about the opening. The ground clearance under a vehicle's lowest cells holds its wheels, which reach down to the road, and nothing else; nothing hangs in the clearance under an empty lane. Details stay inside the cells' volume, standing proud of an outer face by no more than the surface-detail tolerance in `vehicles/parts.ts`, a few hundredths of a cell. A unit test checks every detail's front-projected bounds against its vehicle's cells.
- **A vehicle never crosses its lane lines.** Its two outer sides, on the lane lines either side of it, stand two paint layers inside the lines (`SIDE_INSET` in `vehicles/parts.ts`), and nothing on them, wheels included, stands prouder, so everything it draws stays within its own lanes. Two vehicles side by side then share no space, and no face of one can meet a face of the other however each rumbles, since the rumble never moves a vehicle across the lanes; the same holds for the frog beside it. Details that crossed the line were what flickered between neighbours: a side band running the whole length ends in the neighbour's front and back faces, and hubs and hatches poke through the neighbour's faces as the two bounce out of step. Unit tests hold every vehicle's body and parts within its lanes, and scan every pair of vehicles the road has room for side by side, fronts lined up, at rest and rumbling on their own.
- **Cells stay countable.** Every body cell carries the darker inset border the walls and the frog have: all round on its front and back, and along the seams on its sides and top, so the border never stretches with the length. Details sit inset from the cell edges so the seams show, and colour blocking by cell (a deck, an awning, a load) helps the count.
- **The silhouette is constant along the length.** Each body cell is one solid box the vehicle's whole length (its outer side a hair inside the lane line, as above), two or three cells to suit its archetype, so the opening is the same wherever along the vehicle the frog is. The lengths are the fleet's, in `fleet.ts`, and the rules keep each vehicle solid for exactly its length (see The motion model).
- **No Tetris colours.** The paints are the fleet's own design tokens in `globals.css`, and none belongs to a piece: not the standard seven-colours-per-piece mapping, and never one main colour for every rotation of a piece (a test checks this). Vehicles are told apart by archetype, silhouette and decoration, not colour alone. This is deliberate distance from Tetris's protected look; see the legal note in `ideas/tetris-worlds-look.md`.
- **Procedural, in the game's look.** Vehicles are boxes and discs built in code and merged per paint, in the plain lit materials and lights of Look. There are no models, textures or fonts, so there is nothing to credit.
- **No depth fighting.** Paint lies on a face in layers of one thickness (`LAYER` in `vehicles/parts.ts`): a detail on a decal sits a layer higher and inset from its edges, and stripes of two paints lie side by side in one layer. A layer is more than the depth buffer resolves where the traffic is farthest, and two fit within the surface-detail tolerance. A unit test runs the world's face scanner (`world/coplanar.ts`) over every vehicle and every pose of the frog for faces of different paints closer than that.
- **It rumbles as it drives.** Each end of a vehicle settles a little on its springs and springs back, quickly, with its own rates and phases seeded per vehicle (by row and lane in the game), so the traffic never bounces in step; the two ends bouncing apart tip it a hair along the road. It is a pure function of the frame clock. The whole vehicle moves, wheels and all, and only ever down, by at most the rumble's drop in `vehicles/rumble.ts`: its wheels press that little into the road and never lift off, and it never moves across the lanes. The drop is within the surface-detail tolerance, and a unit test holds every vehicle's body and parts at the rumble's extremes to its cells. The wheels don't spin: a plain drum with a centred hub looks the same turned. The garage's turntable and the world preview's traffic rumble too; the garage's head-on view stands still, so it stays exactly the silhouette.

## The frog

The player is a frog shaped like its piece. It has to read as the same frog character in all 28 poses (seven pieces, four rotations), and never lie about its cells. The fleet's art rules all apply: the art serves the shape, the silhouette is sacred, cells stay countable, and it is boxes, balls and discs built in code in the plain lit materials of Look, with the shared cell border and no outlines or toon shading, so there is nothing to credit.

- **The features stay upright; the head is the top row.** Whatever the piece and rotation, the head is the piece's top row, with two eyes on top of it, centred on the row's middle half a cell apart, and the legs are under its bottom row, on the road. The eyes never rotate with the piece. A frog whose eyes turned with it would be upside down or on its side in half its poses, with its eyes underneath or on its far side, hidden from the camera, and the eyes would jump round the shape on every turn. Upright, the eyes are always at the top of the shape, where the player is already looking to read the next row, and the frog always sits the right way up, like a real frog, whose eyes are its highest point. A turn still swings the whole body; the features are already seated for the new pose.
- **Seen from behind, the eyes bulge up out of the head, never above it.** The camera sees the frog's back and top, and a frog from behind is two eye bumps on its head: that is the look to keep. Eyes above the head would reach into the empty cell above it. The head's cells are shaped along their depth instead, which the silhouette doesn't see: a thin full-height snout ridge across the front keeps each head cell a full square head-on, the back of the head sits lower, and the eyes bulge up out of that dip, just behind the snout, their highest part (the pupil, anywhere on its dazed circle) at exactly the cells' top.
- **The pupils face the camera; the frog faces down the road.** The frog faces the oncoming traffic, and the player sees it from behind and above, so the eyes sit high on the head, as a tree frog's do: each eye is a skin bump with its eyeball bulging out of the bump's top, toward the outside and a touch back, and its pupil in the middle of that bulge, looking up and out. From the gameplay camera each pupil shows plainly, on the far, outer side of its eyeball, which reads as a frog seen from behind looking ahead and out; the turntable shows it from the front looking up. Unit tests hold each pupil turned to the gameplay camera's direction, on the far side of its eye in the camera's picture, and in its view past the bump and the head, open and dazed. The bar pupil lies across the road, so seen from above it is a bar across the eye.
- **No eye part ever runs alongside another paint.** Balls of different paints that meet at a shallow angle flicker through each other, as a white barely poking out of its lid did. So each eye's white stands well out of its bump and its pupil out of its white, each crossing the surface beneath it steeply, open and dazed. A blink sinks the eyeball into the bump until it is hidden, and the pupil, which stands proudest, shrinks away ahead of the white so it never shows on the lid; the dazed lids droop only as far as the white's rim stays steep. Unit tests sample the eyes' surfaces in every distinct placement and hold them to that.
- **The legs stand in the ground clearance.** Everything drawn in the cell grid is lifted by the ground clearance (see Look), so feet and wheels touch the road while the rules stay unchanged; the frog takes it as a parameter, the shared clearance by default. The frog's legs live only in that gap, only under its bottom row, and face down the road with the frog: two small front feet, and two hind legs at the row's ends, each folded under the body as a sitting frog's is, the haunch along the body's underside and the long webbed foot on the road beneath it, from the heel at the back to the toes fanned out ahead and to the side. The body hides all but the back of the gap from the gameplay camera, so the camera sees each haunch with its heel below it, never toes; feet whose toes reached back into that strip, toward the camera, read as a frog facing the wrong way. With no clearance it has no legs. The hind legs kick out on takeoff, stretching back along the road and lifting off it, never across or up, so seen head-on they only ever shrink within the gap; then the legs tuck away into the body for the rest of the hop, so a frog riding a vehicle sits on its roof rather than standing in it, and on landing they come down still kicked out and fold back. A move's small hop kicks them out a little and folds them back as it lands. The drop and the finish leap are hops, so they kick and fold too.
- **Its hind legs can't sit on its sides.** A sitting frog's haunches bulge from its sides, but seen head-on the frog may fill only its cells and the gap under its bottom row, and along the road only its own depth, which the rules hold solid. So a haunch on the side could only be carved into the back corner of an end cell, which splits that cell's back into two bordered faces, so it would count as two cells. The legs stay folded in the gap instead. A carved corner that keeps one border round the cell's back would need a notched-cell shape of its own, with its own border mapping: a renderer change, the owner's call.
- **Cells stay countable.** Every cell carries the cell border, and every cell's back, which always faces the camera, carries the same marks, as do exposed tops away from the head: count the spots, count the cells. The mouth, a wide dark line across the front of the head, and the throat under it are on the front, seen on the turntable.
- **Distinct from Tetris.** It is plainly a frog, with eyes, spotted skin and webbed feet, and its colours are its own tokens in `globals.css`, the same for every piece and far from any Tetris piece colour (a test checks this); see the legal note in `ideas/tetris-worlds-look.md`.
- **Life, kept subtle.** It breathes, blinks now and then (sometimes twice), and puffs its throat, each on a deterministic schedule seeded per frog, so two frogs are never in step. A hop narrows it as it stretches up, kicks its hind legs out and tucks its legs, a landing folds them back and squashes it, each move's small hop narrows it a touch and draws its legs a little up and kicks them a little out, with a touch of a squash as the last of a run of moves lands, and a bonk flattens it against the row along the road, then leaves it dazed for a moment, its pupils circling and its lids drooping. None of it can reach into an empty cell: every cell only ever shrinks toward a point in itself, never grows, and the throat grows only as far as its cell has room for. Unit tests hold every part of every pose, with the throat fully puffed and the pupils anywhere on their circle, to the frog's cells and the clearance under its bottom row, and every motion to its limits.
- **The co-op pair.** The two players are a matched pair of real frogs, not a recolour: P1, Sprout, a leaf-green tree frog with a sunny belly, orange feet, one big round spot per cell and round pupils; P2, Splash, an azure dart frog with a pale belly, pink feet, two freckles per cell and bar pupils. Green and orange beside blue and pink complement each other, and both stand out on the slate road. Their skins differ in lightness as well as hue, and their markings in shape, so they tell apart at a glance even for a colour-blind player, and even as the same piece side by side.
**The frog preview** is `?frog` on Frogmino's URL. It shows every piece in every rotation, from the gameplay camera's direction over the lane × row grid on the frog's front face (lifted by the clearance), or flat from straight behind, where the outline is exactly the silhouette on the grid; a turntable of the selected pose; the two players side by side; and the co-op pair together, as the same piece and as different pieces. Its buttons play a hop (up a cell and down again, as the game's arc does), a move forward, back, left or right (three in a row, as when the key is held, with the game's own glide and small hop, from as far away as that takes the frog to its resting place), a bonk, idle, the drop and the finish leap on every frog at once, and switch which frog the grid shows. The drop and the finish leap run the game's own paths, with the frog's resting place as the road: the drop starts the helicopter's deck height up and the drop distance back and lands at rest, and the leap lands a deck's height up, holds a moment and goes back to rest. While any of these is cued, the views pull back to fit its whole path.

**In the game.** The gameplay scene draws every frog of the team with `FrogBody`, each as its player's variant: P1 Sprout and P2 Splash. Each has its own drawn-frog state (its glides, height, riding, bonk, drop and finish leap), its own silhouette and its own fit outline, and its own life schedule, so the two never blink in step. The camera follows the team's drawn depth, which is the same for both frogs as their rule depth is, and stays over the middle of the road. It is placed by the frog's pivot (`frogPivot`, the middle of its cells), so it turns about the piece's middle, and drawn at the rule rotation at once, easing only the leftover swing, since the features are seated for the rule pose. It plays a hop when a hop starts (and as it jumps out of the helicopter or leaps onto the gantry), a landing when the drawn frog comes down, onto the road or its partner, after riding too, a bonk on a bonk, and a move's small hop on each jump and slide. React draws the frog's model a frame after the rules change its rotation, so the drawn frog holds its pose until the model has caught up.

## Look

Fun, quirky and colourful, in keeping with Novelty World: bright, flat and cartoony. Inspiration: see `ideas/tetris-worlds-look.md`.

- **Cells touch,** in walls and the frog, with no gaps between cubes. Every cell face has a darker inset border so the grid still reads.
- **Plain lit materials,** with no cel shading and no outline around shapes: toon bands and an inverted-hull outline were tried and dropped. The cell borders are what keep shapes and cells readable.
- **Lighting is simple:** one ambient light and one directional light. Richer lighting (postprocessing, ambient occlusion) is deferred.
- Walls are solid and opaque at all times.
- **The ground clearance.** Everything drawn in the cell grid stands a quarter of a cell above the ground under it: the vehicles, the frog and the fit outline. It leaves room for a vehicle's wheels and the frog's legs to reach down to the road. It is purely visual, one constant (`GROUND_CLEARANCE` in `clearance.ts`) that the vehicles, the frog, and the headroom under the helicopter's and the gantry's decks all read; the rules count rows from the top of it.
- **The frog's silhouette.** Wherever a vehicle, the helicopter or the finish gantry hides the frog from the camera, its hidden parts show as a flat silhouette in its own token colour, a light tint of each player's skin (Sprout's green, Splash's azure): the frog drawn a second time, in one unlit material that draws only where something is in front of it, after the traffic and the world and before the frog itself. Vehicles stay opaque.

## Theme

The obstacles are oncoming cars and trucks, as in Frogger (see Traffic). Cells blocked above an empty floor cell are fine: they are odd-looking vehicles with overhanging parts, like trailers and cabs. That is why the traffic moves toward the frog rather than the frog running at it. The frog is a frog: see The frog.

## World

The world around the road frames the gameplay and never competes with it: the same rules as the fleet's art. Its theme is a mountain road at golden hour: the mountainside rising on the left with more rocks and trees, the valley side on the right with more guard rails. It is procedural, built from boxes in code in the fleet's plain lit materials, with a fainter version of the cell border on its blocks, and no models, textures or fonts, so there is nothing to credit.

- **The road never ends,** either way. A bonk can knock the frog back past the start, and traffic comes from far up the road, so the road, its roadside and the land are laid out in tiles around the camera and rebuilt as it moves onto a new one. Each tile is laid out from the course seed and its own index alone, so it comes back the same every time. The sky, the clouds and the ground follow the camera. Ahead, the road reaches past the fog's far end, so it fades into the horizon haze rather than ending.
- **The road:** a slate surface over the traffic lanes, with faint dashed lines between the lanes, a step lighter than the road so they help judge lanes without looking like the cell grid or competing with the fit outline. Solid edge lines run along both edges. Beyond each edge are a gravel shoulder and a low kerb, unbroken.
- **Lay-bys** are scenery only: patches of packed gravel beyond the kerb where a driver might stop for the view, with a bench or two. A guard rail runs between each and the road along its whole length, with no gap, and the kerb runs on unbroken, so nothing suggests the frog could go there; the frog can't, since it never leaves the traffic lanes. The world places them from the seed like the rest of the roadside, about one road tile in three, more often on the valley side.
- **The helicopter** starts the course (see The motion model): the Lily Lifter, a twin-fuselage heavy lifter whose open deck, a tile per lane, spans the road, with a striped lip along its front edge, the edge the frogs jump from, and a low railing along its back. Everything else (the two fuselages with frog-eyed canopies, their rotors, tails and skids) flies beside the road on short wings, so a ray from the camera over the road's middle through anything beyond the road's edges only meets the ground further out: nothing but the deck can hide a lane, a vehicle or a frog. Its deck flies as low as the traffic allows, the tallest vehicle clearing its underside with the gantry's headroom at the bottom of its gentle bob, and the frogs aboard bob with it; the camera doesn't. While the team lines up, the road and its scenery go by beneath it, the drawing's cruise with the traffic; at the jump they stop, and the course's frame is the road's from then on. The rows, the finish gantry and the finish's paint are drawn in the course's frame throughout, so the far gantry holds still against the moving road until the jump. The roadside keeps clear of the gantry where it stands on the road: exactly once the road has stopped, and, while it still goes by, where it stood when the road around the camera was last laid out, which matters only at the far end, in the haze. The finish's paint lies a layer above the road's lane dashes and edge lines, so it covers them wherever they fall under it. `?heli` shows the helicopter with its passengers on a turntable.
- **The finish gantry** spans the road at the course's end, a chequered beam on chequered towers with pink flags, with a deck the frog can stand on after finishing. Beneath its near face a chequered finish line is painted across the road, and just short of it a pink spring pad with a chevron per lane, which is decoration until the frog leaps from it. Its deck clears the tallest vehicle, lifted by the ground clearance, with headroom, and its towers stand clear of the lanes and verges. The frog finishes on it (see The motion model).
- **Readability rules,** which unit tests hold the layout to. Nothing stands on the road, or within a clearance around it, nor on a lay-by's gravel but its benches. Near the road everything stays under the near-height limit, well under a cell, so it never hides a lane or the frog from the camera; anything taller, such as trees and hills, keeps its whole footprint beyond the tall offset from the road's edge. The world's paints are its own tokens in `globals.css`, soft and muted, and none is a vehicle's saturated body paint, so nothing beside the road reads as traffic.
- **Depth.** The land beyond the roadside comes in layers of stepped, terraced hills: grassy knolls, then ridges, then snowy peaks, with two landmarks along the course (a boulder with a frog's eyes, and a mesa shaped like a T). The further a layer, the paler and bluer its paints, and a fog in the horizon's colour carries that on, starting beyond the rows the player reads, so the background recedes and the road and traffic stand out. The camera travels along the road, so each layer's distance sets how slowly it drifts by: that is the parallax.
- **The sky** is a gradient dome from warm haze at the horizon to blue overhead, with a blocky low sun and chunky clouds drifting slowly across. At the gameplay camera's pitch, a wide screen's view ends on the ground well short of the horizon, so the sky and the far peaks show on narrow portrait screens, and the knolls, trees and nearer ridges frame the road on wide ones.
- **Cheap on older phones.** Every list of boxes is one instanced mesh, so the whole world is a handful of draw calls, and the tiles are rebuilt only when the camera crosses into a new one.
- **No depth fighting.** No two faces of the world that look the same way share a plane, since the depth buffer can't tell which is in front and they flicker. Paint lies on a surface in thin layers, paint on paint a layer higher; the chequers on a deck's faces are set into the deck rather than laid on it; and parts that touch meet face to face or only along an edge. A unit test scans the whole layout, over several seeds, for faces sharing a plane (`world/coplanar.ts`). The depth buffer's precision depends mostly on the camera's near plane, which sits as far out as the camera allows (`CAMERA_NEAR` in `components/camera-fit.ts`).

`FrogminoWorld` takes the course length, the course seed and the road's width in lanes: the game passes its course's, and the whole layout follows whatever width it is given, the road, its markings and verges, the roadside's clearances, the lay-bys, the land and the finish gantry, with the readability and depth-fighting tests run at each width the preview shows. The width is the course's, so it is passed in rather than read from tuning. It draws in the game scene's axes, adds the fog, and relies on the scene's lights. The gameplay scene mounts it in place of its own floor. The scene's old per-jump cross-lines are gone: the world's lane dashes and the steady rhythm of the kerb blocks and reflector posts already give distance, and lines across the lanes read as the cell grid.

**The world preview** is `?world` on Frogmino's URL: the world from the gameplay camera, with a few rows of traffic coming at the co-op pair (`FrogBody`), Sprout (P1) as an L and Splash (P2) as a J, side by side at one depth, each in its own lanes, taking poses passing the next row. It drives a road 7, 9 or 10 lanes wide, with the gameplay camera's field of view fitted to it, for judging which width reads right, phones included. Each width has its own hand-made rows (`world/preview-rows.ts`), alternating an opening for each frog with one opening they pass only interlocked, one frog hopping over the other, and tests hold every row to letting the frogs through together, clear of each other. The frogs wait aboard the helicopter, then leap out together along the game's drop while it flies off, and drive up the road, past the finish gantry and on. The buttons pick the road's width (each starts the drive over), drive the camera up or back down the road (past the start, to check the road goes on), fast or paused, restart it, and switch the world off to compare with the bare road. A collapsible camera panel has a slider for each camera knob (height, follow distance, look-ahead), with the pitch and fitted field of view they give, applied live through the gameplay camera's own maths; it resets to `tuning.ts` and copies the values in that file's shape, to paste in. The game itself reads only `tuning.ts`.

## Sound

Sound complements the gameplay and never gets in its way, like the art. It adds a little polish and fun, never overwhelms the player, and must not get repetitive or annoying, so only moments worth marking have a sound. It is made with ZzFX, the MIT micro-library that generates retro sounds in code from arrays of parameters: no audio files, and nothing to credit, since code libraries aren't credited. The palette is soft retro: sine and triangle waves, mostly through a low-pass filter, never harsh square beeps.

**The set.**

- **Hop:** a light springy boing as a frog's own hop starts, the top frog's double jump included; a frog carried up by its partner's hop makes none of its own. Each frog hops in its own voice, so the partner's hop, or the double jump a moment after the hop under it, is always heard. It comes at most twice per airtime per frog, so it is short and quiet.
- **Bonk:** a comedic thud, a falling boink over a low bump, when a row knocks the frog back.
- **Row passed:** a small pop as the frog fits through a row. Each clean pass in a row climbs one note of the major pentatonic scale, up to the octave, and a bonk or a restart starts it over: the reward for a streak, heard rather than shown.
- **Jump out onto the road:** a short falling whistle and plop, as the team jumps out of the helicopter.
- **Finish:** a short, soft four-note fanfare as the frog reaches the end.

**Slides, turns and jumps are silent.** They are the game's constant input: a player lines up a fit with a burst of slides and turns before every row, and holds a jump key for most of a run, repeating several times a second. A sound on each would be by far the most repeated sound in the game, a drone under everything else, however quiet and varied. What they do is already shown at once by the frog and the fit outline, and forward progress is heard in the pass pops. Refused moves, landings and restarts are silent too.

**Anti-annoyance rules,** applied to every play:

- A small random stray in pitch and volume, from a range per sound, so a repeated sound doesn't sound like a recording. Musical sounds stray least: the pass pop barely, so its scale stays in tune, and the fanfare not at all.
- A minimum gap per sound, measured on the audio clock: a play sooner than that after the last play of the same sound is dropped, so no sound can machine-gun. A sound each frog makes (the hop) keeps a gap per frog, its voice, so one frog's never swallows its partner's; a cue test holds both frogs' hops heard together.
- Frequent sounds are the quietest and shortest.

**Playback.** One AudioContext, ZzFX's own. The sounds load as the game opens, before any input: ZzFX is imported, which creates the context, and every sound is rendered once to an AudioBuffer and replayed from it, which is cheap on older phones. They must not wait for the first input: creating a browser session's first AudioContext blocks the page for a tenth of a second or more, and the first input is usually the jump out of the helicopter, where that showed as a skipped frame. Browsers keep audio off until the player interacts with the page, so a context created before then starts suspended, and the first key press, click or tap resumes it, inside that input event; that first press is heard, the drop included. ZzFX's own per-play randomness is off, since it would be baked into the one rendering; the variation comes at playback instead. A sound triggered before that first input is dropped, not queued.

**Mute.** A small button in the game's top corner, or the M key, mutes all sound, the music included. Mute and the master volume are remembered in local storage; if storage refuses, the setting still holds for the visit.

**The sound audition** is `?sounds` on Frogmino's URL. It lists each sound with its trigger, its variation and minimum gap, and its ZzFX parameters, which paste into the ZzFX designer. Each has a play button and a ten-in-a-row button, which plays it ten times at about the busiest rate the game can trigger it (passes climbing their streak), to judge how it wears. It has the master volume and the mute toggle. The designs are tuned by editing `audio/sounds.ts`.

**Triggers.** The rules know nothing about sound. The game view watches the store and turns each state change into sounds (`soundCues` in `audio/cues.ts`): a pass when the run counts a new pass, once per row each time the traffic brings it round (a row going by beneath the frog on the gantry is no pass), a bonk when the run records a new bonk, a hop, in its frog's voice, for each frog whose own hop starts, the drop when the team jumps out of the helicopter, and the finish the moment the frog crosses the finish line, before its leap. A restart is silent. An event that shows in the run's state belongs there, as a new cue with a test. An event the state can't show, such as a moment in an animation, calls the trigger API, `playSound` in `audio/sound-board.ts`, with the sound's id, when it happens.

**Music.** Every course has a theme of its own, a loop about a course's length (a minute or more, in sections rather than one short loop repeated), playing under the sound effects. They are made with ZzFXM, Keith Clark's MIT music renderer for ZzFX: songs are data (instruments as ZzFX parameters, patterns of notes, and the order the patterns play in), rendered in code to an audio buffer, so there are no audio files, and nothing to credit but a folk tune a theme arranges (below). ZzFXM has no npm package, so it is vendored as a TypeScript port with its licence header (`audio/music/zzfxm.ts`), handed ZzFX's own sample builder. The instruments keep the sound effects' soft retro palette, a little brighter, with one brighter voice for the Game Boy's pulse channels: a square wave, kept quiet and through a low-pass so it rings rather than beeps. The craft behind the themes, and what composing them has taught, is `ideas/music.md`.

- **The courses' themes are new compositions,** one per course: for the co-op courses Two Squares Waltz (O and O, the gentle one), Tee Time (T and T, the interlock), Tall and Square (I and O, the odd couple), Mirror, Mirror (S and Z, the mirror pair), Hurry Up and Wait (T and L, chains and pace) and Final Stretch (the hard course). Each matches its course's mood and has a style of its own: a sleepy town waltz with a music box; a calypso for steel pans whose tune is a hocket, dealt out note by note between two pans panned apart, so the two halves interlock; a tuba and piccolo double act; a two-part invention on harpsichord where each phrase is answered by its mirror image; a bouncy ride that changes pace, waiting in half-time and dashing in sixteenths; and a Pokémon battle theme. Hop Along Route, a Pokémon route march with a quiet town in the middle, waits for a course to come. Leg Up and High Rise have no theme of their own yet: each borrows one (Mirror, Mirror and Tall and Square) as a placeholder, marked as such in `course-music.ts`.
- **The Frogmino motif** (`motif.ts`) is the music's identity: hop, hop, leap up the chord and tumble down, a call landing short and an answer landing home. Every course's theme reworks it in its own key, metre and style, as Grant Kirkhope reorchestrates one world theme for each area.
- **The touchstones are Grant Kirkhope and Pokémon.** Kirkhope's Banjo-Kazooie: the walking tuba, chromatic staccato tunes, cheeky woodwind leads, jazzy changes, sudden key shifts and a world theme reorchestrated per area. Pokémon's Game Boy era onward (Junichi Masuda, Go Ichinose): bright, catchy pulse-wave leads, driving route marches, punchy bass, adventurous major-key hooks, battle urgency, and the contrast of town and route. Each theme's card on the page says which traits it borrows.
- **Original tunes, borrowed style.** The themes never quote, arrange or closely paraphrase a melody those games or composers used, nor one from the arcade Frogger or Game Boy Tetris music the earlier sketches drew on (Tetris's Korobeiniki and the Japanese songs Frogger used included): the same distance from Tetris as the look (see the legal note in `ideas/tetris-worlds-look.md`).
- **Folk tunes are the exception.** A theme may arrange a traditional folk melody in the public domain, as Tetris did with Korobeiniki, credited in `credits.ts`, since a melody is a work the site plays. A recording's style (its instruments, groove and tempo) may be borrowed like the games' styles; its own parts may not. No course's theme arranges one; two sketches do (below).
- **Which theme plays where** is `course-music.ts`: each co-op course's theme, by its id. A test fails, naming the course, for a registered co-op course without one, and the game fails loudly on one too. The themes for planned courses that don't exist yet are listed under placeholder names, to move under the course's id when it lands. A course playing another's theme until its own is composed is listed as a placeholder (`PLACEHOLDER_MUSIC`), the one exception to every course having a theme of its own.
- **When it plays.** From the team's jump out of the helicopter, the moment the clock starts, looping, until the team crosses the finish line, where it fades out over a second and a half under the finish fanfare. Lining up aboard and the done screen are quiet. A restart stops it, and the next drop starts it from the top. It plays the same in local and online co-op and replays, since it follows the session's run, as the sound cues do.
- **The mix.** The themes are about as loud as the sound effects, so the music plays at a fixed level some 9 dB under the master volume (`MUSIC_LEVEL` in `music-board.ts`), leaving the effects clear. Mute and the master volume govern it with everything else; there is no separate music volume.
- **Rendering.** A full-length theme takes about a fifth of a second to render on a desktop, and around a second on a phone, too long for the time between two frames, so themes render in a Web Worker and come back as buffers, never holding up the game or a menu. They render ahead, as soon as the next theme is known: the lobby renders the default course's, local co-op's join screen the picked course's, and a waiting room the host's pick; the game asks again as it opens, in case. A render finishing after the drop starts its theme then. The three themes wanted last are kept, since each is some 30 MB of samples. ZzFX creates an AudioContext as it loads and a worker has none, so the worker loads it beside a stand-in. Music plays through ZzFX's AudioContext, the game's one context, so the first input that unlocks the sounds unlocks it too, and a theme started before then waits for it.
- **The earlier sketches** stay on the `?music` page, to listen to, and none plays in the game: themes for the lobby, the run, the hard waves and co-op in the styles of the arcade Frogger, Game Boy Tetris and Kirkhope, a jingle for the finish, and the two Frog Polkkas (`frog-polkka.ts`), which arrange the Finnish folk tune Ievan Polkka, one in the sound of Loituma's a cappella recording and one in the sound of the electronic Hatsune Miku cover. Loituma's arrangement and scat verse are under copyright, and the Miku cover sings that scat verse throughout, so their scat section is a variation of our own; Sammakkopolkka borrows only the polka's style. The tune is credited.
- **The page** lists the courses' themes, each marked with the course it plays on (planned courses by the brief's names), then the themes waiting for a course, then the sketches, each with its use, mood, style traits, key, tempo and length, with play and stop, a loop switch (on for loops, off for jingles), and a copy of the song for the ZzFXM tracker, which plays it a little brighter since its ZzFX has no low-pass filter. It plays at the game's music level, through the same board.
- **Composing.** A theme is a file of data: its notes are lines of note names, one per sixteenth, with dots to rest and dashes to release, compiled to ZzFXM's format, with helpers for writing on coarser grids, a bar per chord, transposing, and mirroring or shifting a line along a scale. Tests hold every bar to a full bar, every note to its instrument's range, every loop to two minutes at most, and every theme loud enough to hear and quiet enough to sit under the effects; an end-to-end test renders one in its worker in a real browser and plays it.

## Open questions

- Are the bonk knock-back (three jumps) and the held-key repeat interval (0.22 s) right? To be tuned in playtesting.
- **Which road widths make good play.** A wider road gives a row room for more decoy vehicles and more openings to weigh; a narrower one is tighter and quicker to read. To be found by playing hand-made rows at each width, starting with local co-op.
- **Co-op feel, to playtest:** the newest press winning when partners press different jumps, and how the shared bonk looks with the frogs apart.
- **Local co-op, to playtest:** whether the split keyboard is comfortable (the right half's , . / beside the arrows) and whether the keyboard drops keys with two players on it; the controller mapping (A hop, X B and LB RB rotate, the stick's thresholds); whether two fit outlines in one colour read, or each wants its frog's colour; and the silhouettes' two tints.
- **Touch controls are on hold.** The owner played the drags in all four directions and the centre-tap hop as built; they work as asked but don't feel right yet, and are set aside to come back to. Open when revisited: the axis lock (or tracking both axes at once), whether the centre tap's hop, on the lift, comes soon enough, and whether the hold to keep jumping is missed on long runs.
- **Co-op's rule calls, to playtest:** a frog in the air staying up through the team's bonk; the kick order (centred, then the turn's own way, up to two lanes) and whether a two-lane kick for every piece, not just the I, feels like a teleport; and the start, each frog in the middle of its half of the road.
- **Online co-op over a real network, to playtest:** whether a correction to the partner's frog reads as the partner simply moving a moment late, or jars (a bonk undone or added, the team's depth jumping); whether a correction's bonk or pass sound playing twice is noticed; whether the rewind window (at least half a second) is long enough on a phone hotspot, or inputs get moved to the host's tick, which shows as the guest's own frog correcting; the latency itself on a phone hotspot and over the internet, and how the first second feels while the guest's clock settles; and a backgrounded tab catching up on return.
- **The designed co-op courses, to playtest:** is coordinating fun, or does one player direct the other? Which rows read at a glance, and which only after a bonk? Does Long and Short teach each stacking move, one at a time, and is the double jump's timing (the I standing up in its slot while the O is still in the air) fair or fiddly? Do the slots read as "stand here", and the van or crane beside them as "fly over"? Is crossing over and back a skill players enjoy learning, and is one direction harder, as meant? Is the reading room right, early and late, and is the road's width right for each pair? Is each course the right length before it goes round again?
- **Stacking, to playtest:** whether ride-along feels right (a slide carrying the partner on top, and a slide going ahead without a rider that has no room); whether the double jump's window, the bottom frog's airtime, is too tight or too loose; whether a turn carrying nothing, so a turn under a partner drops it, reads; and how tall stacks read from the camera, which sits a cell or two above the tallest vehicle: an upright I with a frog on it, hopping, is seven cells up.
- **Kicks through a partner.** A turn's kick tests only the spot the frog lands in, so a two-lane kick can carry a frog through its partner and swap their sides (the solver does this on a make-room row). That contradicts frogs never passing through each other. Undecided: refuse a kick whose path crosses the partner, or keep it as a move; refusing it means re-proving the co-op courses.
- **The solver's reach:** should it also plan moves while a row overlaps the team (sliding through a passed row, riding into the next), and should it race for the fastest route, to set medal times from?
- **The medal times are provisional,** set from the solver's proof and the held-jump floor (see Courses and medals), until playtesting, or a faster automated player, says what a good run takes. Open: whether gold is within reach of a strong player and bronze of a new one, and whether co-op's times should allow for coordinating more than they do. A course's times are for its own finish: change its length and they need setting again.
- The code rename from wall to vehicle terminology, once the model changes.
- **Frog poses that may not read as a frog:** the flat I (it looks like a log), poses with a single bottom cell under a wide top, and head-to-one-side J, L, S and Z poses.
- **The frog seen from behind.** Do the pupils read from the gameplay camera, and does the frog still read as facing down the road? Do the folded hind legs and their kick read as a frog's?
- **The moves' feel.** A move glides over the held-key repeat interval with a small hop (its height is `MOVE_HOP_HEIGHT` in `frog/moves.ts`). Is the drawn frog's lag behind the rules short enough to judge a row by, and is the hop subtle enough held? The camera follows the drawn frog, so a held jump moves it in gentle surges.
