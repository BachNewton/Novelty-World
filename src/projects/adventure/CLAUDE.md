# Adventure

A cozy 3D adventure in the spirit of *Stardew Valley* and *Animal Crossing*: a gentle world to live in rather than a challenge to beat. It is assembled from Kenney's CC0 3D asset packs (kenney.nl), and grown in small slices, each one seen working before the next is chosen. It is its own game, unrelated to Hearthwood.

## Goals

- **Cozy, not testing.** Nothing about play is decided yet beyond the feel: what a day holds, what the player does, whether anything can fail.
- **The look is Kenney's.** Art comes from Kenney's packs as made, so the game shares one simple, toy-like style without art built in code. Where packs leave a gap, how to fill it is open.
- **Built in slices.** Each slice settles only the decisions it meets; the rest stay open below.

## Open

To decide when a slice meets them, by the game's goals:

- Who it is for, and the floor device: desktop only, or phones too.
- Inputs: whether touch plays.
- The world: its scale, whether it streams, and its units (the characters are about 0.7 units tall as made).
- Real-time or turn-based, and whether play goes online.
- The camera in play.
- How long the game is meant to live, and so whether saves must stay readable.
- The game's own visual style beyond the packs: palette tokens, light, sky.

## Decided

- **Rendering.** Plain three.js on `WebGPURenderer` (`three/webgpu`), which falls back to WebGL 2 by itself, so its reach is WebGL's while it stays on the path three.js is developing. The page shows which backend it got, and a lost device stops drawing and says so. No React Three Fiber: the frame loop owns the scene, React only the overlay.
- **The map editor and the character and animal viewer are desktop only.** They are tools for building the game, used with a keyboard and mouse on a big screen, so neither has a phone layout or touch controls, and the repo's phone floor doesn't apply to them.
- **The performance target is the integrated GPU** (the laptop's Radeon 780M) at the monitor's 100 Hz, with the eGPU plugged in or not: the 4070 is far stronger, so what plays well on the integrated GPU plays well on it, and the game is measured on the integrated GPU alone.
- **Assets are served as Kenney ships them**, for now: the packs' GLB files copied unchanged into `public/adventure/`, and where kits differ from one another, each is fitted to the world as it loads (see "Fitting the kits together"). A build tool (compression, merged meshes, one shared texture, a chosen world scale) comes when a slice needs one, most likely the first world built from several packs.
- **Clips are read from the file**, never listed in code: every character carries the same set, and the viewer offers whatever the loaded file holds.
- **Keyboard and mouse, and a controller**, both at once. The pad is laid out as third-person games lay theirs: the left stick walks, the right stick turns and tilts the camera, A jumps, the left stick clicked in runs until it is clicked again or let go to rest, the triggers zoom (right in, left out), and View is the debug overlay's key. The hints show the pad's buttons while a pad was the last thing used, and the keys otherwise.
- **Local co-op, split left and right.** A second player joins on the same machine, as couch co-op games share inputs out: the keyboard and mouse is one input and each pad another. Alone, the first player plays on all of them. Menu on a pad, or Enter on the keyboard, joins a second player on that input, as long as it isn't the one the first player is using (so the keyboard and a pad, or two pads, one each); the second player holds that input alone and the first keeps every other, and the second leaves by pressing it again, or when their pad is unplugged. Two at most, since the screen splits in two. Pressing join never counts as using an input, so it can't make the joining input the first player's. The mouse goes with the keyboard. The second player walks as Male F, the villager by the cart, who steps out of the village while they play and is back in his place when they leave, or as Female F while the first plays as him: a character from the roster that isn't the first player's, and no villager standing twice.

## The debug view

The page is, for now, a debug view: the player, Female F, on the world's ground, and the camera orbiting round her (drag, the arrow keys or a pad's right stick: left and right swing it round, up and down tilt it) and zooming (wheel, pinch, + and −, or a pad's triggers). Panning is off, and the camera never goes below the ground. It opens on idle.

The world is physical: what is seen is met, stood on and jumped onto (`colliders.ts`, `physics.ts`, on Rapier, `@dimforge/rapier3d-compat`). As games collide, each kind of piece is met as a few simple stand-in shapes, never its own triangles, fitted once from its geometry as `kit-fit.ts` fits it, by one rule: **a stand-in only has to be right where the player touches it.** A surface the player stands on matches the drawn one within `STANDING` (a flight of steps within `CLIMBED` of its treads, as a slope over them); what is only bumped into may be rough; a gap the player could pass through or stand in stays open, judged by the player's width with a margin, and any narrower gap is filled (a window's sill, the slots between boards, the dips in a hedge's leaves); and it takes the fewest shapes that keep all that. So each piece starts as one shape (a trunk, a post or a pumpkin an upright cylinder, a rock or a log its hull, anything else the hull of the whole piece) and is cut only where a kept gap or a standing surface needs it, into boxes, wedges and hulls over parts of it (`fitStandIns`, `src/shared/lib/physics/stand-ins.ts`). A piece that is climbed (stairs, the Nature Kit's cliff steps and ramps, sloped paving, bridges) is first its flights, each a slope across the piece along its steps' noses, run down to the ground at its foot so the first step is no lip, the higher of them where two meet as an inner corner's do, with any rail or wall standing up from them fitted on its own. A piece no higher than the lip the player's round foot rides over has none: the ground holds the player, and their drawn feet still follow its drawn surface. The few kinds the rule can't find are set by hand in `OVERRIDES`, each with its reason (the round fountains' rims, a ring round their water). `tools/stand-ins.ts` checks every piece of every kit by the same rule, reporting how far each stand-in's standing surface lies from the drawn one, any gap it closes, whether a climbed piece can be climbed, and how many shapes it takes, and lists the outliers: it is run whenever a pack is added, and its outliers fixed. The ground is boxes too: each cell with ground a column to its height, the columns of one height in a chunk joined into one, so grass, paths, dirt and water are crossed at the grass's level with no lip at their edges; a path's sunk bed and a stream's surface are only drawn lower, and the player's drawn feet dip into them. A rise is its columns, and the cliffs' faces stand in the low cells round it as the map's pieces are met. Boxes of one height that touch in a chunk are joined, so a run of paving or of wall has no seam to catch on. Only what a player would expect to brush through has none: flowers, grass, small plants and bushes, mushrooms, the leafy crops (corn, wheat, greens, bamboo, carrots, turnips), lilies and hanging moss. Roofs are solid, and stood on: every roof the kit makes climbs no steeper than its stairs. Buildings are solid until they have insides: every door and doorway, and the timber arch a barn's side is built of, is closed across its cell by a box as tall as the piece, a wide doorway's two halves together closing the opening; the stone arch stays open, since it is a way through a wall rather than into a building, so the town wall's gate is walked through under its arch, and a ruin's fallen side into the ruin. Water is met at its face, so a player wades through a stream at its surface and stands in a fountain's basin on its water, from where its rim is a step up. A box over every empty cell (see "The ground") and a wall round the grid's edge stop the players walking or falling into nothing, and nothing else: no ray meets them.

A player is a capsule (`BODY_RADIUS`, `BODY_HEIGHT`) slimmer than the drawn character, as games make it: narrow enough, with its skin and `PASS_MARGIN`, to fit the narrowest ways the kits mean to be walked (the walk between a flight's handrails, a hedge's gate, measured from the pieces), its head and arms let brush into a wall; as tall as the characters stand. The villagers are the same, and the animals are cylinders slimmed by as much (`SLIM`). It moves by Rapier's kinematic character controller: `player.ts` says how far a step asks to go (the gait's speed, a jump's rise and fall) and the controller says where it ends, sweeping the capsule along the move so nothing is passed through at any speed or on any frame, sliding it along a wall and round a corner, and standing it on top of whatever it lands on. On the ground a move goes along the face it stands on, read by a ray straight down, up or down it as it slopes, and is then pressed straight down onto it: the controller fits a level move to a slope by squeezing it onto the face, which would halve a walk's speed up a flight, and a press mixed into a move across slides it back. So a walk goes up and down a flight or a ramp as fast as on the level, and never faster than asked: where two shapes meet edge to edge, the controller's keeping its skin's width off the seam would carry a body a hair further, and a move is never let go further across than it asked. It walks up and stands on any slope up to `MAX_SLOPE`, steep enough for every flight and roof the kits make, and anything steeper drops it, sliding off at the speed of a fall; it is kept on the ground going down by up to `SNAP`; and a lip as low as a curb is ridden over by the capsule's round foot. Nothing is climbed as a step: what stands up higher than a lip, a pumpkin, a fence, a rock or a bench, is jumped onto or walked round. The villagers and the animals block where they stand, and the players block each other; the villager a player plays as is taken out while they play. The villagers and animals stand where a ray down meets the ground, cast from a little over the ground's own height in their cell, so a stall's awning or a tree's crown over them is never stood on; they are stood again whenever the colliders change. On the ground a player's feet are drawn on the drawn surface under them (a ray onto the drawn pieces there, no further than `FOLLOW` from where the world stands them): into a path's bed, on a stair's tread under the slope the world walks them up, every change eased in as a short settle, the camera following more slowly, so walking into a path's bed or up a flight reads as steps rather than a slide or a jolt. Rapier is loaded only by this page, and the world is drawn while it loads; no one moves until it has, and the page says so if it fails.

Adding or removing a collider doesn't update the tree Rapier's queries walk until the world steps, and a query in between can trap its WebAssembly. Nothing here is simulated, so the world steps only for that: every change to the colliders is asked for by group (a layer's chunk, the empty cells, the bodies, each player) and applied at the start of a frame, straight before a step, and the players' moves and the rays come after it (`ColliderGroups`, in `src/shared/lib/physics/`). A map editor's change rebuilds only the chunks it touched, their colliders with them, so editing costs collision as little as drawing.

The ground runs 80 cells a side, the village in its middle and the country round it (see "The country"). WASD or a pad's left stick walks the character about, relative to the camera (W, or the stick pushed up, goes away from it), and Shift held with them, or a pad's left stick clicked in, sprints; the stick pushed part way walks slower, down to about a third of the full walk, with the walk clip slowed to match so a planted foot still stays still, while a sprint always runs at full speed; the camera follows, and so does the sun's shadow box, small and sharp round the player. Only the players cast shadows, so nothing else moves in and out of the sun's reach. A village built from the town kit's pieces stands round where the player starts, and uses every one of the kit's pieces somewhere it makes sense, so it doubles as a map of how each piece is put together with the others; only pieces that have no place yet that suits them are left out (the half chamfers, the full stairs' inner corner, the plaster small window, the narrow, corner and spiral stone stairs, the wide stone stairs with a rail, the timber half block, and the spare sail). At its heart, round a green with market stalls and lanterns, stand a stone cottage, an inn of stone below and timber above with a balcony up its side and a chimney at its back, a windmill on a stone tower, a mill house whose wheel turns in a race along its back, a stone smithy with a fenced yard, and a lookout under a pointed roof. South of the green a paved street leads to a plaza with a fountain, a town hall on a stepped stone plinth, a street east round a bend and up a ramp to a guildhall on a raised terrace, a shop with a loggia, a house with bent corners and a balcony, and a small house chamfered at every corner, with a porch. West of the plaza is a walled garden with ponds, a fountain and a pavilion; beyond it, at the village's west edge, the old town wall, its rampart walked behind a parapet, a gate two storeys high between two towers where the street leaves the village paved, a round tower at its north end, stairs up to its walk, and a crumbled end in rubble, and inside it a gatekeeper's hut, two ruined houses and a coach house with a trough. North, past the windmill, a farm lane runs from an L-shaped farmhouse past a granary rounded at its ends, a barn, a privy, a house going up in a frame of poles, a tool shed and a paddock to an eight-sided chapel and an L-shaped manor. East of the mill race, a sawmill turns a wide wheel in it. Trees of every kind the kit has stand round it all. The ground is the Nature Kit's grass, with dirt paths from each door to a street through the village and a lane through the farm, and a few flowers and tufts of grass by the doors. Every other character of the pack stands about the village playing its idle, each placed by what is there (keeping a stall, at a door, by a cart), their idles out of step so none breathe together; like the town, they cast no shadow. A few sit, on the market's bench and stool: the `sit` clip swings the legs forward but leaves the thighs sloping down, and the leg blocks reach below the hips, so no height rests both body and thighs on a seat as made: a sitter's hips go a little ahead of the seat's middle and a little above its top, chosen by eye across every character. Every animal of the Cube Pets pack stands somewhere its kind belongs, playing its idle, out of step with each other and the villagers: a cow and a pig at the paddock's trough, a chick at the barn, a hog in the pen by the ditch; the innkeeper's dog at his feet and a cat at the market; a bunny at the kitchen garden's carrots and a caterpillar at its greens; a bee among the meadow's flowers and a giraffe grazing there; at the oasis a parrot and a monkey under the palms, an elephant drinking and a lion watching it from the ledge; a tiger in the wildwood, a deer in the autumn wood, a fox north of the farm, a koala at a pine in the west wood; a polar bear at the waterfall's pool, a beaver on the river's bank, and at the lake a panda in the bamboo, a penguin at the jetty's end, a crab on the sand and a fish with its back out of the water. They stand on the ground under them as villagers do, up on a rise too, cast no shadow, and the ~ overlay names each by its file (`animal-cow`). Space or a pad's A jumps, on the press, so holding it doesn't bounce; the player can still steer in the air. The jump clips hold a pose without moving the body, so the game supplies the height: `jump` plays while rising and `fall` while coming down. Walking, sprinting and standing still play the `walk`, `sprint` and `idle` clips, blended into each other; a clip chosen in the viewer (see "The character and animal viewer") plays until the character sets off. The ground speeds keep a planted foot still: each is how fast its clip sweeps a foot backward as it touches down, measured from the clip's leg swing. The character moves on the frame's own time step, which is all a debug walk needs: whether play runs on a fixed step stays open with real-time play. The player can hold one of the pack's weapons in either hand, chosen in the viewer; the `holding-*`, `*-shoot` and `attack-melee-*` clips are the ones made for a held weapon. A reload always opens on Female F, idle, holding nothing. The shared frame meter sits in the top right.

Enter on the keyboard, or Menu on a pad the first player isn't using, brings in a second player beside the first, to their right as the first's camera sees it, with a camera looking the same way, and the screen splits: the first player on the left, the second on the right, a line between. Each half is its own camera following its player, turned, tilted and zoomed by that player alone; each player has their own ground height, settle, gait and animation on the one world. Each half shows its own player's hints, in the keys or the pad they play on; alone, the hints add how a second joins (Menu, and Enter too while the first plays on a pad), and the second's half adds how they leave. The viewer is the first player's. Each half has the sun's shadow drawn round its own player: before a half is drawn, the sun's small box is moved round that half's player, and round the other player too while that half sees them near (`SHADOW_SEEN`), so a player's own shadow is as sharp as one player's however far apart they are, and the other's is there wherever it would be seen. A half that sees the other player near holds both in a box grown by how far apart they are, a little softer; further off, the other is small enough in the view that their shadow isn't missed. The renderer draws a light's shadow once for each camera a frame, so each half's shadow is its own pass. Drawing a shadow walks the whole scene, however few things cast one (here only the players), as drawing a view does, so the second player costs the second view and its shadow: the scene is drawn six times a frame (each view, its shadow and the output pass three adds for a view) against three alone. The two halves together shade as many pixels as one full view; at the opening views two players draw about one and a half times the draws and triangles of one (264 draws and 94 thousand triangles against 148 and 65 thousand with the players side by side).

~, or a pad's View, toggles the debug overlay, for the owner to name what they see in the code's own words: over each thing near the player it floats the name the code gives it (a kit piece's, a plant's, a character's, and over a whole building or part of the town the name of the constant that places it in `town.ts`, such as `INN` or `PLAZA`; over a cliff the ground's heights stand, its kit piece's name, marked apart from a placed piece's (`CLIFF_MARK` and `CLIFF_TONE` in `scene/debug-overlay.ts`), since no place puts it there and the editor's raise or face brush, not the palette, makes more of it), numbers the cells round the player in the corner the code names each by, draws the colliders within three cells of them in pink as Rapier holds them (the stand-ins of the pieces and the ground, and the capsules and cylinders of the villagers, animals and players): what never moves asked of Rapier again only when the player moves to another cell or the colliders change, the bodies every frame, after everyone has moved, so a capsule is drawn where its player is, and shows the player's cell and exact place at the top of the screen. With two players it is open for both or neither (either one's key toggles it), each half naming what is round its own player and numbering its cells, with each player's place shown over their hints. Labels at one point, such as two pieces in one cell, stack. A label is drawn pulled a little toward the camera, so what it names doesn't cut through it, while anything between it and the camera still hides it.

### The character and animal viewer

C (or a pad's Y, or the button at the top left) opens the viewer: every character and every animal, one at a time on a stage of their own, a disc of the world's grass, with the camera turned round them by a drag, the arrow keys or a pad's right stick, and zoomed by the wheel, + and − or the triggers. The panel on the left shows the roster as portraits, characters by head and shoulders and animals whole, the character the player plays as badged; under them the clips of whoever is on the stage, those made for a held weapon marked with a sword; and for a character, what they hold (nothing, or a weapon by its picture) and in which hand. A pad moves round the panel with its d-pad or left stick and presses with A; B, Y or C closes it. Play waits while the viewer is open, exactly as it was: no one moves, and a second player who joins or leaves meanwhile does so once it closes.

Closing the viewer with a character on the stage makes the first player that character, where they stood, holding what was chosen and playing the clip chosen until they set off; the villager who is that character steps out of the village while they play, as the second player's does. Closing it on an animal leaves the player as they were: playing as an animal waits on its own gait, each pack's walk and run speeds measured as the characters' were, and then needs only the stage's choice carried over the same way.

The stage is its own small scene drawn by the game's one renderer in the world's place while the viewer is open, rather than the world with the camera on the player: a model reads clearly against nothing behind it, the world needn't stand still round a pose, and while the viewer is shut the frame loop never touches the stage, so play costs nothing for it. Each subject is framed as it comes onto the stage, from the way the camera looked: an animal by its own size, a character by one frame every character shares, since a skinned model's bounds are its bind pose's. The portraits are taken the first time the viewer opens, each drawn once into an offscreen target of the format the screen is drawn through and read back as an image, and kept for the page's life (the map editor's pictures of the pieces are taken the same way); the map editor, opened over the viewer, draws in its place, and the viewer is back when it closes.

### The map editor

M (or the map button at the bottom left) opens the map editor over the town, and closes it back to play with the camera where it was. While it is open the left button is the editor's: click a piece to select it (outlined in pink, drawn over whatever hides it) and move it by its handles, three.js's transform gizmo: an arrow along each way, by whole cells across the map and a quarter storey up (with Shift, a twentieth), or, after T, a ring that turns it a quarter at a time (a see-through copy shows where it will go). Q and E turn it a quarter either way, R and F raise and lower it a quarter storey (with Shift, a twentieth), X mirrors it, and Delete takes it away. The panel shows the selected piece and its place (the constant in `town.ts` that places it) on one line, and where it stands on the next, in a box two lines high whatever it holds, so nothing below it moves as a piece is picked or selected. The palette under it shows every piece of both kits as a picture with its name under it, four to a row, grouped by what each is for and found by the words of its name ("wood door"), a house or a leaf marking its kit, and the two kits' buttons under the search narrow it to either (neither, or both, shows every piece); pick one and click a cell to place it, as many times as wanted, turned, mirrored and raised by the same keys before it goes down. A town piece that the kit's variation A colour map really changes is listed twice, the second time pictured in those colours and marked A after its name (`roof-high` A), and placed in them: the high roofs, the red banner and the red stall's awning blue, and the low roofs, the green banner and the green stall's awning purple. Pieces it leaves as they are (walls, stairs, paving, most of the kit) are listed once. The search finds both, and "variation" finds only the pieces in it; the selected piece's name carries the same mark. The Nature Kit has no texture. The cell under the pointer is outlined in blue at the height of what is being placed or moved. I or a middle click (a middle drag still slides the camera), the eyedropper, picks up whatever is under the pointer to make more of it, whichever the left button is doing: a placed piece is picked from the palette, in its texture, turned, mirrored and raised as it stands, its group opened and scrolled to whatever the search and the kit buttons had narrowed it to; a tile or a cliff of the ground takes up the brush that makes it (see below). Its button does the same for the next click, the pointer a crosshair until then. Esc lets go of the eyedropper's click, else of the piece picked, or else of the selection. Ctrl+Z undoes, back through the last two hundred changes, and Ctrl+Y redoes. The camera is the editor's own: the right button turns it, the middle slides it, the wheel and + and − zoom it further out than play's, the arrow keys turn and tilt it, and WASD slide it over the map (Shift faster). The panel shows each action's key, and its buttons do the same.

The palette's pictures are the game's own pieces as the map draws them, fitted to the world (the Nature Kit in Adventure's tokens, which Kenney's own previews aren't), each from above a front corner where a town wall shows its outside. Each is drawn on the game's renderer the first time its row comes into view, which a closed group's never does, and kept for the page's life, so opening the editor costs nothing until a group is opened, and a group costs one small offscreen draw and read-back for each picture, a few dozen at most. Drawn rather than kept as image files, they need no tool to run again, nor the repo to hold them, and are never out of step when a piece's fitting or a token changes.

What a click would select is lightened under the pointer: the piece drawn again over itself, faintly and only where it is seen, well short of the selection's pink outline. Nothing is lightened over a handle, while a drag turns or slides the camera or moves a handle, or while a piece is picked from the palette, whose see-through copy shows where it goes. It is picked once a frame at most, from where the pointer last was, and only when the pointer, the camera or the map moved since; a pick tests the boxes of the chunks the ray passes through, nearest first, then the boxes of their pieces, then the faces of those it meets, so hovering costs little however big the map.

G (or the brush button beside the pieces one) turns the left button to painting the ground: click a cell, or drag across cells, to paint them with the brush, a stroke one change for undo. The first row of brushes paints a kind at whatever height each cell stands: grass (1), which rubs dirt and water out, dirt (2), water (3), and empty (4), which takes the ground away; a stretch two cells or more across is a dirt patch or a pond, and a way one cell wide a track or a stream, so the kinds are only these and the tiles follow. The second row raises (5) and lowers (6) the ground: each cell a stroke crosses goes up or down a storey, once a stroke (a stroke finds its cells at the height of the one it started on, so the ground it raises never stands in its way), or half a storey with Shift held as the stroke starts, no lower than the grid's level and no higher than four and a half storeys, and the cliffs round it follow (see "The ground"); a cell raised takes the cliff chosen in the row under the brushes, any the ground's cliff marks can make, each by its picture, or, chosen first, the stone of the highest rise beside it where it is raised from the grid's level, so a rise grown outward keeps its look, and an empty cell has nothing to raise. The face brush (7, and again for the next face) gives each cell a stroke crosses the face chosen in its row, any of the faces a map's file names (`FACES` in `cliffs.ts`), or rubs a cell's face out, a stroke one change for undo; it gives one only to a cell with ground, as a map's file does, and each stands only where it fits (see "The ground"). Every cell round a painted one is laid again by the same rules as the code's ground (see "Fitting the kits together"), its tile and turn from which neighbours are the same kind, so a bend, branch or end never needs turning by hand; a shape the kit has no tile for, such as a narrow way running into a wide stretch flush with its side, is laid as the narrow way joining the same neighbours, and crossed out in orange while the editor is open (`unfitted` in `ground.ts`), so a join that looks wrong for want of a piece the kit lacks is told from one the painter got wrong. Where the kit makes more than one tile for a cell's shape, V (or its button) gives the cell under the pointer the next: a straight plain or strewn with rocks, a bend plain or with a wide bank round its outside, an end closed in a bank or running out level. A cell remembers the tile chosen for it while its shape still has it; a stroke that changes its shape lets it go, and the cell takes the plain tile. A kind's brush may carry a tile of its own, shown under the brushes, which the eyedropper gives it: each cell a stroke crosses whose shape the kit makes it for takes it, and the others the plain tile; Esc, or choosing a brush, lets it go. The eyedropper on the ground takes up the brush that makes what it is on: a tile, its kind's brush with the tile chosen for its cell; a face, a post or a corner of a cliff, the raise brush with the faces of the raised cell it stands against; a face a cell shows in place of its plain one, the face brush with that face. An end with no tile chosen runs out level where it meets the foot of steps or a ramp at its own height (the ground's own, or the Nature Kit's cliff steps and sloped blocks and the town kit's stairs placed on the map), and is closed elsewhere. The cell a stroke would change is the first the pointer meets on the ground, on its top or up its side, lightened as a piece is and outlined in blue at its height.

The map is data. The page opens on the map saved in this browser if there is one, and on the town as the code places it if not, and every change is saved to the browser as it is made (`nw:adventure:map` in local storage). While a saved map is shown, the ↺ button throws it away and goes back to the code's town; a saved map that can't be read says why and keeps the editor shut until it is thrown away. Save downloads the map as a JSON file, and Load reads one in the map's place, as one change undo takes back. A file is read whole or not at all: an unknown piece, a missing or misspelt field, a place named twice, a row of the ground of the wrong length or with a mark that is no kind, no height or no cliff, an empty cell raised, a tile chosen off the ground, on grass or an empty cell, twice or that the kit hasn't, a face that is none, off the ground, on an empty cell or given twice, a texture the town kit hasn't or on a Nature Kit piece, or another format's version refuses it, naming every problem.

A map's file holds the pieces and the ground: `{ "version": 4, "places": [{ "name": "INN", "placements": [{ "piece": "wall-door", "x": -6, "z": -7, "turn": 1 }, { "piece": "roof-high", "x": -6, "z": -6, "y": 2, "texture": "variation-a" }, ...] }, ...], "ground": { "rows": ["....dd..ww...", ...], "heights": ["0000224444...", ...], "cliffs": ["pppppPPPP...", ...], "variants": [{ "x": 6, "z": -28, "tile": "Rocks" }, ...], "faces": [{ "x": -20, "z": -30, "face": "steps" }, ...] } }`, one placement a line, each a `Placement` as the code writes it (`piece`, `x`, `z`, and `y`, `turn`, `mirror` and `texture` where it has them; a town piece without a `texture` wears the kit's own colour map). Its places are the code's: `TREES`, each part of the town by its constant in `PLACES`, and `PLANTS`, and pieces placed in the editor go into `PLACED`. So a map maps back onto the code: a session bringing an edited map into the game reads each place's placements into its constant. The ground is each cell's kind, height and cliff, never its tiles or faces, which follow from them: three sets of 80 rows from the grid's −z edge, each 80 marks from its −x end, one row a line: the kinds (`.` grass, `d` dirt, `w` water, `x` empty), the heights in half storeys (a digit, `0` to `9`), and the cliffs (`p` plain, `l` large, `s` steps, in capitals for red rock, which a cell at the grid's level carries unseen); then the tiles chosen where the kit makes more than one for a shape, by cell (`Rocks`, `BendBank` and `End`, or a plain one where the choice overrides an end that would run out level), and the faces cells show in place of the plain one (`steps`, `ramp`, `cave`, `cut`). The code's ground, its paths, the country's water, dirt, rough stretches and open ends, and its rises (`COUNTRY_RISES`), is one such ground (`codeGround`), so the editor starts from the ground the code lays; bringing a painted ground into the code means turning its rows back into paths, patches and rises. The villagers stay in the code: they are characters, not pieces. The version tells a later format's reader an old file from a new one: a version 1 file, from before the ground was the map's, is read with the code's ground; a version 1 or 2 file, from before textures, has every piece in the kit's own colours; and a file of versions 1 to 3, from before the ground had heights, when the country's rises stood as pieces among its places, is read on the code's rises: each cell the code leaves at the grid's level takes the file's kind and chosen tile, each it raises keeps the code's (the file's lay hidden under the rise), and a piece the rises' heights now stand (every cliff, and every tile above the grid's level) is dropped from its place, so the rises aren't drawn twice. A piece moved off where the rise stood it stays a piece.

Outside the editor the map and its ground are drawn as they always are, merged by chunk; in it, a change rebuilds only the chunks it touched. A stroke lays again only the cells within two of those it changed (a tile follows its neighbours, and whether each is part of a wide stretch, which follows theirs; a cliff follows its neighbours' heights, and whether a cut beside it stands, which follows the cut's), and a cell whose tile and cliffs come out the same keeps their objects, so only the chunks whose cells changed are rebuilt; steps or a ramp moved on the map lay the whole ground again, since any end may run out onto them. What stands on the ground follows it: the colliders are rebuilt with the chunks, and the villagers stood again once a change is drawn. So editing costs play nothing.

## Building with the town kit

A session placing or building with the Fantasy Town Kit starts from these tools, not from guesses at the pieces:

- **`buildings.ts`** holds the kit's rules for putting a building together, in its opening comment: which way a wall's outside faces and so where its piece goes, posts and pillars, the kit's corner walls (squared, rounded, chamfered, cut, bent and curved, four curved making a round tower), open frames, the halves of two-cell openings and their mirrors, storeys and plinths, the roofs (gabled one, two or three cells wide, flat-topped, hipped, round, pointed, with dormers, each high and red or low and teal), what hangs on a wall and what it mustn't cover, chimneys and balconies. `building()` turns a footprint, its storeys and their wall pieces into placements, facing any way, and `winged()` an L of a hipped block and a wing under one roof with a valley. Its tests pin the turns and ends that are easy to get wrong. A shape the kit offers that the builder can't make yet is worked out on the bench, then added to the builder and its rules. Pieces that aren't part of a building's shell (sails, a mill wheel, stalls, fences, hedges, paving, basins, stairs to a wall's walk, lanterns) are placed directly, by the helpers in `town.ts` where a kind of piece makes a shape of its own: curbed paving, a ring of hedge or fence, a basin built from its rim's pieces. A placement can be mirrored, for the pieces the kit makes in one hand only.
- **The piece bench**, `?view=pieces` (`&kit=nature` for the Nature Kit): every piece of a kit on the grid, named, with a pink arrow along its own +x (where a wall stands) and a blue one along +z, to walk round.
- **The kit's own pictures** in Kenney's download: `Previews/` shows each piece alone, and `Sample.png` a village built from them, the one place the kit shows how its pieces go together (a porch, an arch two storeys high, stairs up a building's side). A piece's use is checked against it before it is placed.
- **`tools/look.mjs`** shoots stills of the game or the bench from exact views, headless, so a session sees what it built.
- **`tools/kit-bounds.mjs`** measures pieces from their files: their extent, and where their highest points lie, which tells which end of a slope is up.

A piece's name is not proof of what it is. Some of the kit's pieces aren't what their names suggest (`wall-wood-window-small` is a plaster wall with no timber, `wall-wood-corner-edge` a wall bent across its cell rather than a post), and some decorations are made to lie in a wall's very face. So each new piece is looked at on the bench before it is used.

Pieces are named by `TownPiece`, so a misspelt piece fails the typecheck. A building is judged in the running game from all round, as art is.

## Fitting the kits together

The world is built from several of Kenney's kits, made apart, so each kit is fitted to one ground and one palette as its files load (`scene/kit-fit.ts`), never by editing the files:

- **Height.** The town's pieces and the characters stand on y = 0. The Nature Kit's grass lies 0.05 low and its paths are sunk 0.05 below that, so its pieces are lifted to put the grass a hair under 0: a town piece's base, such as a basin's floor seen through its water, never fights the grass for depth. A path's bed stays sunk as drawn: the world holds the player at the grass's level across it, and their drawn feet dip into it (see "The debug view").
- **Materials.** The Nature Kit's files mark every material wholly metallic, and write their colours as the screen shows them where glTF means them linear; both are corrected, so its pieces draw matte in their own colours.
- **Palette.** Its greens are teal and its dirt pale beside the town kit's colour map, so its grass, leaves and dirt are repainted with Adventure's own tokens (`scene/tokens.css`), each a swatch of the town kit's map. Its flowers and other colours keep its own.
- **Scale.** The kits share a one-unit cell and a storey of 1 (the Nature Kit's cliffs climb what a town wall does), and every piece is drawn as made: each kit is in proportion within itself, so the only scale a kit could take is one for the whole kit, and anything but 1 would take its tiles off the shared grid. Neither kit is drawn to real-world sizes but each shrinks the world its own way for the cartoon look (a storey of the town kit's stairs has eight steps, a Nature cliff's five; the Nature Kit's fences and trees are lower than the town kit's), so a difference in size between the kits is their style, not an error, and where two styles sit badly together the fix is which pieces go where, never their size. The characters are drawn as made, 0.67 to 0.85 tall against a storey of 1: a little taller than the town kit's doorways (0.75), which are the outside of a building, not a way in at the characters' own scale. The Cube Pets don't sit on the grid, so the pack takes one scale for all of it as it loads, a quarter (`ANIMAL_SCALE`): as made its animals stand 1.4 to 2 tall (most about 1.6, measured through their nodes' transforms), twice a character; at a quarter, 0.36 to 0.5, so a dog's or a cow's head comes to a character's hip, a pet beside its owner, and a farm animal as small as the pack's one size for every animal makes it.

The two kits' paths are for different places: the town kit's `road` pieces are paving with curbs, raised on the ground, for a village's streets and squares; the Nature Kit's `ground_path*` tiles are dirt tracks sunk in grass, for ways through the country. The Nature Kit lays dirt and water alike, its river tiles the shapes of its path tiles (`ways.ts`): a way one cell wide joins by which neighbours are path (or water) too, its ends closed in a rounded bank; a stretch two cells or more across is open in its middle, banked along its sides, rounded at its outer corners, with a nub at an inner one, and opens between two nubs where a narrow way leaves it; a lone cell is a pit or pond of its own. The kit's `End` is not that joint but a way running out level, onto steps, a ramp or a field, and its `Rocks` and `BendBank` are a straight and a bend made rough.

## The ground

The ground is data, cell by cell over the whole grid (`ground.ts`): each cell's kind (grass, dirt, water, or empty), its height in half storeys, up to four and a half storeys, and how the faces round it are made where it is raised. Nothing drawn on it is stored: the tiles follow from the kinds (see "Fitting the kits together") and the cliffs from the heights (`cliffs.ts`), as a path's bends follow from its neighbours, so neither a broken bank nor a broken cliff can be made.

- **Heights.** Each cell's tile lies at its height, and a way joins only its neighbours of its kind at its own height, so a path on a rise ends in a bank at the edge rather than running into the face below. Water joins water below it too, running on over the edge, so a stream on a rise reaches the edge open while the pool under it keeps its bank.
- **Cliffs.** Every face, corner and post round raised ground stands in the low cells round it, a storey at a time from each low cell's height to its highest neighbour's, as the kit builds them (the opening comment of `cliffs.ts`); where a neighbour is only half a storey higher, the kit's half faces. A raised cell's faces are grey stone or red rock, and plain, large below their top storey, or steps all round. A face whose raised cell is water is a waterfall. A low cell between rises on opposite sides has a face on each, and one walled in on three or four sides an inner corner wherever two sides meet, so any heights at all stand whole.
- **Faces.** A low cell may show another face at its own level in place of the plain one: steps up to the cell it faces, a walled ramp (up a storey, or the kit's half ramp up a half), a cave's mouth, or, at a corner of a rise, the corner cut on the diagonal, its diagonal faces up to the lower of its two raised sides and no post at its corners. Each stands only where it fits, the cell showing its plain faces elsewhere; raising and lowering keep it, and painting the cell empty takes it away. The code's rises give them, and the editor's face brush.
- **Empty cells.** An empty cell has no ground: nothing of its own is drawn, and a box over it stops the players walking, jumping or falling into it. Its height is the grid's level. Higher ground beside it still stands its faces in it, down to the grid's level, so raised ground beside a hole is a cliff over nothing, while ground at the grid's level ends in its tile's own thin edge, as the grid's edge does; through the hole shows the sky, as past the grid's edge.
- **What stays placed.** The kit's blocks (`cliff_block*`: whole, half, quarter, sloped and diagonal), of which the quarry's benches and the badlands' blocks are built, are pieces on the ground, since a quarter block is finer than the heights; they are moved and deleted as pieces, and the ground under them is the grid's. So are moss on a face, a block filling a cave's mouth, and whatever stands on a rise.
- **Standing.** The ground is met as its columns, each cell's at its height, with the faces, steps, ramps and cut corners its cliffs stand met as the map's pieces are (see "The debug view"). Raised ground has nothing under it at the grid's level, so a player climbs it only by steps, a ramp or a jump up a half storey, and its faces stop them as a wall does.
- **Drawing.** The ground's tiles and cliffs are drawn merged by chunk as the map's pieces are, on a layer of their own (`scene/ground-tiles.ts`), so a rise costs the frame what placed pieces would, a draw for each material in each chunk, and a raised cell is one tile, at its height, with nothing under it.

## The country

Round the village lies the country, built from every piece of the Nature Kit (`country.ts`), each part named in the ~ overlay by its constant. Across the north runs a grey stone `RIDGE` two storeys high, a terrace a storey up along its foot with steps up to it and on to the top, a spur jutting south with a cave in its face and its west corner cut on the diagonal, moss trailing down its faces, and on top a pine wood (`RIDGE_WOOD`), a walk to a lookout's fire ring, and a stream from a spring that crosses the walk under an arched footbridge and goes over the edge as a waterfall into a pool. The `RIVER` runs from the pool south past the town wall, winding and narrow, over rocks, with footbridges where the paths cross it, a spring's brook joining it and a stepping stone, then broadens past the road, which crosses on a stone bridge of three spans, and an arched timber bridge on the way south, into a lake in the south-west. Its `LAKE_SHORE` has an island out on an arched stone bridge, a bay crossed by a timber bridge, a jetty with a canoe tied up, sand along the east shore, lilies, hummocks and reeds; a `FISHING_CAMP` of two tents round a fire pit stands by it. West of the river a pine wood (`WEST_WOOD`) holds a `WEST_CAMP` round its fire; between the river and the wall is the `WILDWOOD`. North of the farm a `QUARRY` is cut back in benches of stone blocks, with ramps up them and a working in its face; north-east, an `AUTUMN_WOOD` of turned leaves with a `CRAG` of stone on a broad base and a `WOODCUTTERS` yard. East of the village the farm track runs between fields watered by a brook and ditches crossed by small bridges (`FIELD_BRIDGES`): `CORN` sown a row at a time, `WHEAT` on looped double rows, a `KITCHEN_GARDEN`, a fenced `PUMPKIN_PATCH` whose four corners are each the kit's own corner (square, diagonal, bent, bent with a post), a `PEN` cut at its corners, bamboo by the water, and `HEDGEROWS`. South-east is red-rock badlands round a dry wash: a `MESA` with a shelf, steps up both, a cave, a cut corner and a waterfall into an `OASIS` of palms; a half-storey `LEDGE` with a ramp, a `KNOLL` stepped all round, a `BUTTE` on a broad foot, `BLOCKS` weathered into a stepped shelf, and spires, boulders and cacti (`BADLANDS`). South of the village, stepping stones lead to a `RUIN_HILL`, a temple fallen to its columns on a hill stepped all round, and branch at a round flag to a `STONE_CIRCLE`; a low `STONE_LEDGE` lies in the `MEADOW`.

- **Height.** Each rise is raised ground (see "The ground"): `COUNTRY_RISES` lays it in the code's ground as rectangles of heights, a storey or half a storey each, with its stone, its faces and its cut corners, so the editor raises, lowers and paints it as any other ground. The streams on the ridge and the mesa and the ridge's walk are water and dirt on the raised cells. Pieces on a path's or dirt patch's sunk bed stand 0.05 lower, and those floating on water (lilies, a canoe, hummocks) at the water's level. The player wades through water at its surface, the water's own face being what they stand on.
- **Scattered growth.** Woods and meadows are scattered on a loose grid from a fixed seed, so they stand where they stood, each kept clear of water, paths, dirt, the village, every cell another piece stands in and every cell a rise raises or stands a face in, and what grows under the trees kept clear of their trunks. Each scatter goes round its list of pieces, so every tree, flower and mushroom it lists appears.
- **The kit's own pictures.** The Nature Kit's download shows each piece from all four corners in `Isometric/<piece>_<NE|NW|SE|SW>.png` and from the side in `Side/`, and in `Sample.png` an island built from them: cliffs storeys high with grass on top, a waterfall, a river under bridges, a tent and a fire, palms on sand, and fields on stepped terraces. A cliff's shape is read from the bench before it is placed: which edge a face stands on, which corner a post fills, which way a ramp, stairs or a cave's mouth faces (`tools/kit-bounds.mjs` and a top-down still settle it).
- **Left out.** Every piece of the kit has a place. Its narrow path crossing is used by the village's paths, where two meet.

## Code map

- `characters.ts`: the Mini Characters roster, by file name, and where each is served.
- `villagers.ts`: which character each player is, where every other one stands or sits and which way they look, and the kit's seats by height.
- `animals.ts`: the Cube Pets roster, by file name, where each is served, and the pack's one scale. `fauna.ts`: where every animal stands and which way it looks.
- `input/controls.ts`: the game's keyboard and controller: actions bound to keys by their place on the keyboard and to a pad's buttons and sticks (through `src/shared/lib/gamepad/`), held keys and every standard pad tracked outside React, and read by the frame loop once a frame as what each player asks for, the keys winning where both ask. It tells the page only the lineup, which the hints follow.
- `input/lineup.ts`: who plays with what, pure: the input the first player uses, the one a second holds, and how join, leaving and an unplugged pad change them. A game's input is not a page's: it is read per frame in actions, not handled per key event, so it doesn't use the shared hooks built for tools.
- `fantasy-town.ts` and `nature-kit.ts`: every piece of each kit by name, and where each is served, and the town kit's other colour maps; `kit.ts` names a piece of either.
- `ways.ts`: the grid's extent and cells, and the Nature Kit's tiles for a stretch of path or water, each the tile and turn its neighbours call for, cell by cell or over a stretch, and the tiles the kit makes alike for one shape.
- `ground.ts`: the ground as data, pure: each cell's kind, height and cliff, the tiles chosen and the faces, the code's ground (grass everywhere but the paths, the country's dirt patches, its water and its rises), what each cell draws (its tile at its height, and its cliffs), painting, raising and lowering cells, choosing and cycling a cell's tile, giving cells a face, and which cells a change reaches.
- `cliffs.ts`: the Nature Kit's cliffs, in its opening comment: how its faces, corners, steps and diagonals stand round raised ground; `cliffPieces()`, which derives the pieces standing in a cell from the heights round it; and `Rise`, the code's way of laying a rise as rectangles.
- `country.ts`: the country as placements, water, dirt, paths and rises, its parts named in `COUNTRY` for the overlay, and the scattering of woods and meadows clear of everything placed and raised.
- `editor/map.ts`: the map as data, pure: the document, the code's map, an edit as a new map, and the JSON file written and read (validated whole). `editor/saved.ts` keeps it in the browser, `editor/palette.ts` lists the palette's entries (each piece, and the town pieces again in each colour map that changes them), groups them and searches them, `editor/keys.ts` holds the editor's keys, and `editor/eyedropper.ts` says what the eyedropper picks up from a placed piece, a tile or a cliff: which entry or brush, and how it stands or what it makes.
- `town-variants.ts`: the town pieces each of the kit's other colour maps changes, written by `tools/town-variants.mjs`, which lays each piece's triangles over the maps in UV space and lists the pieces that sample a texel that differs; rerun it when the kit or a map changes.
- `scene/map-editor.ts`: the editor in the scene: picking, dragging and placing pieces under the pointer, painting the ground, the eyedropper, the hover, the selection and cell outlines, the see-through copy, its keys, undo, and its camera. `components/map-editor-panel.tsx` is its panel and the map button, and opens the page on the saved map.
- `components/side-panel.tsx`: the panel down the left that the editor and the viewer share: its frame and width (four of the editor's pictures a row), the part that stays put over the part that scrolls, the buttons' and pressed choices' styles, and the tile a choice is shown as by its picture (a piece, or a portrait).
- `town.ts`: the town as the code places it on the grid: the trees, the village's buildings and what stands round them, its paving, gardens and walls, its paths and plants. `PLACES` names each part of the town and the country by its constant, for the overlay and the map's file.
- `buildings.ts`: the kit's rules for a building, and the builder.
- `bench.ts` and `scene/bench-marks.ts`: the piece bench's layout, and its names and axis arrows.
- `scene/debug-overlay.ts` and `scene/name-label.ts`: the ~ overlay's names and cell numbers, and the name plates it and the bench share. `scene/collider-lines.ts`: its lines of the colliders round each player.
- `scene/town.ts`: builds the piece bench's pieces, each file loaded once and every placement a clone, an object of its own to name and mark.
- `scene/merged.ts`: builds placed pieces that never move (the map and the ground's tiles, a chunk at a time) merged into one mesh for each material in each square of five cells by five. The kits share a handful of materials, so a square is a few draws, and one more for each of the town kit's other colour maps worn in it (a piece in another texture draws in that map's one copy of the material, shared by every chunk); it is culled whole, and drawn nearest first, so what it hides is never shaded. The frame is bound by the pixels the GPU shades, so that order is what counts: instanced meshes all sort as if at the world's origin, and draw in no useful order. A mirrored piece's faces are turned back round as it is merged. A placement can still be had as an object of its own, outside the scene, to cast a ray at.
- `scene/town-layer.ts`: placements drawn merged by chunk, and redrawn a chunk at a time: a chunk whose placements are the same objects as before is kept, so an edit rebuilds only what it touched, and each chunk drawn gives its colliders to the world in its place. It picks the nearest placement a ray meets, chunk by chunk, and draws one again in a material of its own for the hover.
- `scene/ground-tiles.ts`: the ground's tiles and cliffs on a layer of their own, laid again only round what a change touched, and the ground's columns and the boxes over its empty cells given to the colliders, a chunk at a time where they changed.
- `scene/kit-fit.ts` and `scene/tokens.css`: fits each kit's pieces to the world as they load, and puts a town piece's material in another of the kit's colour maps (`TownTextures`).
- `scene/snapshot.ts`: pictures of models drawn on the game's renderer into an offscreen target and read back as images, for the viewer's portraits and the editor's pictures. `scene/thumbnails.ts`: the editor's picture of each piece in each texture, drawn when first asked for and kept.
- `player.ts`: the player on foot, pure: position, facing and gait, stepped from what the controls ask for, with where a move ends given by a `Mover`; the gaits' ground speeds, a jump, and how fast a part-pushed stick walks and its clip plays.
- `colliders.ts`: which kinds of piece are met and how (walked through, closed across a doorway, or as their stand-ins), how each kind is fitted (its tolerances, whether it is climbed, the kinds shaped by what they are and the `OVERRIDES` set by hand), the body stand-ins are fitted for (`WALKER`), where a placement puts its piece, a chunk's pieces as stand-ins placed, and the ground as columns by chunk. The fitting itself (reading a model's columns, keeping its gaps, fitting boxes, wedges, flights and hulls by the rule, and judging stand-ins against the drawn model) is game-agnostic, in `src/shared/lib/physics/stand-ins.ts`. `tools/stand-ins.ts` runs it over every piece of every kit and lists the outliers (`npx tsx src/projects/adventure/tools/stand-ins.ts [piece ...]`). `physics.ts`: the world in Rapier: the players' capsules and how the controller moves them, the bodies, the boxes over empty cells and round the grid, the ray down, and the overlay's lines.
- `scene/character-viewer.ts`: the viewer: the renderer, lights, the players and the second's joining and leaving, drawing the screen whole or in two halves, and the villagers and animals, each a clone of its file with its own mixer, the animals sharing one material. Each file loads once.
- `scene/player-view.ts`: one player on foot and the camera that follows them: their place, their drawn feet settling onto the drawn surface under them, their character and clips, and their camera's orbit, turn, tilt and zoom.
- `weapons.ts`: the pack's weapons, each with the point in its own model the hand closes round, and which clips are made for a held weapon. `scene/held.ts` puts one in a character's hand.
- `scene/stage.ts`: the character and animal viewer's stage: its scene, the subject on it as a player standing still (so it plays, blends and is orbited as a player is), what a character holds there, and the roster's portraits. `components/stage-panel.tsx` is its panel, key and button, and carries its choice back to the player.
- `components/adventure.tsx`: the page: the key hints, the backend, loading and any failure. In development it puts a probe on the page for tools: `window.__adventure.lookFrom(eye, at, player?)` drives a player's camera (the first's unless told) to an exact view, `players()` says how many play, `drawn()` counts what a frame drew, the scene's renders and what each walked, `loaded()` lists the parts of the world in place, `leaveOut(part, out)` takes one out of the frame (or halves the resolution), `gpuTime()` reads the GPU's own time when the page opened with `?gpu-time`, `standAt(x, z, player?)` stands a player at a point, on the highest ground there, `playerAt(player?)` says where a player's feet are and whether they are in the air, and `collision()` counts collision's work: the colliders in the world, in all and of each shape, and the controller's moves and the rays the last frame made. `tools/profile.mjs` uses them to price each part of the frame on the integrated GPU, in alternated pairs, and to profile the main thread; it draws uncapped frames on the GPU that drives the owner's screen, so it is run only once the owner has agreed, and a count of the frame's work from `drawn()` is the first evidence; with `--players 2` it joins a second player on a stand-in pad, and its `second` lever prices them.
- `credits.ts`: Kenney's credit for every pack used.

Each is 400 to 950 triangles in one material, a draw for each part, so the whole pack is about 130 draws when every animal is in view; from the opening views a few are, and they add 10 to 35 draws.
- `public/adventure/nature-kit/`: every GLB of Kenney's Nature Kit 2.1. Each carries its own flat colours, with no texture. Its ground tiles fill one unit cell each, centred on their origin, like the town kit's.
- The skeleton has no hand joints: a held weapon is a child of its arm's joint, at a point out along the arm that fits every character, since the arms are rigid. The weapons don't all have their origin where the hand holds them, so each records its own grip point.
- The characters share one rigid seven-joint skeleton and the same clips. They are not all one height (about 0.67 to 0.85), and their bind pose has the arms out, so a bounding box of the model at rest is wider than the character as it stands in any clip.
- Each GLB loads its own copy of the shared colour map, filtered with mipmaps, as the pack sets it. Both stop mattering only once many characters share a scene, or are seen small: then one shared texture, and a look at how mipmaps blend the map's colour swatches, belong in the build step.
